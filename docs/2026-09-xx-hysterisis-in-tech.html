Hysterisis in Tech

Jeff Uren ★ 2026-XX-XX

In Breaking the Trust Thermocline Is the Biggest Hidden Risk in Business, Gareth Edwards talks us through a problem that's pretty difficult to visualize because while it's happening the system carries on functioning like it isn't a problem. In the article, it comes at trust from the perspective of Customers of a Product-oriented company, and posits that customers can tolerate a fair amount from a company that they've put their trust into. You can do things like hike prices, have reliability issues, even remove features, or fail to hold up your promises with only a modicum of thrashing. From inside the business, it's really easy to mistake the lack of change in numbers as acceptance by customers. Your revenue and retention can stay flat or even grow with each decision seeming like the customer-base has absorbed it without any real consequences to the business.

Eventually it can all come crashing down, and you can accidentally attribute it to a single event, when the truth is it was a long process of attrition.

I'm really into the premise of this article, but I think it's worth spinning it around and evaluating it from the perspetive of the people who are responsible for building and operating the technology too, and their relationship with the organisation that they're operating within.

In that situation it has less to do with customers deciding whether to keep buying a product than whether the people responsible for building and operating technology still trust the organisation around them. Sure, the surface indicators are different, but the underlying problem is actually pretty similar. Things tend to still carry on happening like shipping code, handling incidents, meetings, q&a's and retros and all that. All the visible operations more or less stay on an even keel throughout the time.

Similar to with Customers though, the conditions under which good engineering happens kind of start to disappear and you end up in a situation where bad things start to happen:

  • Architecture gets acknowledged but not actioned
  • Technical debt keeps getting shunted further down the backlog
  • Platform creates standards that get abandoned because their inconvenient
  • Specialists come in in after decisions are made
  • Restructures remove strong knowledge-keepers without replacing them
  • Teams (and individuals) increasingly absorb more without a corresponding reduction somewhere else
  • Programmes promise to simplify things, but just add more layers to maintain

A single one of those isn't generally going to lead to some humongous disaster. Your engineers and technology people are generally pretty good at compromise overall, or operating with not enough info, or operating under a wide, complicated cross-section of constraints that don't have clear solutions. Basically everything in technology is a trade-off and that's baked into how good engineers make decisions, through experience and having your fingertips burnt a few times. The more general consideration inthere is that an engineering team is going to have a much deeper well of tolerance and accommodation to draw from than you might realize. Engineers will accept compromises if they think there's some larger sense of coherent philosophy behind it, and they'll take on super awkward work if they can trust that at some point it'll end, becoming something more reasonable. They'll event tolerate multiple, successive poor decisions as long as the next has the promise of at least being incrementally better than the last.

It's really easy to confuse that well for being some magical, limitless reservoir of capacity though.

Engineers are really good at compensating for broken systems. A competent engineer rarely encounters a bad organisational boundary and just stops there. They'll build a script, create an informal process, figure who they need to talk to or ask. Cumulatively they maintain undocumented translations between multiple systems whose owners probably don't speak each other anymore or maybe don't even exist. Architectural context for large swathes of the system sits in their noggins', and they use it to quietly correct bad assumptions, and they spend a little longer than anyone really notices making something work sometimes. Even in spite of some gnarly conditions that work gets commissioned under.

Basically, all of that coping and resilience, from the surface level, can look like a concretely well-functioning system, but under the hood it's just a collection of behaviours and traits that are giving the appearance of stability.

This might get kind of uncomfortable. The stronger your engineers, the longer organisational dysfunction can stay hidden. The people most capable of recognising those structural problems are often also those most capable of compensating for them, the engineers. Their competence essentially becomes a shock absorber in between poor organisational decisions and their actual consequences. So long as the consequences remain manageable, the decisions themselves obtain the appearance of success.

An organisation can subsist on that well of capacity for a pretty long time overall but it doesn't last forever and it doesn't refill as quickly as it gets used up.

We can frame this around technical debt a bit here, although debt being accumulated isn't just in the code. Debt also exists in relationships, expectations, and things like credibility. Every commitment that's made and sort of casually abandoned changes the cost of the next commitment. Every consultation whose outcome was actually predetermined changes the meaning of the next invite to contribue. Every request to raise risks followed by punishment for raising inconvenient ones teaches people something about what the organisation actually values. Every declaration that engineering quality matters, followed by a decision in which it clearly doesn't, makes the next declaration worth less and less.

This is where Edwards' thermocline breaks out. Trust inside an organisation isn't just a mood that rises and falls in response to the most recent event. It's got institutional memory.

Hysterisis

In engineering and physics, hysterisis describes systems whose current behaviour depends partly on the path through which they arrived at the current position. Push a system in one direction for some time and then try to reverse the force, and it's highly unlikely to retrace the same path backwards. Something changes in the system itself which means the path back just isn't valid anymore.

You have the same situation with people within an organisation. If as leadership, you've been consistently overruling technical advice, or repeatedly postponing foundational work, or restructuring teams you're going to have a hard time restoring trust and confidence by just walking back those behaviours. If your teams been watching colleagues disappear through a succession of restructures, each next announcement of change, the words can be different, and maybe the intentions are sincere, but the people hearing them carry the evidence of everything that came before.

If you have a culture that's learned that commitments are more or less temporary through years of attrition, or that risks are unwelcome, people start to treat all things as temporary and all the risks start to get a whole heck of a lot quieter.

Specifically in engineering, once they've learned that careful engineering is systemically sacrificed at the altar for urgency, they all stop planning as if careful engineering will eventually be OK.

Sounds like cynicism right? It's really easy to label it cynicism, or even lack of personal resilience. Really, it's actually a pretty rational response to the environment on the whole. You can chock it up to a lack of morale, or people being jaded, but if more and more people are behaving this way, you're really just watching people adapt to the environment in real-time.

Building the Wrong Skills

You can actually create a weird microcosm of a skillset within your organisation where people are insanely skilled at surviving the orgranisation they're actually working inside of versus the one that's described by the operating model. People will do things like shorten their planning horizons, reduce their personal investment in improvements where there isn't certainty, and even stop maintaining things that only make sense if someone is still going to care about them two years from now. Overall, these people become extremely cautious about ownership, especially where that ownership doesn't come with correlating authority, because ownership without authority is essentially seen as liability, it's just got better branding.

Let's be super clear, all throughout this, your org will still keep delivering things, all of this can be happening right under your nose without any super obvious signs as it's spread out across teams and individuals. It's actually really difficult to recognize.

Most technology management systems tend to observe outputs or measures far more aggressively than they observe the conditions under which the outputs emerge. We sure can count lots of stuff: delivery, incidents, vacancies, attrition, MTTRs, velocities. The concept of Trust is a lot harder to measure and the lack of it tends to reveal itself at sort of right angles to everything else. Primarily peoples behaviour changes, and some of that behaviour is really easy to explain away in isolation or framed in the light of isolated or recent events.

You'll see things like Senior engineers not really challenging qestionable decisions and they might even seem like they're easier to work with, more agreeable. On the other side you can see engineers forcing change through stricter and stricter formal processes, even when the fix is something that could be done informally and pretty qiuickly. You'll have had engineers that seemed like they were octopii, while they solved their assigned work, they would also spend time improving shared tooling that made everyone's assigned work easier and faster, they'll stop doing that, and spend their time focused on just what's expected of them. Overall, people will stop volunteering for difficult chunks of work, and a lot of that work may fall to people who are newer, or less experienced.

There's not going to be an alert or an alarm when all this is happening, it's slow, and insidious. And some of all this can easily be spun and framed as improvement when viewed in isolation.

Meetings become less contentious, responsibilities clearer. People interfere less in work outside their immediate remit. Delivery might become more predictable for awhile because engineers have stopped spending discretionary effort on problems that don't belong to them.

The difficulty is that much of the resilience of a technology organisation lives precisely in that discretionary space.

Formal structures rarely describe all the work required to keep complex systems alive. There are gaps between teams, mismatches between models, old decisions whose consequences have outlived their authors, and incidents whose resolution depends on someone remembering a detail that was never important enough to document. Health engineering organisations survive these imperfections because people cross boundaries for one another. They share context, challenge assumptions, repair thingsbefore they become crises, and invest in improvements whose value can't always be attached neatly to a quarterly objective.

Trust is what makes most of that behaviour possible, which makes trust less like employee sentiment and more like critical infrastructure.

We're used to thinking of infrastructure as the machiner beneath software: networks, databases, identity systems, build pipelines, cloud platforms and observability. Yet engineering organisations depend just as heavily on another layer of infrastructure that's rarely drawn in their diagrams. It's made of credible commitments, professional respect, shared language, institutional memory, psychological safety, stable expectations and confidence that decisions have some relationship to the evidence presented in makign them.

When the infrastructure deteriorates, the applications running on top of it can continune for a long while but eventually to cost has to move somewhere else.

It sort of takes up residence in the engineer who leaves and takes five years of context with them. Or in the redesign that unknowingly repeats an abandoned experiment because nobody left remembers why it failed. In technical debt that nobody is willing to champion because previous attempts ended in cancellation. In incidents taht take six hours rather than forty minutes because the informal network that used to connect the relevant people has weakened into non-existence. In programmes whose estimates become padded because no one trusts the surrounding organisation to protect them from uncertainty.

Then, at some point, an organisation cross its own thermocline.

I can tell you now it won't be a big ceremonial point when it does happen. There won't be alarm bells ringin' that everyones trust has fallen below a safe operating level. The visible trigger will probably be surprisingly small. Maybe another restructure. A disappointing pay decision. A respected colleague leaving. A project cancelled after months of effort. A senior appointment that confirms something people had already begun to suspect about the direction of the organisation.

Leadership might understandably focus on that event because it's the one immediatel preceding the change in behaviour, but the event is usually only just the final measurement of a much older, and longer process.

The engineer that resigns after the latest reorganisation might not be leaving because of that reorganisation. They might be leaving beacuse the previous four taught them how to interpret this one. The team that refuses to absorb another responsibility might not have suddenly become inflexible. It might just simply have exhausted the goodwill which it absorbed the previous ten. The architect who no longer fights a technically damaging proposal may not have been persuaded by it. They just might have concluded that persuasion isn't really worth the expenditure anymore.

By the time these things become visible, reversing the most recent decision can do very little, which is the organisational form of hysterisis, the path back isn't always the same path in reverse.

Restoring a training budget doesn't restore the people who left when development stopped being funded. Announcing protected engineering time doesn't immediately restore the belief that the time will stay protected. Asking for candour doesn't instantly revive a culture that learned caution. Hiring new people can replace capacity, but it can't cheaply replace the network of relaitonships through which the old organisation understood itself.

Trust has to be rebuilt throug accumulated evidence in much the same way that it ended up lost.

This is might be the most uncomfortable implication of the thermocline because it leaves very little room for symbolic repair. Organisations usually respond to cultural deterioration with communication, as though the central problem were that people had misunderstood the organisation's intentions. Sometimes they have, more often the organisation has communicated perfectly well through it's decisions.

People believe in patterns.

If the pattern changes, believe can eventually change with it, but the change has to survive long enough to become a new history. Commitments have to repeatedly be kept when breaking them would have been inconvenient. Technical have to alter the direction of programmes rather than just be recorded in their risk registers. Team sneed to see foundational work survive contact with deadlines. Leaders have to tolerate disagreement without quietly converting it into reputational damage.

There's no announcement capable of substituting for all of this.

Edward's original argument was concerned with companies discovering too late that customers who appeared loyal had in fact been spending down their trust for years. Technology organisations face the same danger internally. They can look at continued delivery, modest attrition, completed projects and functioning systems and conclude that the organisation remains healthy enough.

And maybe it will, or maybe its engineers are still compensating.

The difference between those is important because compensation has limits. A mature technology organisation isn't one where people can endure unlimited amounts of dysfunction. It's one wthat understands how much of its apparent stability is being produced by invisible acts of judgement, cooperation and trust, and treats those things as resources that have to be renewed rather than reserves that can be endlessly extracted.

So the really dangerous moment, isn't really the point where your engineers start to complain, it's when they stop.
