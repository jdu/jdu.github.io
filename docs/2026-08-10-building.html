Pickin' Parts

Jeff Uren ★ 2026-07-21

"Build" isn't a dirty word

Most software systems are assembled from existing components. No one ever calls an application bespoke because it uses Postgres, Linux and HTTP. Tableau server uses postgres, Jetty, Kafka, and other libraries and packages that are freely available for people to use. The moment an organisation combines an orchestrator, object storage, and compute, it starts to worry that it's attempting to become a software vendor.

A data platform assembled from established components is in fact normal software engineering. The alternative isn't the absence of engineering, it's placing more of the engineering inside a commercial product and accepting the boundaries that come with it.

Platforms are shaped by their organisations

Data estates differ in ways that products can't fully generalize away. They have different source systems, security models, data volumes, operating rhythms, regulatory constraints, skills and expectations about how quickly things should change.

A composed platform can follow those contours. It can run small python jobs beside large spark workloads. It can use event-driven processing where that suits the source and scheduled processing where it doesn't. It can keep data in formats that stay useful outside the current tool.

The platform doesn't have to pretend every workload is exactly the same.

Mature components give you more, not less

Airflow, Spark, Postgres, Kafka, and S3 are not primitive building blocks in the sense of nuts and bolts. Each represents years of engineering, documentation, operational knowledge, and community use.

An organisation assembling them is inheriting an enormous amount of existing capability. It's not starting at zero. It's beginning closer to the point where it's own requirements are met.

The engineering effort goes into the parts that commercial platforms can't know in advance: how the organisation deploys, secures, observes, governs and understands its data.

You can improve one part without replacing everything

A composed platform allows capabilities to move at different speeds.

The storage layer can remain stable while compute changes. A new execution engine can be introduced for one class of workload. Airflow can continue orchestrating work without becoming the place where all business logic lives. A query engine can be replaced without migrating underlying data into another proprietary representation.

A monolithic platform often ties these decisions together. A weakness in one part becomes a migration of the whole.

Open interfaces preserve leverage

When data remains in parquet, iceberg, SQL tables or other broadly supported forms, the organisation retains options.

Different tools can work over the same data. New services can be introduced without first extracting everything from the old one. Engineers can inspect the underlying state directly. The platform can evolve without each change becoming a procurement excercise.

This isn't abstract portability. It changes who has leverage when prices rise, product direction changes, or a vendor stops serving the organisation well.

Building creates technical capability

Operating a platform develops knowledge that remains useful beyond the platform itself.

Teams learn how data moves through the organisation, where failures originate, how permissions are applied, what workloads actually cost and which abstractions survive contact with real work. Those capabilities improve security, integration, software delivery and architectural judgement elsewhere.

Buying a platform can provide a faster route to a particular set of features. It doesn't necessarily develop the organisation’s ability to understand or change the system beneath them.

Commercial platforms do not remove the joins

A purchased product still has to connect to identity, networks, source systems, reporting tools, security processes and organisational governance. Pipelines still fail. Data still needs ownership. Costs still need controlling. Product limitations still need working around.

The engineering doesn't just disappear. Some of it moves, some becomes constrained, even more only gets performed through configuration, consultancy and support tickets.

A composed platform makes more of those joins available to ordinary engineering practices: code, tests, version control, automation and observable interfaces.

The economics are broader than headcount

Build-versus-buy comparisons often place engineer salaries on one side and licence costs on the other. That misses a lot of the actual cost.

Commercial platforms accumulate consumption charges, premium features, consultancy, migration work, duplicated storage, data-egress costs and teams dedicated to administering the product. They can also make small architectural decisions expensive because each departure from the intended model can require yet another product or service.

A composed platform has visible engineering costs, but it can often run on inexpensive commodity infrastructure and scale in ways the organisation can inspect and control.

Building does require stewardship

The platform needs ownership, maintenance and enough continuity to evolve. Components need upgrading. Shared conventions need restraint. Internal abstractions need to remain smaller than the problems they solve.

These aren't arguments against building though. They're the ordinary obligations that come with owning any important technical capability.

Build the parts that let the organisation remain itself

The organisation doesn't build object storage, orchestration or distributed computation. It borrows these things from mature projects and cloud services. It builds the relationships between them, the operating model around them and the parts that reflect how its own work is done.

That's often the part most worth owning.
