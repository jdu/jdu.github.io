Defragmentation - JDU

Jeff Uren ★ 2026-03-24

Some of you may remember the days when you had to defrag your hard drive once in a while. There were specific reasons to do this with spinning platter drives and how we approached formatting and storing data on hard disk drives.

For those of you who don't know or didn't live throug hthis era, the essential idea was that over time as your wrote data, deleted data, and moved data around a disk, it would end up spread all over the sectors of the disk, with large gaps between populated sectors.

Because these drives were a bit like a record player, where there were a series or a single needle which travelled over the surface of a disk, if for instance a jpeg you had downloaded's bytes were spread across the surface of the platter in chunks, it meant that the needle had to move a lot, and jump around a lot in order to put that jpeg back together again to show on your screen.

Defragmentation essentially was a big ole' spring clean. You opened the defrag tool and your computer would shifta dn move around data so taht it lived in nice contiguous blocks, which when you were trying to open a file, meant less jumpy seeks for data across the platters of the disk. Which meant quicker reads.

On a slow disk, you would have a noticeable performance boost a lot of the time after a defrag. The only issue was that it could take a really long time. Depending on the size of the drive, the performance of the machine, and other factors, defragging a disk could in some cases take days to complete. It really cut into your Starcraft and Quake LAN party time.

The underlying lesson here is that fragmentation, for performance, can be really bad, and it can make getting things done prohibitively difficult, especially during awkward moments.
