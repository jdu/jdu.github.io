How to Break a Technology team

Jeff Uren ★ 2026-07-06

Technology teams generally don't fail because they lack talented people.

They do beacuse the organisation, slowly, over time, teaches those people that their judgement doesn't matter.

You rarely actually notice it happening, and inevitably when you look back, you realizing it was happening earlier than you thought. Productivity doesn't collapse overnight. The people who care the most often work even harder to compensate. Eventually, though, the organisation crosses a threshold where the people who carry the most institutional knowledge about your organisation, and the people who care the most about the work, leave.

If you wanted to build that outcome deliberately, here's how.

Step 1: Stop listening to the people closest to the work

When difficult technical decisions come around, ask everyone except the engineers.

Hire consultants, run steering groups, benchmark against other organisations.

The peopled oing the work know they don't have perfect answers. What they expect is that their experience informs the conversation.

Once they realize it doesn't, they stop offering it.

Step 2: Confuse activity with progress

Every quarter, produce a new roadmap, rename programmes, redraw organisation charts, launch another transformation.

Just make sure nothing stays stable long enough for anyone to finish improving it because technology compounds.

Constant strategic resets destroy compound interest.

Step 3. Assume every organisation has the same problems

Find a successful company (on paper, don't actually probe it or look past the commerical case study), and them copy it's technology stack, its governance, and its operating model.

Ignore entirely that organisations accumulate decades of history, regulation, culture, funding models, and technical constraints.

Software can be bought, context can't.

Step 4. Reward certainty over curiosity

Encourage people to have answers, but discourage them from asking uncomfortable questions.

Soon you'll find out that all your meetings are chock full of agreement, unfortunately, reality doesn't care.

Step 5. Measure everything except what matters

Count tickets, story points, velocity, and build dashboard describing delivery.

Spend very little time understanding why delivery became slower, people optimize whatevers measures, not necessarily what's actually valuable.

Step 6. Replace trust with process

When one thing goes wrong introduce another approval.

More meetings, sign-offs, and governance boards will accumulate to a point where eventually no one owns any decisions, they just participate in them.

Step 7. Treat engineers as implementers

Invite them after the strategy, not before, then ask for estimates, and not their opinions.

Eventually, they'll become unbelievably good at delivering exactly what was requested, and not a single bit more because innovation is usually the first casualty of being ignored.

Step 8. Make every year a transformation year

Technology needs rhythm, architecture needs time, and platforms need stability.

Engineers need enough uninterrupted time to make something genuinely better, transformation without any consolidation is just the organisation thrashing around.

Step 9. Conventiently forget that people are time poor

Every mandatory meeting, commute, new process, duplicated report, status upate.

You're spending someone else's life, and most organisations budget money far more carefully than they budget thir people's time.

Step 10. Mistake silence for alignment

People stop arguing long before they stop disengaging, which means the most dangerous organisation isn't the one without conflict, it's the one where experienced people quietly decide there's no point in speaking anymore.

Step 11. Hire consultants to interview managers about engineering

Whatever you do, don't ask the engineers, they'll only confuse things with facts.

Step 12. Never visit the engine room

Technology should only be observed through dashboards and steering committes, direct contact with reality introduces bias.

Step 13. Mistake confidence for competence

The person speaking most confidently probably understands the problem best.

The quiet engineer who's spent three years building the platform is probably overthinking it.

Step 14. Benchmark against organisations that aren't remotely like yours

Banks, retail, insurance, SaaS platform companies. The fewer similarities they have to your organisation the better. Context is just unnecessary complexity.

Step 15. Mistake organisational memory for resistance

When experienced engineers say,

"We tried this before..."

Treat that as negativity, certainly do not ask why it failed.

Step 16. Make every discussion about tooling

Culture? Communication? Incentives? Nah. Buy another platform.

Step 17. Confuse procurement with strategy

Buying technology is not the same thing as building capability. Fortunately, procurement reports are much shorter.

Step 18. Celebrate launches. Forget maintenance.

New things are exciting, keeping them working is somebody else's problem.

Step 19. Reward agreement

People who ask difficult questions clearly aren't "aligned". Alignment is much easier once everyone has stopped speaking.

Step 20. Never admit the organisation already has the capability

There's nothing more awkward than discovering your engineering teams quietly solved the problem last year.

Much safer to commission another discovery phase.

Step 21. Make sure every decision is technically reverisible, except management decisions

If an engineer makes a mistake, hold a retrospective. If leadership makes a mistake, reorganise.

Step 22. Never let expertise become authority

Technical expertise is dangerous, it creates inconvenient opinions. Much better to ensure that expertise remains purely advisory.

Step 34. Replace trust with permission

If someone needs approval often enough, eventually they'll stop trying.

Congratulations! You've standardized mediocrity.

Step 35. Make psychological safety conditional

Tell people:

"We value honest feedback"

Then remember exactly who gave it.

Step 36. Promote optimism over accuracy

Reward people who say,

"No problem."

Punish people who say,

"Here's the risl"

Eventually reality becomes very expensive.

Step 36. Treat disagreement as disloyalty

If someone challenges a proposal, assume they're challenging the people.

Soon your meetings will become wonderfully harmonious, just not particularly useful.

Step 37. Ensure every difficult conversation has witnesses

Nothing builds openness quite like making sure that people know they'll be judged for speaking honestly.

Step 38. Never close the feedback loop

Ask for opinions, run surveys, hold workshops, publish nothing, change nothing, repeat as needed.

Step 39. Routinely "soft threaten" redundancy

It's an inevitability, but make sure they know that, as hazily as possible, with little timelines or context as often as is safe.

Step 40. Optimize away ownership

Split responsibility across multiple teams, across enough that the responsiblity is diffuse enough that no one feels responsible anymore.

The failure becomes everyones, successes become leaderhips.

Step 41. Teach people that effort is invisible

The easiest way to reduce discretionary effort is to consistently fail to notice it.

People don't stop caring because they're lazy, they stop caring because caring because it's irrational.

Step 42. Build learned helplessness

Whenever someone solves a problem independently, change the process so next time they need approval.

Repeat.

Step 43. Mistake exhaustion for commitment

Celebrate the people who answer emails at midnight, ignore the fact they haven' thad an uninterrupted weekend in months.

Call it "high performance".

Step 44. Ensure nobody sees the consequences of their decisions

The people approving a process should never have to follow it, and people defining delivery dates should never have to deliver, also the people selecting the tooling, they should never have to use it.

Step 45. Confuse visibility with value

If the work isn't in a steering committee deck, did it really happen?

Step 46. Keep everyone permanently provisional

Never quite finish the reorganisation, the transformation, or quite settle the strategy. People make long-term investments only when they believe the organisation will.

Step 47. Teach people that speaking up changes nothing

This is the final step, this will get them really quiet and we all know that quiet organisations look healthy.

Until they aren't.

Conclusion

Broken technology teams still deliver, they still solve incidents, and still hit deadlines, but the things that made them exceptional just kind of, disappear.

Things like curiosity, ownership, accountability, initiative, trust, and pride. If you want to break a team, teach them that the environment they work in, doesn't find those things useful from them anymore.

Organisations technological functions don't become mediocre because they hire mediocre people, or because they're not "enterprise". They become mediocre because they systematically teach the exceptional people to stop caring.
