Mental Health

Jeff Uren ★ 2026-XX-XX

I'm not 100% sure how to start this off in general, it's a hard topic to broach really. People don't generally like to talk about mental health other than in sort of roundabout terms, and I think we need to talk about the intersection of Mental Health and Software and Technology a lot more than we currently do.

Maybe I should start off with a disclaimer that I'm not a mental health professional, and this is not a professional opinion from that angle. What it is, however, is opinion peppered by personal experience.

I can't speak to the wider domain of mental health, all I can really do is speak about my own experiences and the experiences of people I know, and how their mental health has been impacted on and off throughout the years and decades that I've been involved in Software and Technology.

We'll try and ease into this I think, and I'll try and pepper personal experience into it where I can because mental health is a very personal thing.

It's Effect on Your Career

Popular culture, media, movies, and general attitudes towards mental health position it as something that affects your career, as something external to the work you do and the people you work with. It's generally treated as something that comes in from outside of the office, and in the worst cases is treated as something that shouldn't be brought to the office, or brought up a tall.

Some organisationsIncluding my own. provide access to mental health professionals, the vast majority of organisations either don't have the necessary capital to do so, or don't prioritize it enough to want to.

Imposter Syndrome

This is a very specific type of mental health that can erode away into depression, anxiety, and paranoia pretty quickly if left unchecked. It happens in teams where there's little to not positive reinforcement happening, or there's a sort of wall of work that never lets engineers really expand and do more than the business as usual work.

The general ideas is that a person carries with them an internal belief that they're not good enough, and that they don't deserve the position that they've landed themselves in. You internalize the idea that you're an "imposter", you're not really who people think that you are, or capable in the way that people thing you are, and that at some point, people will find out, and there will some sort of reckoning.

Burnout in Engineers

Burnout is hard to come back from.

We tend to brush it off as being a little tired or worn out, but in reality it's not a something trivial that a little sleep and a cup of coffee will resolve.

The good news is that there is a ton of research and literature on burnout, specifically in software engineering.

The bad news is that it's pretty bad.

The list of causes of burnout across loads of the literature are pretty consistent and fall into some broad categories, but they're also pretty endemic to software engineering as a whole.

  • Tension and Control at work
  • Job overload
  • Role conflicts
  • Job specialisation
  • Job demands
  • Information overload
  • Severe data cascade
  • Long streaks of uninterrupted contributions
  • Long job strains from COVID-19

A lot of the literature that's out there primarily focuses on the triggers and environments that create burnout, but they don't discuss or address the actual concrete consequences of burnout itself.

The few papers that do, break the components pieces out around some core components of your work life:

  • Agility Practices
  • Communication Practices
  • Work-related factors
  • Turnover
  • Personality Traits
  • Physiological Factors

Interestingly, the literature draws a direct causal link between these things and burnout, with varying things feeding into the different aspects. The relation is unambiguous and there are feedback loops which form within the causal links which heighten and potentially logarithmically increase the likelihood of more and more catastrophic outcomes from sustained burnout or dipping in and out of burnout.

The Dip

You can be burn out, and not realize it. Your focus can be concentrated on something so much that you fail to even realize that your mental health is suffering, and you may only notice when the effects of that burnout start to manifest in your work, and more importantly your personal relationships.

Concurrency

All about how working on multiple concurrent and heavily diverging things contributes towards an uneven mental state, and can exacerbate feelings of imposter syndrome, and depression.

I've had a complicated relationship with burnout over the years, and I think it's worth exploring a little bit to help people understand what it is, and how insidiously it can creep up on you. What I can't do is make assumptions about your personal experiences, and I can't make recommendations on what will work for you for avoiding burnout other than in roundabout ways. It's an intensely personal experience and different people will react and handle burnout in very different ways. The same way we deal with grief, loss, or trauma, we all cope differently and develop different coping mechanisms, and some of those are sub-consciously leaned on rather than actively engaged with.
