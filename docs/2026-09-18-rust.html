Corrosion

Jeff Uren ★ 2026-XX-XX

To start out, I want to be really clear. I'm not setting out in this article to ruffle anyones feathers. Enough years have gone by that we can look back on things with a little more clarity than we had at the time and I like understand socio-technical and socio-political things as they evolve over time. Rust presented engendered an environment that I'm not really sure the original incentives set out to create, but nonetheless did and it's worth poking all of that at least a little bit.

So let's set the stage a bit. It's the early 2010's, PHP is eating the internet, it feels like every site is a Wordpress site, a few are built on Python frameworkes like Django or Pyramid. Another subset on Ruby and RoR. There are a number of commerical languages and frameworks floating around like C# / .NET and Adobe ColdFusion.

You've got a large number of web-oriented, mobile-oriented programmers who are becoming more and more proficient at programming and who are tackling higher and higher complexity problems incrementally lower down the stack.

The leap from web to systems downward at certains points was a pretty large one. Nonetheless, more and more developers started using the languages they were comfortable in to do work that more and more looked like systems programming or danced around the edges of it. These developers slowly but surely moved their way down to in the technological stack and because of familiarity and some hard barriers, they tended to bring their preferred languages with them.

At systems-level at the time, that layer of programming was actually kind of stale. C was (and still is) a hugely important language, but no one I think would have made the assertion of labelling it "modern" at that time. C++ had been churning along accreting "ideas" since the early 80's with C++ 11 bringing things forwards by leaps and bounds, but modern C++ more or less sits on top of old C++ and building real strong expertise in C++ is and always has been very path-dependent. Knowing C++ then and now generally means knowing C++ 10-15 years before including things like which compiler supports which flags and features, what hits the ABI, and when you're working in an established code-base, you very much might need to know which era of conventions that code-base was actually developed under, and it's not always as clear-cut as you might think.

Again, I'm not out to skewer either C or C++ at the moment (and never will be). I like both of them and I wrote and still write a bit of both here and there when I feel like there's call for it. But we're setting the stage for where Rust entered the ecosystem. Rust came along in the midst of all this and more or less explicitly presented itself as taking lessons from that landscape and history but explicitly called out that C++ factored very heavily into its design.

hammered out by generations of C and C++ programmers

The whole Rust proposition aligned heavily with a weird gap that had formed in programming and software development overall. It created a situation where someone new to systems programming in 2016 had a choice, either spend years acquiring all that accumulated folklore of C++, or join something where everyone was still relatively new.

A twenty-five year old developer could reasonably become a recognised Rust expert. Becoming equivalently authoritative in C++ meant walking into a community containing people who'd been doing things like template metaprogramming since well before you'd even finished primary school. Speaking primarily for myself though I had more lower-level systems understanding than a number of others at the time, becoming an expert in C/C++ was actually astronomically intimidating.

There were some concrete examples of this, for instance, I remember reading a forum online at one point (a normal forum, I don't think SO was around or prevalent yet) where someone had asked about building a game engine. And the prevailing answer was essentially "don't". The implication was that it would be too hard for the person.

We're not not just focusing on Rust here though, the truth is the Go programming language is actually really important here too in this context. It sort of filled the same historical vaccum that Rust was trying to, but from a very different direction.

Go came out of Google, essentially to solve Google's software engineering problems. Rob Pike describes its beginnings as discussions in 2007 about problems he and his colleagues were experiencing building the large systems that Google needed.

Go's answer to C++ was (in summation) to strip away all of the complexity and build up a language that was simpler, but still powerful enough to do serious systems work.

Contrasted with Rust's being closer to the idea that we can have sophisticated abstractions without inheriting the historical baggage, and you have two very ideologically different approaches to a very similar problem.

If you were someone who was really frustrated by complexity in and of itself, Go was super compelling. But if you liked type systems, abstractions, generics, functional programming ideas, and zero-cost abstractions with your systems programming, but struggled with the historical layers of C++, Rust actually ended up enormously attractive.

We can't gloss over something just as important as the technical differentiators between the two. Go at that time was unmistakebly a Google language. Rust was started by Mozilla, absolutely, but Mozilla had a very different cultural position in open source at the time, and Rust intentionally developed community governance before eventually moving to an independent foundation.

There was some hullabaloo around the time about which of these two languages would "win" and a sort of attitude of them vying for the crown of the "next C++". Honestly, they were and still are, very different languages, with very different goals, communities, and trade-offs. They've settled into a world where they both have their own idioms, and both are extremely successful in their own right, but the narrative at the time created an "us and them" environment in certain circles which I found really unfortunate because from a pure programming perspective, I actually like them both.

C and C++ predate things like GitHub, Reddit, Stack Overflow, Discord, social media in generalIt's easy to forget this, or get mixed up. and the entire modern open-source contribution identity.

Rust, in contrast, was born in the deep innards of that world. So it didn't end up with users, it had Rustaceans. Terminology, imagery, conferences, community teams, RFC participation, working groups, roadmaps, mentorship and in contrast to others at the time, an explicit contributor culture.

You can see this if you look at the language that was used by the project itself around 2017 to 2018. Rust's roadmap wasn't just "compiler features we're implementing". It actively talked about mentoring, empathy, accessibility, underrepresented groups, community participation and making systems programming available to people who historically were discouraged from doing so.

The 2018 roadmap specifically talks about community-wide calls for blog posts, hundreds of participants, domain working groups, mentoring and loads of ways for newcomers to get involved. The proverbial door was wide open.

The outcome was that Rust wasn't just baking up a language, it was actively, and continually manufacturing opportunity for a broad, diverse number of people to become a contributing part of it.

So looking at things how they stood, engineers (me included) who were interested in the jump to systems programming, were presented with two paths:

The C++ path meant we encountered history, a lot of static values and accreted decisions, and a lot of people who had been doing things specific ways for a very long time.

A newcomer to Rust was presented with possibility. If you didn't like something, it could be changed, through RFCs, better documentation, missing functionality could be filled with a crate, if compiler diagnostics weren't great, you could contribute improvements. The whole Rust language was actually visibly moving.

The 2017 roadmap for Rust explicitly concrentrated on lowering the learning curve, improving tools, mentoring contributors and making the language more productive. All these things in concert tend to create a strong psychological relationship with technology. People don't defend things that they have a consumptive relationship with, I'm not going to defend my choice of laundry detergent very hard, but people defend things ardently that they believe they or their peers helped build, that they've personally invested in whether through contribution, or time and effort learning.

One of the things that I always struggled with in terms of C++, was that expertise in C++ is tightly intertwined with accumulated experience over longer breadths of time. You needed to have started your career more or less in C++ in order to be perceived as proficient in it. The timelines to be considered proficient were notably, and perceptibly long.

For Rust, at least initially, proficiency was derived from participation. A programmer who had spent twenty years becoming exceptionally good at C++ could reasonably look at Rust and see an immature language recreating things they already knew how to do. But a programmer who had spent three years becoming exceptionally good at Rust could look at C++ and see decades of accidental complexity that their chosen language had avoided.

Both of those views can be and are valid, but the different perspectives clash where they're both claiming authority over what systems programming ought to be.

Here's where we talk about "safety". The proposition that Rust made wasn't just that this was some new way of managing memory. The talk around how memory was managed really rapidly turned into something a lot closer to an assertion. Mainly that we know memory-unsafety causes a large number of classes of defects, so continuing to choose technology susceptible to them requires justification.

What really was a technical preference started taking on a lot of moral weight.

I can say something like "Python is a lot nicer to work with than Ruby", and frankly, that doesn't say a whole hell of a lot about a Ruby programmer, and it shouldn't. The programmer isn't the subject, the language is and I'm just expressing my preference for it. The entire implication is that I think Python is better than Ruby. Some people can read deeper into that and infer that I mean that Ruby is inferior, but really all I'm saying is that I prefer to write Python.

"I believe this technology prevents classes of vulnerabilities which repeatedly harm users" is a different type of discourse, especially when pushed far, and forces a moral decision into a technology choice making the engineer fully responsible for the implied moral failing.

Instead of "I like what Rust gives me," it becomes: "Why are you still writing C++?"

That's the right mix of ingredients for tribalism. The "Rust Evangelism Strike Force" joke works pretty much because enough people recognised this specific behaviour, and it turned into caricature. There are Hacker News discussions explicitly complaining about Rust advocacy being unusually political or fanatical, while Rust-oriented joke communities themselves repeatedly use the same term.

All this formed a feedback loop, Rust very quickly acquired evidence that Rust people really liked Rust. In the 2015 Stack Overflow survey, the year that 1.0 of Rust shipped, it was already the third "most loved" language.

By 2018 it had topped that measure for three consecutive years, and by 2022 it had done it for seven.

That statistic is basically now part of Rusts whole identity. There's a big different between lots of software is written in python and People who know our language love it.

That latter message, that's a recruiting message, it's reinforcing the idea that there is something special here that outsiders haven't yet found. Which makes the evangelism self-reinforcing.

All of this kind of culminates in a sort of status reset. C++ has an enormouse accumulated hierarchy of expertise. In 2015, someone could have twenty-five years of experience in C++, no one had twenty-five years of experience in Rust. Rust created a new context within which a relatively young programmer could become an authority, crate maintainer, compiler contributor, conference speaker or language designer pretty damn quickly with a lot less friction.

The whole project actively created routes into working groups and mentored contributions. This isn't cynicism, people genuinely contributed enormouse amounts. But it changes the sociology of adoption. A new language creates new experts.

The other thing this did, was let application programmers become "systems programmers". System programming traditionally had an apprenticeship barrier. You needed to learn pointers, linkers, compilation models, undefined behaviour, build systems, decades of C/C++ lore. Rust deliberately presented itself as a way of opening that world to a new generation.

In 2015 the Rust project itself described its potential as unlocking "a new generation of systems programmers," explicitly saying newcomers didn't already need to understand things like stacks and heaps.

The first Rust survey was noted for having a huge amount of interest from Python programmers. Rust offered something really potent, you didn't have to have been a systems programmer already to become one.

If you look at C++, it's not really one coherent environment. It's a language surrounded by CMaake, Make, Conan, vcpkg, compiler variations, platform toolchains, library discovery issues and project-specific conventions. Rust arrived with cargo build, cargo test, and cargo publish, crates.io and eventually rustfmt, Clippy and rustup.

Cargo was consciously modelled on the successful ecosystems around Bundler and npm. That created a shared cultural experience, two Rust programmers who have never met probably recognise approximately the same project structure and commands. Cargo didn't just solve dependency management, it actually helped to manufacture the community.

One of the things, if you were programming during this period, you'll know that OO was the prevailing approach to, well, everything. A lot developers had experience that culture through Java, Ruby, Python, PHP, JavaScript's explosion, and tangentially experienced functional programming concepts from Haskell/ML/Scala, immutable data, algebraic types, pattern matching and increasingly more complex type systems.

Rust bundled a huge amount of that intellectual movement into something that could still write an operating system. So there was a population of thinking, roughly, I like what we've learned since C++, but I don't want to give up control of the machine. Rust gave that population somewhere to go.

Lets talk about that huge initial lift of learning Rust and how it functioned as a sort of initiaition. Rust more or less famously asks beginners to change how they reason about programs. Fighting the borrow checker, eventually understanding why it rejected something, and then beginning to "think in Rust" produces a weirdly strong sense of acquired mastery.

Once that transition happens, contructs in other languages can start looking not merely different, but actually reckless. There's a sociological pattern in there, where difficulty followed by mastery can strengthen identification with the thing mastered. The borrow checker was simulataneously an adoption barrier, and paradoxically a community-binding mechanism.

So we look back at a specific point and we see a landscape that more or less looked like this:

  • C++ had become an establishment
  • Java, C# and the JVM represented managed enterprise computing
  • Dynamic languages had enjoyed an enormouse 200s/early-2010s uplift but weren't really systems languages
  • The functional languages had many of the intellectual ideas Rust developers found attractive but stayed in the periphery

Rust shows up and basically says: we can start systems programming over

Not necessarily in a simplistic way, but not with a VM, or under the constraints of C++. Or purely for one organisations commercial benefit. Not only that, youyes you can help decide what it becomes.

That's powerful, and it should also make you more sympathetic for the aggressive commmunity. Maybe the hardcore Rustaceans are a little annoying, but that annoyingness is probably a consequence of the conditions that actually made Rust successful.

I think if you look at the individual people, in constrained spaces that speak endlessly about the benefits of Rust, it's hard to see the wider framing. People can be really bad at articulating large concepts in the abstract, and it's very easy for things to erode into what feels like attacks.

That's doubly-so when the people doing the talking are also the people who are actually building the language, and in an industry where people have tendency to let their programming language of choice intermingle with their sense of identity and self.

If you look at things from these sort of isolated incidents, you see the "tribalism", "evangelism", and "fanaticism" that people complain about. But if you look at the wider context, you can see that it's a weird consequence of the conditions that made Rust successful in the first place. Maybe it's an unfortunate context, but invariably, people who find success with Rust, seem to want to share, and they want to share it with people who are willing to listen, and learn. Excitement can very easily be misinterpreted as something other than what it is, and the people who are excited about Rust are often people who have been involved in its development or day to day to use.

C/C++ were born in a different era, and in a academic context. There weren't enough people and avenues available for people to actually participate in the development of the language, or to speak it's praises. But I'd like to think that if the internet had been around in the 80's and 90's the way it is now, with the avenues for discussion we have now, that C/C++ would have had a similar community to Rust. But it didn't, and the same people that were involved in it's development are now in positions of authority and influence, and they have a different perspective on what systems programming should be, and how it should be done. The Rust community is a new generation of sysmtes programmers, and they're coming from it at a wholly different angle.
