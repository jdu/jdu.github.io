Part I. What Are We Building?

- Introduction — Set out what the book is trying to do: give engineers ways to
  see, reason about, build, change, and sustain software rather than prescribe
  one correct way to engineer it.
- Good is a Noun — Establish that "good" only exists relative to purpose,
  constraints, people, environment, and time. Reject universal notions of good
  architecture or good code.
- Software is Material — Explore software as something with shape, inertia,
  accumulated constraints, dependencies, interfaces, and history, even though
  it is not physically tangible.
- From Instructions to Systems — Begin with a simple program and progressively
  add state, persistence, processes, networks, users, and organisations to show
  how systems emerge.
- There is No Such Thing as Finished Software — Introduce change and
  sustainment as normal conditions of software rather than exceptional
  maintenance work.
- Constraints Are the Problem — Explore technical, organisational, financial,
  regulatory, temporal, and human constraints, and distinguish genuine
  constraints from inherited assumptions.

Part II. Looking Inside

- Code Has Shape — Teach readers to see structure in code: clusters,
  dependencies, flows, responsibilities, seams, repetition, and irregularity.
- State Changes Everything — Show how introducing mutable or persistent state
  changes the reasoning required around otherwise simple software.
- Coupling — Explore the many forms of coupling, including behavioural,
  temporal, data, implementation, operational, and organisational coupling,
  without treating coupling as inherently bad.
- Cohesion — Examine why some behaviours and data naturally belong together and
  how cohesion helps expose useful boundaries.
- Boundaries Have a Cost — Show that every boundary introduces translation,
  contracts, coordination, failure modes, and cognitive overhead.
- Ownership — Explore who or what owns state, behaviour, resources, decisions,
  schemas, and lifecycle, and why unclear ownership creates complexity.

Part III. Yer Toolbox

- Calling Things — Explore direct invocation: functions, methods, commands,
  procedure calls, RPC, HTTP calls, and other ways one piece of software asks
  another to do work.
- Moving Things — Cover messages, events, streams, queues, files, pipes, and
  other mechanisms for moving information between parts of a system.
- Remembering Things — Introduce memory, files, databases, logs, caches, object
  storage, and the different kinds of persistence available to engineers.
- Dividing Things — Explore modules, packages, libraries, processes, services,
  plugins, applications, and the consequences of splitting software apart.
- Doing Things Later — Cover scheduling, delayed work, queues, jobs, callbacks,
  asynchronous execution, and deferred processing.
- Doing Many Things, At Once — Introduce concurrency, parallelism, threads,
  processes, workers, actors, and distributed work as different ways of
  arranging simultaneous activity.
- Letting Other People In — Explore APIs, command-line interfaces, extension
  points, plugins, embedded languages, and other mechanisms for opening
  software to external use.
- Interfaces and Protocols — Treat interfaces as agreements between
  independently changing things, covering syntax, semantics, compatibility,
  versioning, and failure behaviour.

Part IV. Building Blocks

- Values and Representation — Explore how raw values represent things in the
  world and how representation choices affect what can be expressed or
  confused.
- Types and Meaning — Show how type systems, enums, records, structures,
  optionality, units, and richer representations can preserve meaning and
  constrain invalid states.
- Naming Things — Examine naming as modelling: how vocabulary shapes
  understanding, reveals ambiguity, and creates shared or conflicting
  interpretations of a domain.
- Functions and Composition — Explore functions as mechanisms for naming
  behaviour, creating seams, constraining effects, and composing larger
  behaviours from smaller ones.
- Control Flow — Cover sequencing, branching, iteration, recursion, pipelines,
  events, and state machines as different shapes of behaviour rather than
  merely syntax.
- State — Examine local, shared, persistent, cached, derived, and distributed
  state, and the problems introduced by mutation.
- Identity — Explore what makes something the same thing over time, including
  identifiers, equality, references, natural keys, and object identity.
- Resources and Lifetime — Examine allocation, ownership, sharing, exhaustion,
  cleanup, memory, files, sockets, database connections, locks, and other
  finite resources.
- Contracts and Invariants — Explore the assumptions that must remain true and
  where those assumptions can be expressed or enforced.
- Indirection — Cover references, pointers, lookup tables, interfaces, handles,
  proxies, dependency injection, DNS, and other ways software separates
  identity from location or implementation.
- Boundaries — Bring the building blocks together to examine the different
  kinds of boundary an engineer can intentionally create inside software.

Part V. Beneath the Software

- The Machine Underneath — Build a working mental model from application code
  down through runtimes, system calls, kernels, processors, memory, storage,
  and devices.
- From Source Code to Running Program — Explain compilation, interpretation,
  linking, loading, runtimes, bytecode, JIT compilation, executable artefacts,
  and process creation.
- What Happens When You Click the Button? — Trace a real interaction through
  input devices, operating systems, browsers or applications, networking,
  servers, storage, and back again.
- Data Is Bytes Eventually — Follow information from domain concepts through
  structures, serialization, encoding, compression, files, memory, and network
  packets.
- Processes, Threads and Work — Explain processes, threads, event loops,
  coroutines, blocking, scheduling, concurrency, and parallelism from the
  machine upward.
- Networks Are Machines Too — Demystify DNS, addresses, routing, ports, TCP,
  UDP, QUIC, TLS, proxies, NAT, load balancers, and connection management.
- The Problem of Time — Explore clocks, ordering, deadlines, latency, retries,
  TTLs, leases, eventual consistency, and the fact that distributed systems
  cannot assume one shared present.
- Queues Mean Pressure — Explain buffers, queues, backpressure, throughput,
  rate mismatch, and the unavoidable question of where excess work accumulates.
- Caches Create Two Truths — Treat caching as creation of another
  representation of state, bringing synchronization, invalidation, ownership,
  and consistency problems.
- Configuration Is Software — Explore configuration files, environment
  variables, flags, secrets, and the point at which configuration becomes a
  programming language.
- Deployment Is Part of the Design — Follow software from source through build,
  packaging, configuration, deployment, execution, upgrade, rollback, and
  retirement.
- Errors Are Information — Examine error values, exceptions, partial failure,
  ambiguity, retryability, and the difference between knowing something failed
  and not knowing what happened.
- Observability Is an Interface — Treat logs, metrics, traces, diagnostics,
  admin tools, and runtime introspection as the interface a running system
  exposes to its maintainers.
- Performance Is Shape — Explore algorithms, I/O, allocations, serialization,
  round trips, locality, batching, contention, parallelism, and how to discover
  where time is actually being spent.
- Dependencies Are Decisions — Frame libraries, frameworks, services, vendors,
  and platforms as exchanges of work now for obligations and constraints later.
- Every Abstraction Has a Floor — Show how abstractions eventually terminate in
  lower layers someone may need to understand when behaviour escapes the
  abstraction.
- Interfaces Outlive Implementations — Explore why APIs, schemas, formats,
  protocols, and assumptions often survive long after the implementations
  behind them have been replaced.

Part VI. Patterns Without Pattern Matching

- What a Pattern Actually Is — Define patterns as recurring shapes that appear
  when similar forces are resolved in similar ways, rather than recipes to be
  applied.
- Learn the Shape, Forget the Name — Use familiar patterns to develop
  recognition and intuition without encouraging catalogue-driven software
  design.
- Forces Before Patterns — Teach readers to identify the pressures and
  constraints that lead towards particular structural choices before naming a
  pattern.
- Patterns Compose — Show how real systems contain overlapping and interacting
  patterns rather than clean textbook implementations of one pattern at a time.
- When the Pattern Stops Helping — Explore cargo culting, accidental
  frameworks, preserved abstractions, and cases where the original forces no
  longer justify the pattern.

Part VII. Designing Software

- Start With the Smallest Thing — Begin with the least machinery capable of
  expressing the actual problem and allow complexity to earn its way into the
  design.
- Find the Constraints — Identify which forces genuinely limit the design and
  which are preferences, assumptions, inherited conventions, or negotiable
  boundaries.
- Count the Costs — Evaluate computational, financial, cognitive, operational,
  coordination, dependency, and future-change costs rather than optimizing
  along one dimension.
- Find the Things That Belong Together — Use behaviour, data, lifecycle, rate
  of change, ownership, and shared invariants to discover useful code and
  system boundaries.
- Draw the System — Teach a lightweight graphical vocabulary for communicating
  containment, flow, state, ownership, uncertainty, interfaces, and boundaries.
- Walk the Data — Trace one real piece of information through a system to
  reveal transformations, dependencies, storage, ownership, and hidden
  assumptions.
- Walk the Failure — Follow failures through the same system to understand
  containment, ambiguity, retry behaviour, recovery, and propagation.
- Walk the Change — Introduce a hypothetical requirement and trace where the
  change travels to expose coupling and assess the shape of the design.
- Preserve Reversibility — Distinguish useful optionality from speculative
  future-proofing and identify decisions whose cost justifies keeping an exit
  path.

Part VIII. Engineering Leverage

- Make the Machine Do the Repetition — Establish leverage as encoding repeated
  structure or decisions so humans decide once and machines reproduce
  consistently.
- Functions Aren't the Only Abstraction — Compare functions, generics, traits,
  templates, macros, generators, schemas, and languages as different ways of
  abstracting different kinds of repetition.
- Macros — Explore declarative, procedural, attribute, and derive-style macros
  using systems such as Rust to show both their structural power and their
  debugging costs.
- Templates and Code Generation — Explore generation of code, schemas, clients,
  configuration, documentation, deployment artefacts, and other mechanically
  derived structures.
- Single Sources of Structure — Examine how one authoritative representation
  can safely generate many others, and when generated layers instead obscure
  where truth lives.
- Declarative Versus Imperative — Explore the trade-off between describing
  desired outcomes and explicitly controlling the steps used to produce them.
- Small Languages — Show how configuration, rules, query languages, format
  strings, parsers, and DSLs emerge, intentionally or accidentally.
- Encode Decisions, Not Just Repetition — Explore how compilers, types,
  schemas, formatters, linters, generators, and tooling can move engineering
  knowledge out of human memory and into machinery.
- The Cost of Magic — Examine abstraction as knowledge compression and the cost
  of mentally decompressing hidden behaviour when debugging or modifying a
  system.

Part IX. Breaking Down the Work

- From Problem to Work — Show how vague outcomes become concrete engineering
  work without prematurely turning requirements into technical solutions.
- Find the Thin Slice — Find the smallest end-to-end piece of functionality
  that produces useful evidence and exercises real parts of the system.
- Build to Learn — Distinguish exploratory code, spikes, prototypes,
  experiments, and disposable work from software intended to be sustained.
- Make Uncertainty Visible — Explicitly separate what is known, assumed,
  undecided, and still needing discovery.
- Estimates Are Models — Treat estimates as models built from incomplete
  information rather than promises or predictions of the future.
- Feedback Loops — Explore how quickly engineers can move from an action to
  evidence through compilation, testing, deployment, observability, user
  feedback, and communication.

Part X. Reading Existing Software

- Where Do I Start? — Give engineers practical ways into unfamiliar codebases
  without attempting to understand everything before touching anything.
- Find the Seams — Locate entry points, boundaries, persistent state,
  integration points, dependency edges, and places where behaviour can be
  isolated.
- Follow Something Real — Trace an actual request, command, record, message, or
  user interaction through the code instead of reading files in arbitrary
  order.
- Read the History — Use version control, commit history, old documentation,
  issues, and removed code to understand why current structures exist.
- Recognising Accretion — Distinguish complexity inherent to the problem from
  complexity accumulated through history, workarounds, abandoned ideas, and
  changing constraints.
- Working the Problem — Reproduce, reduce, trace, measure, isolate, inspect
  lower layers, read source, and build toy examples when faced with unfamiliar
  behaviour.
- Changing Things Safely — Reason about blast radius, reversibility,
  confidence, observation, compatibility, and incremental change rather than
  relying on generic refactoring rules.

Part XI. Building With Other People

- Communicating Designs — Explore sketches, diagrams, prose, examples,
  prototypes, and conversations as different tools for creating shared
  understanding.
- How to Disagree About Software — Move disagreements away from taste and
  status towards forces, constraints, evidence, alternatives, consequences, and
  trade-offs.
- Making Decisions at Your Vantage Point — Show that architectural reasoning
  belongs to every engineer and can be practised from wherever someone stands
  in a system.
- Knowing What You Don't Know — Recognise the boundaries of your understanding,
  when assumptions need validating, and when a problem has crossed into
  somebody else's expertise.
- Coordination Has a Cost — Explore the price of shared ownership, cross-team
  dependencies, distributed decision-making, and designs that require
  continuous agreement between people.

Part XII. Building Confidence

- What Does Correct Mean? — Establish correctness as a collection of
  behavioural, temporal, security, durability, performance, and domain claims
  rather than simply producing the expected output.
- Tests Are Evidence — Treat tests as evidence supporting claims about a system
  rather than proof of quality or correctness.
- Test the Things That Must Remain True — Connect tests to contracts,
  invariants, and behaviours that should survive changes in implementation.
- Test the Boundary — Focus confidence-building effort where independent
  assumptions meet: APIs, processes, databases, files, queues, and external
  systems.
- Test the Failure — Exercise timeouts, unavailable dependencies, duplicates,
  partial execution, resource exhaustion, invalid inputs, and other non-happy
  paths.
- Make Failure Reproducible — Turn intermittent behaviour into repeatable
  experiments that can be observed, measured, and reasoned about.
- Static Evidence — Cover type systems, compilers, schemas, static analysis,
  linters, model checking, and other ways of establishing confidence before
  execution.
- Production Is Evidence Too — Use runtime behaviour, incidents, logs, metrics,
  traces, user behaviour, and real workloads to challenge assumptions made
  before deployment.
- Confidence Has a Cost — Explore how much evidence different levels of risk
  justify and why certainty is neither free nor completely attainable.

Part XIII. Trust and Authority

- Who Gets to Do What? — Introduce security through the simple question of
  which actor is allowed to cause which effect.
- Trust Boundaries — Identify where information, authority, or control crosses
  between areas with different assumptions of trust.
- Identity Is Not Authority — Separate knowing who or what something is from
  deciding what it is permitted to do.
- Authentication and Authorization — Explore mechanisms for establishing
  identity and assigning permissions without conflating the two.
- Capabilities and Privilege — Examine limited authority, scoped access, least
  privilege, and the architectural effect of restricting what components can
  do.
- Input Comes From Somewhere — Treat data entering from users, files, APIs,
  queues, configuration, databases, and devices as crossing assumptions and
  trust boundaries.
- Secrets — Explore credentials, keys, tokens, secret distribution, rotation,
  lifecycle, accidental propagation, and why secrets reshape architecture.
- Delegation and Impersonation — Examine systems acting on behalf of users or
  other systems and how delegated authority should be represented and
  constrained.
- Provenance and Audit — Record not only what happened but who or what caused
  it, where information came from, and what transformations were applied.
- Assume Less — Show how reducing the number of trusted components, actors, and
  implicit guarantees can simplify reasoning about security.

Part XIV. At the Edge

- Programs With People In Them — Explore how software changes once humans
  become part of the control loop and can pause, reconsider, abandon, repeat,
  or contradict actions.
- Events and Interaction — Cover clicks, gestures, keyboard input, timers,
  notifications, sensors, and other event-driven inputs.
- Rendering State — Treat interfaces as representations of underlying state and
  explore what happens when displayed state and authoritative state diverge.
- Local State and Remote State — Examine the relationship between state on a
  device and state maintained elsewhere.
- Humans Are Asynchronous — Explore abandoned workflows, multiple windows,
  stale screens, returning later, interruptions, and other behaviours machines
  must accommodate.
- Waiting — Treat latency as a human design concern as well as a performance
  metric.
- Offline Is a State — Explore disconnected and intermittently connected
  systems without treating lack of connectivity solely as failure.
- Synchronisation — Examine reconciliation, conflicts, ownership, ordering,
  merging, and multiple independently changing copies of information.
- Undo Changes the Design — Show how reversibility affects persistence, command
  modelling, history, side effects, and destructive operations.
- Devices Have Constraints — Explore battery, memory, storage, sensors,
  processing capability, intermittent networks, platform policies, and physical
  interfaces.
- The Browser Is a Computer — Reconnect browser development to processes,
  memory, storage, networking, rendering, security boundaries, and the
  underlying machine.
- Desktop, Mobile, Terminal and Beyond — Compare different application surfaces
  to expose the common engineering ideas underneath platform-specific
  frameworks.

Part XV. Data and History

- Data Has Shape — Explore rows, documents, graphs, events, objects, files,
  streams, and other representations as different structural choices.
- Schemas Are Models — Treat schemas as statements about the world and the
  assumptions the software makes about what can exist.
- Keys and Identity — Examine how persisted information identifies entities and
  why stable identity is difficult.
- Relationships — Explore references, foreign keys, embedding, composition,
  graph relationships, and other ways of representing connection.
- Constraints Belong Near the Data — Examine where data invariants should live
  and what happens when every consumer must independently remember them.
- Transactions — Explore which changes become visible together, what
  consistency guarantees mean, and where transactional boundaries should sit.
- Mutable State and Recorded History — Compare overwriting the current state
  with retaining the sequence of events or changes that produced it.
- Derived Data — Distinguish authoritative information from information that
  can be recomputed and examine the danger of accidental competing truths.
- Copies Create Obligations — Explore replication, caching, exports,
  denormalisation, synchronization, and what it means to have more than one
  representation of information.
- Ownership of Data — Ask who has authority to create, modify, interpret,
  publish, migrate, and delete information.
- Migration — Explore changing schemas and representations while existing
  applications and data continue to operate.
- Compatibility Across Time — Examine old programs reading new data, new
  programs reading old data, mixed deployments, queued messages, and long-lived
  formats.
- Provenance — Track where information originated, what transformed it, and how
  confidently its current meaning can be understood.
- Deletion Is a Design Problem — Explore cascading deletion, retention, derived
  copies, backups, audit requirements, tombstones, and the difficulty of making
  information genuinely disappear.
- Data Outlives Programs — Close on the idea that software implementations are
  temporary custodians of information whose lifetime may be measured in
  decades.

Part XVI. Keeping It Alive

- Build for the Next Person — Design with the assumption that somebody
  unfamiliar with the system will eventually need to understand and alter it.
- Legibility — Treat the ability to form an accurate mental model of software
  as a core engineering property distinct from stylistic cleanliness.
- Repairability — Explore diagnostics, inspection, escape hatches, replacement,
  isolation, manual intervention, and the ability to get inside a broken
  system.
- Changeability — Examine what makes reasonable change possible without
  attempting to support every hypothetical future.
- Compatibility and Migration — Treat coexistence between old and new states as
  a normal part of software evolution rather than an exceptional transformation
  programme.
- Leaving Things Alone — Explore restraint, stability, and the cases where
  changing software creates more risk than continuing to live with
  imperfection.
- Removing Things — Treat deletion of features, code, dependencies, data,
  infrastructure, and whole systems as a deliberate engineering capability.
- Sustainment — Bring together legibility, confidence, repairability,
  knowledge, operation, change, migration, and eventual replacement as the
  long-term work of engineering software.
- Over Muskeg — Close on the central metaphor: engineering is not about finding
  permanently solid ground, but understanding what moves, what must carry load,
  what can be allowed to flex, and how to keep something useful standing as the
  ground beneath it changes.
