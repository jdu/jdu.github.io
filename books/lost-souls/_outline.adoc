= Software for Lost Souls

We have increasingly substituted the selection and arrangement of technologies for the act of understanding and designing software. The answer is not technological austerity, but recovering engineering agency: choosing proportionate abstractions, owning the meaningful parts of our systems, and using modern languages and tools to build software we can understand.

Engagement -> Explanation -> New Ways of Seeing -> Practical Discipline -> Stewardship

== Part 1 - Lost

* Software for Lost Soulds
** what it means to feel estranged from your own systems: unable to explain them, change them safely, or understand where behaviour comes from.
* The Landscape of Pieces
** Modern development as assembly: frameworks, platforms, packages, APIs, configuration systems, generators, and managed services.
* The Disappearing Program
** How the actual program becomes buried beneath build systems, depedenncy grpahs, runtime machiner and conventions.
* Nobody designed this
** Systems that emerge from thousands of individuall reasonable choices without ever being consciously designed as a whole.
* The Framework Mind
** How engineers gradually leanr to think in terms of available products and patterns rather than the problemn itself.
* Borrow Understanding
** Every dependenc import assumptions, operational behaviour, and failure modes that the team may not understand.

== Part II - How We Became Lost

* Abstraction feels like progress
** Why indirection, generalisation, and automation are so intellectually attractive.
* The Fear of Reinvention
** How "never build what already exists" became a dogma rather than a useful heuristic.
* The Safety of Consensus
** Choosing popular tools feels professionally safer than making a smaller, more deliberate technical decision.
* Complexity Without Responsibility
** Outsourced complexity still belongs to you, even when it lives in a framework, cloud service, or package registry.
* The Industry of Motion
** Why the software industry rewards novelty, migration, and adoption more visibly than maintenance and restraint.
* The Lost Confidence of the Programmer
** How engineers came to trust ecosystems, frameworks, and architecture diagrams more than their own ability to build software.


== Part III - Seeing Software Again

* Start with the outcome
** The purpose of software is not to contain particular technologies, it's to reliably produce an outcome for someone.
* The Shape of the Program
** Recovering a clear understanding of inputs, state, behaviour, boundaries, and outputs.
* Local Reasoning
** Good software allows an engineer to understand one part without loading the entire system into their head.
* Visible Machinery
** Prefer systems whose important behaviour can be inspected, traced, and explained.
* The Cost of Distance
** Every layer between intent and execution makes software harder to reason about.
* Abstraction Has a Budget
** Abstraction is not inherently bad, but it must earn its place by removing more complexity than it introduces.
* Meaning Before Reuse
** Code organised around what the system means is often more durable than code organised around technical patterns.

== Part IV - Building Directly

* Choose a Capable Language
** Modern languages can absorb responsibilities that were once delegated to frameworks, generators, and extern tooling.
* Use Libraries, Not Worlds
** The distinction between adopting a focused capability and surrendering the shape of your system to an ecosystem.
* BUild the Small thing
** Many internal systems need less machinery than teams assume.
* Own the Core
** Identify the behaviour that gives the system its meaning and keep it understandable and under your control.
* Keep State Honest
** Make state, persistence, and transitions explicit rather than hiding them behind layers of convenience.
* Prefer Boring Boundaries
** Files, queues, HTTP, SQL and simple data structures survive longer than elaborate system abstractions.
* Design for Removal
** Components should be replaceable, but the system shouldn't be constructed as though everything will be replaced tomorrow.
* Tools that Increase Agency
** Compilers, type systems, debuggers, profilers, macros systems, tests, and observability should strengthen the engineer rather than obscure the program.
* Deployment Is Part of the Program
** A system isn't maintainable when its code is simple but its build and runtime environment are incomreprehensible.

== Part V - Maintaining Meaning

* THe Second Engineer
** Design for the person who inherits the system without its original context.
* Software is Institutional Memory
** Code carries decisions, language and assumptions long after the people who made them have gone.
* Maintenance is the Product
** Delivery is a momment. Maintenance is the majority of the softwares life.
* Change without Excavation
** A healthy ssytem allows changes without archaeological expeditions through frameworks, and dependency internals.
* The Right Kind of Duplication
** Some duplication preserves meaning and independence better than premature generalisation.
* Stability is a feature
** The value of software that does not require continual migration to remain alive.
* Leaving a Smaller System Behind
** Good engineering should reduce the burden inherited by the next person.

== Part VI - Finding Our Way Back

* Not Minimalism
** The goal is not fewer lines of code, fewer tools or technologocial asceticism. It's proportionality.
* Not Reinventing Everything
** Reclaiming engineering judgement doesn't mean rejecting libraries, platforms, or existing work.
* The Courage to Choose Less
** Simpler systems often require more confidence because their decisions can't be justified by popularity alone.
* Programming is understanding
** Writing software is a way of developing a precise understanding of a problem, not just confgiguration a solution.
* Software for Human Beings
** Software should be understandable by the people who build it, dependable for the people who use it, and survivable for those who inherit it.
