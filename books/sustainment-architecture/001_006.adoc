= Truth

In the previous chapter we argued that architecture begins with meaning. Before an organisation can
build software, integrate systems or produce reports, it first has to agree on waht the things inside
it actually are.

Truth is an important problem.

Meaning asks whether everyone is talking about the same thing.

Trust asks whether what they are saying about it is still honest.

Those two ideas are often confused beacuse they fail in similar ways. An organisation might agree
perfectly on waht an _Application_ or a _Grant_ is while slowly losing the ability to describe
what's actually happening to eithe of them. The language remains stable, but reality quietly drifts
away from the story being told about it.

That separation rarely happens deliberately, and usually not all at once, but it does begin with 
small incentives.

A metric becomes a target, and the moment it does people begin optimising for the number
instead of the thing the number was supposed to measure. Reports become political because poor results
have consequences for whoever owns them. Dashboards stop asking what is happening and start presenting
whichever version of events is least likely to cause uncomfortable conversations.

Definitions drift for similar reasons. A report that once measured one thing gradually measures something
slightly different ebcasue the old definition no longer tells a flattering story. Exceptions accumulate.
Caveats multiple. Eventually two reports disagree, not because either contains incorrect data, but because they're
no longer attempting to describe the same reality.

Absolutely none of this requires people to be _dishonest_ or to have done this wholly out of _malice_.

Most of this emerges from perfectly reasonable people responding perfectly understandable incentives. That's 
precisely why it's so difficult to notive it while it's happening. There's usually moments though when it all
becomes visible. Generally when your meetings start with "Which numbers are right?". 

Again, it's easy to chock this up as a technical problem, but it's really not.

People stop trusting data long before they stop collecting it. Once confidence has been lost, every report
becomes negotiable, every dahboard becomes suspect, and every decision begins with an argument about evidence
rather than a decision itself. An organsisation can continue functioning like this for years, even decades, but it's 
no longer navigating by observation. It's navigating by competing interpretations of reality.

There's a profound lesson hiding inside that pattern, and it's that our architecture isn't just how we 
organise our systems. 

It's the discipline of preserbing an honest description of reality, even when that reality is _inconvenient_ or unflattering.

LIneage, provenance, audit trails, semantic models, and goverance are often justified in terms of compliance, regulation,
and operational efficiency. Those are worthwhile outcomes, but they're secondary. Their deeper purpose is much
simpler: they help an organisation remain capable of telling itself the truth.

That truth will sometimes be uncomfortable.

* A programme is failing.
* A migration is behind schedule
* A process no longer works
* A metric was measuring the wrong thing all along

An architecture that can't represent those realities is honeslty not protecting the organisation. It's protecting
the organisation from itself. Every organisation eventually becomes separated from reality.

Processes become ritual. Metrics become performance. Systems become assumptions. Documentation becomes mythology.

The distance grows slowly enough that nobody notices from one month to the next, yet over years it becomes enormous.

The work of architecture is not to eliminate that distance completely—that is impossible.

It is to shorten it.

To build systems that make reality visible again.

To preserve not only shared meaning, but shared truth.

Because an organisation that can no longer describe itself honestly has already lost something far more valuable than accurate reports.

It has lost the ability to learn.


* Meaning, from chapter 5, is agreeing what something means. Truth is a separate problem, whether what gets reported about it is still honest, and organisations eventually stop describing reality even when the meaning was never in question. It rarely happens all at once.
* Metrics become targets, and the moment they do, people optimise for the number instead of the thing the number was supposed to represent. Reports become political, because admitting a number is bad has consequences for whoever owns it.
