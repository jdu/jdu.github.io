= Time is architectures real material

Building are made of steel and concrete. Software is made of code. Architecture, 
the kind this book is actually about, is made of time.

Every architectural decision _spends_ tomorrow.

Sometimes deliberately, more often than not, accidentally.

The systems we inherit are less a snapshot of engineering than a fossil record of 
every compromise, assumption, and shortcut that survived long enough to become normal.
What we call architecture is really the accumulated relationships between an organisation
and time itself.

This chapter argues that time is architecture's true material. Everything else is
simply the medium through which it acts.

== Buildings stand still, organisations don't

A bridge does not wake up one morning and decide it's not a hospital.

An organisation can.

Hospitals become research institutes, universities become global charities, companies 
discover new markets, governments change policy, and crises appear without
any sort of formal invitation.

Architecture is therefore can't simply describe what exists.

It needs to allow the organisation to become something that doesn't exist yet.

That's why architecture is measured less by how well today's systems fit totday's
organisation, and more by how cheaply tomorrow's organisation can emerge.

== Every decision purchases a future

Architectural decisions are often described as "investments".

They're closer to a mortgage than anything else. Every choice commits future
engineers to living with its consequences.

Some of those decision will make tomorrow cheaper, others though will quietly make
every future change slightly more expensive until nobody remembers where the 
cost came from.

[[sidenote:tech_debt]]
--
Technical debt is only one form of debt.

Organisations also accumulate semantic debt, organisational debt, governance debt, 
and historical debt.
--

Good architecture isn't the absence of future work, it's choosing which future work you're
willing to pay for [[ref:tech_debt]].

== Stat is time made explicit

Most systems never model state, they infer it, a checkbox, status field, date. Maybe three 
booleans that are supposed to agree.

Somebody writes documentation explaining how to recontruct the real state from the combination 
of values.

That documentation immediately begins to age.

State isn't simply a field, it's identity observed through time. Every meaningful thing in an organisaiton
moves through recognizable states. 

* An Application is drafted
* Submitted
* Reviewed
* Awarded
* Closed

The transitions between those states are important, just as important as the states themselves. Without
explicit transitions, two people can legitimately disagree abhout what is happening
while looking at the exact same record.

If two ssytesm disagree about state, ask which transition they disagree about.

The answer is usually more revealing than comparing the data itself.

== The cost of yesterday

One of the strangest properties of organisations is that they negotiate with people who 
are no longer actually in the organisation.

A schema exists because sombody once chose it, and approval exisets because somebnody once worried.

An integration exists because two products once needed to co-exist.

Years latery, none of those contexts actually remain. Only the decision has survived, so the architecture
in these places isn't coupled just to the systems which implement it, it's coupled to the history of
the organisation itself.

Changing today's organisation often requries renegotiating decisions made by people whose names people 
don't even remember, and that's an extraordinarily expensive way to carry institutional memory.

== End of Chapter Reflection

Choose one important system.

List three decisions made when it was built that you would not make today.

Then ask a different question:

*How much would it cost to change your mind?*

That number, more than the age of the technology or the elegance of the code, tells you how your
architecture has behaved over time.
