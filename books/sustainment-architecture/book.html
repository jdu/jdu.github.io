Preface

When I was a kid growing up in Northwestern Ontario, Canada, I spent a lot of time in my father's garage. He was the person everyone always brought their machines to, broken outboard motors, snowmobiles, motorcycles, cars, trucks, even the occasional lawnmower. He would take them apart, figure out what had failed and why, and then put them back together again. It was insane to me as a kid how quickly my father could go from a "hello" to the exact thing that was wrong. The machines themselves were always cool, but what really got me was the story that each broken machine told about how it had been designed, built, used, and abused.

I really wanted to know how these things worked, and more importantly how they failed.

Why this bearing wore before that one. Why a seal leaked. Why a bolt sheared. Why one part could survive decades while another failed after a few years. Every broken component told a story about the forces acting on it, and every repair revealed something about how the whole machine had originally been designed. How could this one rusted, beat up old engine run for decades while a brand new one failed after a few months?

Engines specifically are extremely, deceptively simple. At their heart, they really only need three things: air, fuel and a spark. That's almost disappointingly straightforward. But from just those few ingredients emerges one of the most refined pieces of engineering humanity has ever produced. Thousands of individual decisions about timing, compression, cooling, lubrication, tolerances and materials all exist to serve that remarkably simple idea. The principle is easy to explain. Building an engine that survives hundreds of thousands of miles without tearing itself apart is something else entirely.

The principles that make an old motorcycle engine reliable are fundamentally the same principles that underpin Formula One engines, aircraft turbines and spacecraft. The environments become harsher, the engineering more sophisticated and the margins for error almost disappear, but the underlying ideas remain recognisable. Complexity does not eliminate fundamentals. If anything, it makes them more important. The more demanding the system becomes, the less room there is to misunderstand the small number of things on which everything else depends.

Understanding failure teaches you things that success often hides.

A machine that runs perfectly doesn't tell you much about where its real weaknesses are. It's only when something breaks that the hidden assumptions become visible. The loads you underestimated, the compromises you forgot you made, the interfaces that looked simple until they were stressed. Failure exposes the seams.

That habit of looking for seams never really left me, I sort of revel in them now.

As my career moved from software engineering into distributed systems, data platforms and enterprise architecture, I found myself asking the same questions, only about organisations instead of machines.

  • Why do migrations become impossible?
  • Why do reports disagree?
  • Why do organisations repeatedly lose knowledge they once possessed?
  • Why do people lie about success and shy away from failure?

Why do systems that were entirely reasonable when they were built become almost impossible to replace twenty years later?

Why do capable people spend so much of their time translating between different versions of what should be the same idea?

Again and again, the interesting answers rarely sat in the technology itself. They were in the places where understanding had fractured. Where language had drifted. Where context had been lost. Where one decision accumulated consequences until changing it was more expensive than continuing to live with it.

This book has grown out of this obsession.

It's not a book about designing perfect architectures. I don't believe that exists. Organisations are living systems. They change continuously because the world around them changes continuously. Any architecture that assumes otherwise will just become part of the problem it was trying to solve.

Instead, this is a book about understanding where organisations fail, how they forget, how they become separated from reality, and what kinds of architecture allow them to survive it.

Because if my father's workshop taught me anything, it was that the most interesting question is rarely "How does this work?"

It's:

"What has to happen before this stops working?"

Architecture, at its best, is the discipline of ensuring that when that day comes, the organisation understands enough about itself that it can grease the bearings, torque the bolts, hone out the cylinders, and put it all back together again to keep it chugging along.

Welcome

This is a book about a lot of things. But at the heart of it, it's a culmination of decades of working within organisations that have a huge, wide, varied, and constantly changing remit that doesn't always align with the needs of the people who work within them.

Introduction

My formal title doesn't include the term "architect", take from that what you will. Although I don't have the title, I've been doing the work of an architect for a long time, and through that time, and the longer period of being in software engineering, design, and operations, I've got a few opinions about what architecture really is, what people think it is, and what it should actually be.

Live long enough in engineering and eventually you'll become involved with various architects, and leading roles that are often described as "architectural". You'll see people with those titles treated with a kind of reverence, and in some cases even a certain amount of fear.

1.1 What's this book about then?

If you picked up this book expecting another enterprise architecture framework, you're probably going to be disappointed.

There are no maturity models to progress through, no metamodel to memorise, no certification to pass, and no claim that everything would be easier if organisations simply adopted one more methodology. There are already enough frameworks, reference architectures and modelling notations in the world. Many of them are useful. This book is not trying to replace them.

It's not really about software architecture either, even though it's spending a lot of time discussing software.

It's really about the things that sit underneath all of that other stuff, and more importantly it's about how organisations understand themselves.

1
2

Technology runs like a thread the chapters because it's one of the primary ways that organisations express understanding, but technology isn't the subject. Neither are data models, operating models, governance processes or organisational charts. These are all temporary expressions of something that's longer-lasting: the organisation's own understanding of what it is, what it does and why.

The arguments throughout this book are that architecture has gradually become obsessed with describing systems while putting less and less attention on describing the organisation those systems exist to serve. As organisations become larger and more complicated, that missing layer becomes extremely expensive. Systems drift apart, language and vocabulary diverges, migrations become harder, reports disagree and institutional knowledge disappears. None of that is necessarily down to poor technical choices, it more or less arrives because organisations lose a coherent way to describe themselves.

The outcomes are pretty clear, and more and more organisations are hitting walls that are increasingly more expensive to work around. The cost of forgotten knowledge is high, and the cost of rediscovering it's even higher. Along the way, we can end up demoralizing the people who are trying to do the work, slowing progress, and we end up with systems that are more brittle, more expensive, and more difficult to change than they need to be.

In some of the worst cases we end up responsible for psychological harm to the people who are trying to do what's best for the organisation.

The chapters that follow borrow from a variety of disciplines. There's enterprise architecture here, but also systems thinking, ontology engineering, software architecture, military sustainment, cybernetics, distributed systems, philosophy, linguistics and organisational design. None of these disciplines individually is the whole answer. But, individually, they illuminate parts of the same problem from different perspectives.

If we needed to create a single, unified "theme" that runs through this book, it would likely look something like this:

Architecture is the discipline of preserving an organisation's understanding of itself while everything else changes.

All the other pieces, things like frameworks, diagrams, ontologies, application, APIs, data models, governance, principles, and platforms, are ultimately aligned in service of that goal.

1.2 Synthesis

None of the ideas in this book are new, nor are they presented as though they are, or as if I've come up with them in some cathartic understanding of the universe as a whole. Enterprise architecture, systems thinking, cybernetics, ontology engineering, Domain-Driven Design, military sustainment, lean manufacturing, complexity science, software architecture and countless other disciplines have spent decades exploring different parts of the same landscape.

Many of the tools, techniques and observations described here have long histories, and many have been refined by practitioners working in domains far removed from software. The intention here isn't to replace those bodies of work, it's to bring them together in a coherent understanding that addresses a problem they treat separately: how an organisation preserves its understanding of itself while everything around it continues to change.

3

Less common I think, is the angle that we're viewing these ideas from. Traditional enterprise architecture often begins with systems, applications, capabilities or organisational structures and works upward towards the business. This book takes the opposite approach. It begins with meaning, identity, culture and stewardship, and treats technology as ultimately a temporary expression of those things.

From that perspective, ontologies, architectural decision records, fitness functions, process models, knowledge graphs and interface standards stop being isolated techniques. They become different tools for preserving the organisation's ability to understand itself, to adapt deliberately, and to carry that understanding safely through change. If there is anything original here, it's not the individual techniques, it's the synthesis: seeing them as parts of a single discipline concerned less with building systems, and more with stewarding meaning over time.

1.3 Architecture is additive

There are a lot of people who think that architecture is just about making decisions, and that the role itself is more or less about being the person who makes those decisions. In some organisations, the role is considered a person whose job it is to justify decisions that have already been made, or to be the person who makes decisions that others are unwilling or too afraid to make.

We utilize frameworks and tools like TOGAF, C4, and Archimate to help use make those decision, or rationalize those decisions within the confines of the organisation's existing processes and structures.

It's often an additive process, where you're expected to figure out how new decisions and technology slot into a larger, growingly complex system. You're supposed to see the whole, know where the pieces fit, and make sure that the pieces are all compatible with each other, and if they're not, find ways to make them compatible.

Architects can produce thick reams of diagrams, specifications, and documentation, but the reality is that most of that work is actually never really read. People pick out the individual pieces of information they need, and ignore the rest. The diagrams are dense, complex, and require an understanding of the context and time that they were produced within.

At it's ultimate worst, you are a sink for risk. Avoid mistakes, and if you can't avoid them, be the person who takes the blame for them, and defend your decisions through extensive documentation of trade-offs, and the reasoning behind them. File your critiques of decisions made by others so that you have something to point to when you inevitably have to land a soft "I told you so" or "I warned you."

People love to hate architects, and architects love to hate themselves. It can be a lonely job, and it can also be a thankless one. The architect is often the person whose blamed for when things go wrong, but like so many others in technology, their contributions are invisible when things go right.

1.4 Quality before description
4

Robert Pirsig spent a lot of Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance and later Lila exploring a deceptively simple question: what is quality? His conclusion wasn't that quality could be neatly defined, it was that it exists before our attempts to describe it. We recognise good workmanship before we can fully explain why it's good. Our descriptions, classifications and theories follow afterwards, they form as attempts to capture something that was already there.

That observation has surprising consequences for architecture.

Organisations exist before their diagrams. They have identity, purpose, relationships, responsibilities and patterns of behaviour long before somebody creates an organisational chart, designs a database or purchases an enterprise platform. Those things don't create the organisation; they're attempts to describe it. Like maps, they're useful because they simplify reality. They help us navigate, but they shouldn't be mistaken for the landscape itself.

This book repeatedly returns to that distinction. A data model isn't the organisation. A capability map isn't the organisation. An ontology isn't the organisation. They're all descriptions, each emphasising different aspects of the same underlying reality. Their value isn't in being complete, but in helping us preserve and communicate an understanding that would otherwise be forgotten.

Pirsig later developed these ideas further in Lila, describing quality as something that exists before subjects and objects are separated. Whether or not you accept his broader metaphysics, there's an architectural lesson worth keeping in there. Organisations are always richer than the models we build to explain them. Every architecture, ontology and process model is an interpretation, not the actual thing itself. The moment we forget that, we stop learning from reality and begin defending the model instead.

The chapters that follow treat architecture as an ongoing conversation with reality rather than a finished description of it. Models will change. Definitions will evolve. Systems will come and go. The discipline isn't in creating a perfect representation to last for all time, it's continually refining our understanding while remaining humble enough to recognise that the organisation is going to always exceed even our best attempts to describe it.

1.5 What do we mean by "Enterprise"?

The word enterprise has accumulated a shocking amount of baggage.

To some people it means "large organisation". To others it means "big business". For still others it evokes governance, committees and documentation. Even more use it to describe software that's expensive, conservative or designed for large customer bases. Somewhere along the way, "enterprise" stopped describing something specific and started acting as a shorthand for whatever the speaker wanted to legitimise or dismiss.

That in my opinion, makes the word utterly unhelpful.

It's not unusual to hear someone reject an approach because it "isn't enterprise", or recommend a product because it's "enterprise-grade", without ever explaining what either of those statements actually means. The word can become a substitute for actual reasoning. Rather than discussing scalability, reliability, governance, security or maintainability directly, these are bundled together into a label that everyone assumes they already understand.

Throughout this book I try to avoid using the word "enterprise" completely.

5

When I do use the term, I simply mean an organisation large enough that no single person can hold all of it in their head. One where understanding has to be shared across many people, many teams, many systems and, just as importantly, across time. That could be a multinational company, a research institute, a government department, a charity or a temporary coalition of organisations responding to a crisis. The legal structure is actually irrelevant.

The architectural problems i'll talk over in this book don'tt appear because an organisation has become large. They show up because understanding becomes more and more distributed. When that happens, language, memory, trust and shared meaning become architectural concerns in their own right.

Most importantly, "enterprise" should never be used to dismiss an idea.

Small organisations can develop better architectural practices than large ones. Open-source communities commonly solve coordination problems that large corporations struggle with. Techniques developed for embedded systems, aviation, manufacturing or scientific research often translate into enterprise environments with surprisingly little modification. Ultimately good ideas aren't "enterprise or non-enterprise". They're just ideas whose assumptions either match your organisation, or they don't.

In writing this book the way I have, arguments should stand or fall on their merits, not on whether somebody has attached the word "enterprise" to them.

1.6 What the role actually is, or should be

But as the architect in any organisation, your job isn't really to be the smartest guy in the room at all times. Architects aren't there to be a font of wisdom and knowledge, the purveyors of some infinite wisdom that only they have ascended to levels capable of tapping from the wider cosmos.

Being an architect is a stewardship operation. The architect is the custodian of the organisation's understanding of itself, and of the systems built to express that understanding. Their responsibility isn't just to design technology, but to ensure that the organisation's identity, language, and accumulated knowledge survive the departure of individuals, the replacement of systems, organisational upheaval, and the passage of time itself.

You're not here to produce diagrams or approve technology choices. You're here to preserve the organisation's ability to understand itself.

Two kinds of organisations

An assumption held a lot of architecture is that organisations become better by becoming more predictable. Processes become more consistent, operations become more efficient, variation is reduced and change itself becomes something to minimise. This is actually fairly sensible for a lot of organisations. 3.2 Different relationships with change

An organisation optimising for execution treats change as something to control. Every unnecessary variation introduces risk, cost and uncertainty. Good architecture therefore seeks to minimise differences, standardise approaches and make tomorrow look as much like today as possible.

An organisation optimising for discovery has a different relationship with change. New work appears unexpectedly, priorities shift and previously unrelated capabilities suddenly need to collaborate. Change is not a disruption to normal operation. It is the mechanism by which the organisation fulfils its purpose. Architecture cannot prevent it; it must make it survivable.

3.3 Different relationships with knowledge
11

Execution-oriented organisations benefit from knowing the answers. Once a process has been refined, documented and proven, there is considerable value in repeating it consistently. Knowledge becomes something to preserve and disseminate.

Discovery-oriented organisations spend much of their time discovering that yesterday's answers no longer fit today's questions. Knowledge is still valuable, but it is treated as something provisional rather than permanent. Architecture therefore has to preserve not only what the organisation knows, but its ability to revise what it knows without losing coherence.

3.4 Different relationships with risk

For one organisation, the greatest risk is unnecessary variation. A process executed differently in different places produces inconsistent outcomes, increased cost and reduced quality.

For the other, the greatest risk is becoming unable to adapt. A process that cannot evolve, a system that cannot represent new ideas or an organisation that cannot change direction becomes increasingly disconnected from the world it exists to serve. Stability eventually becomes a liability.

3.5 Different relationships with technology

Execution-oriented organisations often expect technology to encode the business. The software becomes the process because the process itself is expected to remain relatively stable.

Discovery-oriented organisations place a different demand on technology. Systems are expected to support work that has not yet been fully understood. The technology therefore needs to separate enduring meaning from temporary implementation. Otherwise every change in the organisation becomes a migration project.

3.6 Different relationships with architecture

These differences lead to different architectural priorities.

One architecture seeks to reduce variation, increase standardisation and optimise known work.

The other seeks to preserve meaning, reduce the cost of change and allow new capabilities to emerge without destabilising the organisation.

Both care about governance.

Both care about quality.

Both care about consistency.

What differs is where they believe stability should exist.

For an execution-oriented organisation, stability often resides in processes, systems and operating models.

For a discovery-oriented organisation, stability resides in meaning. Systems, processes and teams are expected to change, but the organisation's understanding of itself must remain coherent throughout that change.

12
3.7 Different relationships with time

Execution-oriented organisations often assume that success comes from extending today's understanding into the future. Improvements are incremental, and architectural decisions are expected to remain valid for long periods.

Discovery-oriented organisations assume that their understanding will change. Architecture is therefore designed not just to survive time, but to accommodate the organisation learning something fundamentally new about itself.

3.8 Different relationships with meaning

Perhaps the deepest difference is where meaning comes from.

In execution-oriented organisations, meaning often becomes embedded within processes and systems because those things change relatively slowly.

In discovery-oriented organisations, that coupling quickly becomes expensive. Meaning has to exist independently of the systems that happen to express it, otherwise every organisational change requires the organisation to renegotiate its own language.

3.9 Neither is better

It would be a mistake to conclude that one of these organisations is somehow more mature than the other. They simply optimise for different outcomes.

Many large organisations contain both. Manufacturing may optimise for execution while research optimises for discovery. Finance may value consistency while product development depends on experimentation. The architect's responsibility is not to force every part of the organisation into the same operating model, but to understand which assumptions are appropriate in which context.

Before adopting any framework, methodology or architectural practice—including those described in this book—it is worth asking a simple question.

What kind of problem is this part of the organisation trying to solve?

The answer matters far more than whether the approach happens to be described as "best practice."

6.1 Prepare to be hurt

There is now decades of research documenting the disappointing outcomes of large transformation programmes and IT projects. Exact figures vary depending on the study and the definitions used, but the overall pattern is remarkably consistent. Projects routinely exceed their budgets, miss delivery dates, deliver less value than expected or leave behind years of additional work that was never anticipated when the programme began. Yet many of these same programmes are later presented as successes, both internally and in public case studies. Success quietly becomes defined as the system eventually went live, rather than asking whether the organisation achieved the outcomes it originally set out to achieve, what compromises were made along the way or what long-term costs were accepted to get there.

This matters because architecture depends upon organisations being willing to describe reality honestly. If every migration is reported as a success regardless of what it actually cost, the organisation loses the ability to learn. The difficult trade-offs disappear from memory. Future programmes inherit only the official narrative, not the experience of the people who lived through the work. Lessons are replaced by marketing.

The consequence is subtle but profound. Architecture stops accumulating knowledge and starts accumulating stories. Those stories become the evidence used to justify future decisions, despite being incomplete accounts of what really happened. Over time the organisation becomes increasingly confident in practices that may never have achieved the results it believes they did.

This is one of the reasons architecture should concern itself with truth rather than optimism. An honest failure teaches an organisation more than a carefully managed success story. If architecture exists to help an organisation understand itself, then it must preserve not only what worked, but what was harder than expected, what assumptions proved false and what costs only became visible years after the project had officially ended.

Reflection
  • What work was left behind?
  • What capabilities became harder as a result?
  • Which assumptions proved incorrect?
  • What temporary compromises became permanent?
  • Would the people delivering the work choose the same approach again?

Organisations are remarkably bad at telling honest stories about themselves.

6.2 Institutional memory loss

Large transformation programmes often begin with ambitious objectives and optimistic business cases. As the work progresses, those objectives are quietly revised. Deadlines move. Budgets are extended. Capabilities are deferred into later phases. Scope is reduced, integrations are postponed, and temporary workarounds become permanent operating procedures. None of these changes is unusual. Large programmes are honestly difficult, organisations change even while they're being delivered and genuinely new problems emerge along the way.

What's more unusual is what happens afterwards.

20

By the time the programme concludes, the original measures of success have often been replaced by more forgiving ones. Delivering eighteen months late becomes evidence of resilience. A budget that doubled becomes an investment in long-term capability. Functionality that was removed from the scope is no longer counted as something that failed to arrive. If the new system eventually enters production, the transformation itself is quietly recorded as a success, even if the organisation had to redefine success several times to reach that conclusion.

This rarely happens through deliberate deception.

The people involved have invested years of their professional lives in the programme. Careers have been built around it. Budgets have been approved. Public commitments have been made. Organisations naturally prefer stories that justify those investments over stories that question them. Few people set out to rewrite history. Instead, history is gradually simplified until the difficult parts disappear and only the broad narrative remains: we transformed the organisation.

The problem is that future organisations only inherit the simplified version.

Case studies, conference talks, vendor presentations and executive summaries almost never dwell on the years of compromise that followed implementation. They rarely describe the capabilities that were quietly abandoned, the manual processes that survived because nobody had time to replace them, the integrations that never quite worked as intended or the additional teams that had to be created simply to support the new platform. Those details are not usually false; they are simply omitted. What remains is a story whose conclusion is accurate but whose journey has been edited beyond recognition.

This creates an unfortunate form of survivorship bias. Organisations studying these transformations believe they are learning from success, when in reality they are learning from stories that have already been filtered through politics, hindsight and institutional memory. They compare another organisation's polished narrative with their own messy day-to-day experience, concluding that they must simply be executing less effectively. They seldom realise that the other organisation almost certainly experienced the same uncertainty, the same missed milestones and the same compromises, but no longer talks about them.

There is a further irony.

Most people have lived through at least one transformation that was officially declared successful despite arriving late, exceeding its budget or delivering less than originally promised. They know, from direct experience, how much work remained after the project had closed and how many compromises were made to reach the finish line. Yet when they hear another organisation describe a similar programme, they instinctively accept the public account as though those same forces had somehow not been at work elsewhere.

The result is that organisations repeatedly compare their lived experience with everyone else's edited memory.

Architecture has an important role to play here. If its purpose is to help an organisation understand itself honestly, then it must preserve the full history of change, not merely its public summary. Decisions should record their assumptions. Programmes should record what changed, what was abandoned, what proved more difficult than expected and what new work was created as a consequence. Success should not mean that a system eventually entered production. It should mean that the organisation learned something worth preserving, whether the outcome was comfortable or not.

Otherwise the organisation slowly loses the ability to distinguish between a successful transformation and a successful story.

One of the more uncomfortable findings in project management research is not that large transformation programmes often struggle. It's that we've actually known this for decades.

The Standish Group's CHAOS reports have consistently shown that only around 30% of software and IT projects are delivered on time, on budget and with their intended scope. Around half are classified as "challenged", delivering late, over budget or with reduced functionality, while roughly one in five fail outright through cancellation or never being meaningfully used.

Research from the University of Oxford and McKinsey paints an equally sobering picture. Studying thousands of large IT programmes, Bent Flyvbjerg and Alexander Budzier found that while the average cost overrun was significant, the average disguised a much more dangerous reality. Around one in six projects became what they describe as Black Swans—projects with average cost overruns of around 200% and schedule overruns approaching 70%. Later work examining more than 5,000 IT projects concluded that these extreme overruns are not rare accidents but follow a heavy-tailed distribution, meaning catastrophic outcomes occur far more frequently than conventional planning assumes.

The pattern extends well beyond software. Flyvbjerg's broader research into more than 16,000 major projects found that only 8.5% achieved their cost and schedule targets, and only 0.5% met their cost, schedule and benefit objectives simultaneously. He describes this as the iron law of megaprojects: over budget, over time, over and over again.

Those numbers are insane.

The more interesting question is why they appear to have so little influence on how organisations make decisions.

One reason is that transformations never really remember themselves honestly.

As programmes progress, the definition of success mutates. Original deadlines become "aspirational". Budget increases become "additional investment". Features that disappear from scope are just not discussed anymore. Temporary manual workarounds become accepted ways of working. If the system eventually enters production, the transformation itself is often remembered as a success, even if the organisation reached that point only after redefining what success meant.

It's not even the fault of deliberate deception. It's the product of incentives. Careers become attached to programmes. Vendors need reference customers. Executives need to demonstrate progress. Programme teams naturally focus on what was achieved rather than what was quietly abandoned. The official story becomes shorter, cleaner and more optimistic than the lived experience of the people who delivered it.

Over time, those edited stories become the evidence used by the next generation of programmes.

22

That's why organisations repeatedly believe they are learning from success when they are often learning from successful retellings. Conference presentations, case studies and executive summaries almost always describe where the organisation arrived. They seldom describe the capabilities that were deferred indefinitely, the integrations that never worked properly, the years of stabilisation after the official go-live, or the additional teams created simply to compensate for shortcomings in the new platform.

There's an irony here that's easy to miss. Organisations are funny things. You can call just about anything an organisation these days. A group of people who get together to play a game, even a book club is an organisation. But when we talk about organisations in the context of business, we're talking about something a bit more specific. Most people have worked on at least one transformation that was officially declared a success despite missing deadlines, exceeding its budget or delivering less than originally promised. They know from experience how much untidy work remained after the launch. Yet when another organisation presents its own transformation at a conference or publishes it as a case study, those same people instinctively accept the polished narrative as though the same compromises had not been made there as well.

We compare our own lived experience with everyone else's edited memory.

Truth, in architecture, requires resisting that temptation. It requires preserving not only what eventually worked, but what changed, what failed, what was abandoned and what cost more than anyone expected. Without that, every generation inherits the same optimistic stories and wonders why the same failures keep repeating. The organisation does not lose because projects fail. It loses because it forgets how they failed, and replaces that understanding with a story that's easier to tell.

6.4 Success is almost impossible to measure

Very few transformation programmes have objective success criteria that survive until the end.

Instead they drift.

Originally:

Three years later:

The programme has technically succeeded because the definition of success changed.

That's an important architectural observation.

Success is difficult to measure because organisations rarely agree on what it means before the work begins, and even when they do, the definition seldom survives contact with delivery. A programme may begin with promises to reduce cost, improve service, simplify the estate and retire old systems. Years later, those ambitions have been reduced to a narrower claim: the platform went live, users were migrated, or the programme delivered within its revised scope. The organisation has not necessarily falsified the result. It has allowed the measure of success to move until it fits what was eventually achieved.

23

Even a genuine improvement is hard to attribute. Performance may have recovered because demand fell, experienced staff returned, an unrelated process changed or teams quietly worked around the new system. The organisation usually compares the outcome with the troubled condition that justified intervention, not with the realistic alternatives that might have achieved the same result more cheaply or safely. Without that counterfactual, it can show that something improved after the programme, but not that the programme caused the improvement or represented the best available choice.

The final difficulty is timing. Programmes close before many of their benefits or costs can be observed. Benefits remain forecasts, while support burdens, retained legacy systems, manual reconciliation, supplier dependency and staff exhaustion are dispersed into ordinary operations. By the time the full outcome becomes visible, the programme team has gone and the original success criteria have largely been forgotten. What organisations call success is therefore often not a measured condition, but a negotiated story told at the point when they need the programme to end.

Survivorship bias is the tendency to draw conclusions from the examples that remain visible while ignoring those that disappeared, failed or were never publicised. The organisations, programmes and products we hear about are rarely a representative sample. They are the ones that survived long enough, performed well enough, or had sufficient incentive to tell their story. The failures are less likely to appear at conferences, in consultancy reports or as vendor case studies, so the evidence available to us is already filtered before we begin evaluating it.

Imagine an organisation considering a large cloud migration. What it does not see are the organisations that abandoned similar migrations, spent heavily rebuilding capabilities they had lost, remained permanently dependent on consultants, or quietly retained most of their original estate alongside the new platform. The danger is that organisations mistake visibility for probability. A polished case study proves that one organisation was willing and able to present that version of events; it does not reveal how often the same approach failed elsewhere, how unusual the successful organisation was, or how much difficulty was omitted from its account. Good architecture therefore studies the full distribution of outcomes wherever possible, including failed programmes, abandoned products, expensive reversals and organisations that chose not to proceed. Without the missing cases, success stories tell us much less than they appear to.

Hindsight bias is the tendency to look back on an outcome and treat it as more predictable, more coherent and more inevitable than it really was. Once the result is known, the uncertainty surrounding the original decision fades from view. Warnings seem obvious, failed alternatives are forgotten and chance events are absorbed into a story in which the outcome appears to have followed naturally from the choices that were made. The organisation remembers a clear path where, at the time, there was only incomplete information, disagreement and risk.

Imagine a major systems programme that eventually goes live after two years of delay, several budget increases and a long period of manual reconciliation. In the official retrospective, leadership may describe the programme as a necessary modernisation that succeeded because the organisation remained committed to its vision. The early warnings, abandoned designs, emergency interventions and compromises are reduced to ordinary delivery difficulties. Because the system is now live, the final outcome is used to validate the original decision, even though the route taken may bear little resemblance to the plan that was approved.

The danger is that hindsight bias turns survival into evidence of wisdom. A decision that eventually produced an acceptable outcome is remembered as a good decision, while a similar decision that failed is remembered as obviously flawed. In both cases, the organisation learns from the ending rather than from the quality of the reasoning available at the time. Good architecture resists this by preserving assumptions, alternatives, uncertainties and dissent as they existed when the decision was made.

Escalation of commitment is the tendency to continue investing in a decision after evidence suggests it is failing, particularly when significant money, time, reputation or political capital has already been attached to it. Instead of treating the new evidence as a reason to reconsider, organisations reinterpret it as a reason to commit more strongly. Delays justify additional funding, rising costs justify protecting the investment already made, and early doubts are dismissed because changing direction would require admitting that the original decision may have been wrong.

Imagine a large technology programme that has missed several milestones and still cannot demonstrate that its core assumptions are valid. By this point, the organisation has signed contracts, recruited a programme team and publicly committed to the outcome. Cancelling it would make the expenditure visible as a loss and expose senior sponsors to scrutiny. Continuing feels safer, even though it may cost much more. The programme receives another budget increase, its scope is reduced, success measures are softened and previously essential benefits are deferred into later phases. Eventually, the system goes live in a diminished form and the fact of delivery is used to justify the years of continued investment.

The danger is that past expenditure begins to influence decisions about future value. Money and effort already spent cannot be recovered, but they create a powerful pressure to avoid stopping. The organisation no longer asks whether the programme remains the best use of its remaining resources; it asks how much more must be spent to prevent the previous investment from appearing wasted. Good architecture resists this by establishing explicit stopping conditions, reviewing assumptions independently and making it legitimate to change direction before failure becomes too politically expensive to acknowledge.

6.8 Goodharts here again

"go-live"

26

Goodhart’s Law describes what happens when a measure stops being used to observe performance and starts being used to control it:

When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

A measure is usually introduced as a proxy for something more difficult to observe directly. Delivery dates stand in for progress. Ticket closure rates stand in for responsiveness. System adoption stands in for usefulness. These measures can be informative while people are free to pursue the underlying objective. Once rewards, reputation or funding depend on the number itself, behaviour changes. People begin optimising what is measured, even when doing so weakens the thing the measure was intended to represent.

Imagine a transformation programme judged primarily on the number of users migrated to a new platform. The target creates pressure to move accounts quickly, so users are counted as migrated once access has been created or basic data has been transferred. Whether they can complete their work, whether the data is reliable, and whether they continue using parallel spreadsheets receive less attention because those things do not affect the headline figure. The programme reports 95 per cent adoption while much of the organisation still depends on the old processes beneath it.

The measure has not necessarily been falsified. It has been satisfied precisely as defined. The problem is that the definition captured only a convenient approximation of success. Once it became the target, the organisation reorganised its effort around improving the approximation rather than producing the outcome it originally cared about.

Goodhart’s Law becomes particularly dangerous when the measure is attached to status or survival. A team measured by incident closure time learns to close incidents before underlying causes are resolved. A call centre measured by call duration learns to end conversations quickly. A project measured by delivery dates reduces scope, defers difficult work and labels unfinished capabilities as later phases. In each case, the reported performance improves while the real condition may remain unchanged or become worse.

The architectural lesson is not to abandon measurement. It is to avoid confusing indicators with reality. Measures should be interpreted alongside qualitative evidence, operational consequences and countermeasures that reveal how the number might be gamed. They should also be revised when behaviour begins adapting around them. A measure that never changes despite changing circumstances is likely to become a ritual rather than a source of truth.

Good architecture therefore asks two questions whenever a metric is proposed:

What are we actually trying to improve?

and

How could people improve this number without improving that thing?

The second question is often the more important one.

Benefits take time to emerge, operating costs become clearer through use, and compromises that appeared temporary during delivery settle into the permanent structure of the organisation. Temporary work becomes permanent work
28

Large programmes routinely defer work in order to protect a delivery date. Data cleansing is reduced to the minimum needed for migration. Difficult integrations are moved into a later phase. Decommissioning is separated from implementation. Operational improvements are postponed until the new system has had time to settle.

Some deferral is unavoidable. The problem is that deferred work loses the political and financial protection enjoyed by the programme that created it. Once the launch has occurred, senior attention moves elsewhere, specialist contractors leave and the remaining work must compete with ordinary operational demand. “Phase two” gradually becomes a collection of backlog items owned by teams that were never given the capacity to complete it.

Temporary workarounds then become part of the operating model. A spreadsheet introduced to validate migration data becomes the monthly reconciliation process. An emergency interface becomes a production dependency. A manual approval introduced during transition remains because nobody is confident enough to remove it. The organisation adapts around the unfinished transformation until the adaptation itself becomes difficult to change.

Research on legacy-system replacement shows that transformation does not simply eliminate technical debt. The replacement process can generate new sociotechnical debt as organisations preserve parts of the old system, configure the new system around unresolved constraints and create additional arrangements to bridge the two.

The old world rarely disappears cleanly

Transformation business cases often count benefits that depend on removing something: an old platform, a manual process, a supplier contract, a reporting team or a duplicated source of data. Delivery plans tend to concentrate more heavily on introducing the replacement.

Addition and removal are not symmetrical.

A new system can be launched on a chosen date. An old system can be retired only when every remaining dependency has been found, understood and replaced. A rarely used regulatory report, an annual process, an undocumented data feed or a team that retained its old workflow may keep the system alive long after most users have moved. Continuing to operate it appears cheaper than resolving the final dependencies, particularly once the original programme has closed.

The result is not replacement but layering. The organisation acquires the cost of the new world without fully releasing the cost of the old one. The National Audit Office has repeatedly observed that transforming legacy services is expensive and complex, but retaining them also creates continuing service, people and future-change costs that organisations often fail to record completely.

This is one reason transformations can increase complexity while reporting that they have reduced it. The target architecture may be simpler on paper, but the organisation must operate the target architecture, the remaining legacy estate and the machinery connecting them.

Benefits and costs travel on different timelines

Programme costs are usually immediate and visible. Benefits are often delayed and conditional.

29

The organisation pays for software, consultants, implementation and migration during delivery. The promised benefit may depend on later behavioural change, process redesign, data improvement, decommissioning or the organisation using the new capability in ways that have not yet been established. A functioning technical system is therefore only one of the conditions needed for the benefit to appear.

Costs have their own delayed forms. Licence increases arrive at renewal. Performance limitations become apparent as usage grows. Customisations become expensive when the vendor releases a new version. Knowledge disappears as the implementation team moves on. The organisation may not understand the full operating model until several annual cycles have passed.

This creates a convenient asymmetry. The programme closes while its expected benefits remain forecasts, but before many of its enduring costs are visible. Later benefits are attributed to the transformation, while later costs are treated as ordinary operational problems.

Without deliberate post-implementation evaluation, the original business case can remain notionally successful indefinitely. The evidence needed to challenge it arrives only after the governance structure responsible for answering the challenge has disappeared.

The long tail is carried by people

The remaining work does not vanish when the programme closes. It is transferred to the people operating the result.

They answer questions the programme did not resolve, maintain transitional interfaces, correct migrated data and explain why the documented process does not quite work. Experienced staff become permanent interpreters between the old organisation and the new system. New employees learn the workaround without knowing why it exists, and eventually it becomes indistinguishable from the intended design.

This is one of the least visible forms of organisational accretion. The architecture appears complete because its diagrams contain the new platform. The lived architecture includes the platform, the reconciliation spreadsheet, the retained database, the support mailbox, the undocumented extract and the person who knows which figures cannot be trusted before the fifth working day.

The programme records the delivery effort. The organisation inherits the maintenance effort.

Closure is an architecture decision

A programme should not be judged complete merely because it has introduced its replacement. It should be judged by whether the organisation can sustain the result without carrying an indefinite transitional burden.

That requires explicit ownership of the long tail. Before closure, the organisation should know which old systems and processes remain, why they remain, what it costs to retain them and who is accountable for removing them. Deferred work should have named owners, funded capacity and dates at which its continued deferral will be reconsidered. Benefits should remain attached to accountable operational leaders after the programme structure disappears, as established benefits-management guidance recommends.

A useful closure review should therefore ask:

What work has the programme created that will continue after the programme ends?

30

Which systems, contracts, processes and controls have actually disappeared?

What was described as temporary, and what prevents it from becoming permanent?

Who owns benefits that have not yet been realised?

Where have implementation costs been transferred into ordinary operational work?

When will the organisation return to determine whether the transformation was genuinely worthwhile?

The long tail is not an unfortunate period of tidying up after the real work. It is where the truth of the transformation becomes visible. Go-live demonstrates that the organisation succeeded in introducing something. Only the years that follow reveal whether it succeeded in changing anything.

6.10 Missing counterfactuals

Nobody asks

A transformation may have delivered.

Architecture should always think in counterfactuals.

A counterfactual is the outcome that might have occurred had the organisation chosen differently. Transformation programmes are usually judged by comparing the final state with the problems that existed before them. They're much less often compared with the plausible alternatives available at the time: improving the existing system, changing the process without replacing the technology, building a smaller internal capability, or doing nothing until the problem was better understood.

The absence is important because delivery alone doesn't prove that the chosen intervention was worthwhile. A programme can eventually produce an improvement, but that improvement might have been achieved more quickly, cheaply and safely through another route. Without a counterfactual, the organisation can say that the programme delivered something, but not that it was the best use of its resources.

Imagine an organisation spending £20 million replacing a reporting platform because reports are slow, definitions are inconsistent and analysts rely on manual workarounds. Four years later, the new platform is live and reporting has improved. The programme is declared successful. What is never examined is whether the organisation might have achieved most of the same benefit by investing £3 million in shared definitions, data quality, better pipelines and a stronger internal engineering team while retaining the existing platform for another five years.

31

Because that alternative was never attempted, the larger programme appears justified by the fact that conditions improved after it. The organisation compares the result with the troubled past rather than with the best realistic alternative. It attributes every improvement to the transformation and ignores the opportunity cost of the money, time and attention consumed by it.

Good architecture makes counterfactuals explicit before major commitments are made. It asks not just whether a proposal can work, but what else could produce the same outcome, what would happen without intervention, and which option leaves the organisation more capable afterwards. Without those comparisons, success means only that something happened—not that the right thing happened.

Regression to the mean is the tendency for unusually extreme results to be followed by results closer to the ordinary average. Performance varies for many reasons: chance, timing, temporary conditions, measurement error and circumstances that may not persist. When an organisation intervenes at the moment things are unusually bad, some improvement is likely even when the intervention has little effect. The organisation may then attribute that natural recovery to whatever action it happened to take.

Imagine a department whose service performance falls sharply during one quarter. Several experienced staff are absent, demand rises unexpectedly and a supplier suffers a serious outage. Leadership responds by commissioning a transformation programme, appointing new advisers and introducing additional governance. Six months later, service levels have improved. The programme is credited with turning the department around, even though staff have returned, demand has settled and the supplier outage has ended. Performance might have recovered substantially without the transformation.

The same effect can make leadership changes, new products and management methods appear more powerful than they are. Interventions are rarely introduced when performance is ordinary; they are introduced after a crisis, a failed audit or an especially poor set of results. Because the starting point is unusually low, almost any subsequent period is likely to look better. The improvement is real, but its cause remains uncertain.

Regression to the mean can also work in the opposite direction. An organisation may adopt a new method after an unusually successful year, then blame the method when performance later returns to normal. In both cases, the mistake is treating movement away from an exceptional result as evidence that the latest intervention caused the change.

32

Good architecture therefore avoids judging an intervention solely by comparing the period immediately before it with the period immediately afterwards. It examines longer-term trends, comparable teams, external conditions and what would plausibly have happened without the intervention. Otherwise, organisations repeatedly confuse timing with effectiveness and reward the people who arrived immediately before conditions returned to normal.

Human Costs

Architecture changes the conditions under which people work. A new system alters what they must remember, where they obtain information and how much control they have over completing a task. A new operating model changes responsibilities and relationships. A transformation programme may do all of these things at once, often while asking people to maintain the old organisation until the new one is ready.

These effects are usually discussed in terms of adoption, productivity or resistance to change. They should also be understood as matters of health and safety. The World Health Organization identifies excessive workload, low control, unclear roles, authoritarian supervision, job insecurity and conflicting work and home demands as risks to mental health at work. In the United Kingdom, the Health and Safety Executive groups the main sources of work-related stress under six headings: demands, control, support, relationships, role and the management of change. Architecture can influence every one of them.

7.1 Change is not pyschologically neutral

Organisational change is not inherently harmful. It may remove frustrating work, improve services and give people greater control over their jobs. The risk arises from how change is designed and carried out, particularly when it increases demands while reducing control, leaves roles uncertain or asks people to work for long periods between an old organisation and an unfinished replacement.

34

A 2024 study used three representative German workforce surveys covering more than 50,000 observations between 2006 and 2018. Employees who had experienced at least one form of organisational change were 1.82 times as likely to report poor mental health indicators after adjustment for age, gender, occupation and survey year. In the 2012 sample, the predicted probability of poor mental health was 24.4% among employees experiencing change, compared with 14.4% among those who were not. The study was observational, so it does not prove that every change caused the difference, but it provides strong evidence that organisational change and employee health cannot safely be treated as unrelated concerns.

The practical implication is not that organisations should avoid change. It is that change should be treated as an intervention in the working environment. Its effects on workload, control, role clarity, relationships and support should be assessed with the same seriousness as its effects on cost and delivery.

7.2 How transformations increase demands

A transformation rarely replaces one way of working with another in a single movement. For a period, the organisation has to operate both. People enter information into the new system while correcting records in the old one. They learn a new process while continuing to meet the commitments made under the previous process. When the two representations disagree, somebody has to investigate the difference and decide which version should be trusted.

This transitional work is often absent from the programme plan because no single team owns it. It appears instead as small additions to ordinary jobs. A few more checks are introduced, meetings become longer and experienced staff spend more time explaining how the old organisation worked. Each addition appears manageable, but together they can significantly increase the demands placed on the people expected to keep the organisation operating.

The people most affected are often the same people whose knowledge the transformation depends upon. They are asked to deliver their existing work, explain it to the programme, test its replacement and then help everyone else adopt it. Treating this as incidental participation hides a substantial transfer of effort from the programme into the existing workforce.

7.3 Control matters as much as workload

High workload is not the only source of strain. The amount of control people retain over their work also matters.

Control does not mean allowing everyone to choose their own objectives or ignore organisational constraints. It means having enough influence over the methods, sequencing and immediate decisions involved in doing the work well. A demanding job can remain manageable when people have authority to adjust priorities and respond to local conditions. The same demands become harder to sustain when deadlines, tools and procedures are imposed while the people responsible for the outcome are prevented from changing any of them.

Transformation programmes often reduce control at the same time as they increase demand. Decisions move into programme governance, while responsibility for dealing with their consequences remains with operational teams. People may be accountable for an outcome without having authority over the design causing the problem. HSE treats demands and control as separate but interacting psychosocial risk factors and recommends addressing organisational causes rather than returning individuals to the same harmful conditions after they have become unwell.

35

This is why consultation must involve more than informing people that a decision has been made. The people doing the work need a credible route to alter decisions when evidence from practice shows that the design is incomplete.

7.4 Uncertainty becomes part of the job

Poorly managed change also creates uncertainty about matters that were previously settled. People may not know whether their role will continue, which team will own a capability or whether the system they are learning will still exist in a year. During repeated transformations, that uncertainty can cease to be temporary and become an ordinary feature of employment.

Uncertainty consumes attention because people must continually reassess their position. They hold back from improving the current system because it may be replaced, but cannot rely on the replacement because its design is still moving. Decisions that would normally be straightforward acquire another question: whether the surrounding organisation will remain stable long enough for the decision to matter.

Leadership may experience this as caution or resistance. From the perspective of the people affected, it may be a reasonable response to an environment in which commitments are repeatedly revised and information arrives late. Job insecurity, unclear roles and poorly communicated organisational change are recognised psychosocial risks, not merely obstacles to programme adoption.

7.5 Systems can damage professional judgement

People usually want to do work they consider competent and worthwhile. Architecture can support that judgement, or repeatedly place people in situations where doing the right thing requires working against the system.

A grants officer may know that two applicants should not be treated as equivalent, while the system provides no way to record the distinction. An analyst may know that a headline measure is misleading, but still be required to publish it because the programme reports against it. An engineer may be told to implement a design whose risks are understood but no longer open for discussion. Over time, the difficulty is no longer simply that the tools are frustrating. People are required to choose between complying with the system and exercising the professional judgement for which they were employed.

Research uses terms such as moral distress and moral injury for more serious forms of this conflict, particularly in healthcare and other professions with strong ethical duties. These terms should not be applied casually to every disagreement at work. The relevant lesson for architecture is narrower: sustained organisational constraints that prevent people from acting according to accepted professional standards can cause genuine psychological distress. Modern reviews in health and social care identify institutional constraints, workload and an inability to act according to best practice as significant contributors.

Architecture should not make professional judgement unnecessary. It should provide enough structure for that judgement to be exercised consistently and enough feedback for the structure to change when it no longer fits the work.

7.6 Silence is not evidence of acceptance

The absence of complaints does not demonstrate that a transformation is harmless. People learn quickly whether raising concerns leads to investigation, delay, reputational damage or exclusion from future decisions. When the cost of speaking becomes clear, the organisation may receive less disagreement while the underlying problems continue.

Contemporary research distinguishes between interpersonal psychological safety within teams and the wider psychosocial safety climate created by management priorities, policies and practices. A large 2024 systematic review covering 990 publications found that both concepts have become central to understanding employee wellbeing and organisational resilience. The wider point is that psychological safety cannot be created solely by asking managers to invite feedback. People watch what happens after difficult information is provided.

An architecture function should therefore treat dissent as a source of evidence. When people repeatedly report that a workflow is unsafe, a definition is misleading or a migration is creating unmanageable work, the first question should be what the architecture has failed to represent. Labelling the response as resistance may remove the messenger while preserving the cause.

7.7 The cost travels beyond the organisation

Work does not remain neatly contained within working hours. Prolonged uncertainty, overload and loss of control affect sleep, attention and emotional availability. Those effects alter how people participate in family and social life, even when they make deliberate efforts not to carry work home.

Psychological research describes this through spillover, where strain in one area of a person's life affects another, and crossover, where that strain affects other people. A 2024 systematic review of 57 studies found that direct crossover was the most commonly observed mechanism and that effects were not limited to couples; they also appeared in relationships between parents and children, managers and staff, and employees and customers.

This does not mean that an organisation is responsible for every difficulty experienced outside work. It means that the costs created by its decisions do not stop at the boundary of its payroll. A programme may record consultancy fees, licences and redundancy payments while leaving exhaustion, disrupted family time and lost confidence entirely outside its accounting. Those costs remain real even when the organisation has no mechanism for measuring them.

7.8 Why the cost remains hidden

Psychological harm rarely appears as a single programme expense. It is distributed across absence, turnover, reduced concentration, slower decisions and the quiet withdrawal of discretionary effort. Some people leave. Others remain but stop volunteering information or taking risks. The organisation sees separate operational symptoms rather than a common effect of the way work has been designed.

The scale of the wider problem is substantial. HSE estimates that stress, depression or anxiety accounted for 22.1 million working days lost in Great Britain during 2024/25. Each affected worker lost an average of 22.9 days. These figures describe work-related ill health across the economy rather than transformation programmes specifically, but they show why psychological conditions at work cannot be dismissed as an intangible or marginal cost.

37

HSE recommends combining sickness absence with turnover, complaints, exit interviews, occupational health information and direct feedback from staff. It also recommends examining when changes occur: whether absence rises after periods of intense demand, whether one part of the organisation loses more people than another, and whether operational complaints coincide with changes in working methods. A survey on its own provides an indicator, not a diagnosis.

The same approach should be applied to architecture. A programme should not wait for a mental-health absence figure before concluding that its design is creating strain. Rework, growing manual reconciliation, unresolved role disputes and a decline in people volunteering problems are earlier signals.

7.9 Wellbeing support does not replace good work design

Individual support has value. Counselling, reasonable adjustments and access to clinical care can help people experiencing distress. They should not, however, be used as substitutes for removing avoidable hazards from the work itself.

A 2024 study compared 46,336 workers across 233 British organisations, examining participation in common individual-level interventions such as resilience training, mindfulness and wellbeing applications. Across multiple subjective wellbeing measures, participants appeared no better off than non-participants. The research was cross-sectional and therefore cannot establish that such interventions never help an individual, but its findings question whether they provide an adequate response to harmful job demands.

WHO's guidance places organisational intervention first: assess the risks created by working conditions, then mitigate, modify or remove them. Support for individuals remains important, but teaching people to cope with an avoidable source of harm is not equivalent to correcting it.

7.10 Assess the human effects of change

The human effects of architecture should be reviewed before a major change is approved, monitored while it is being introduced and examined after the programme has formally closed. The purpose is not to prevent difficult work or eliminate every period of uncertainty. It is to identify where the design is imposing costs that are avoidable, unowned or absent from the business case.

7.11 Stewardship includes working conditions

Architecture is not solely responsible for employee mental health. Health is shaped by many personal, social and economic factors, and no architecture can remove every difficult experience from work.

Architecture is responsible for the conditions it knowingly creates.

When a design adds unnecessary work, removes control, obscures responsibility or prevents people from exercising sound professional judgement, those are not merely usability defects. They change the daily experience of work. When a transformation keeps those conditions in place for years, the effects may become serious and may extend beyond the people directly assigned to the programme.

Stewardship therefore includes more than preserving systems and organisational knowledge. It includes taking reasonable care over the human environment produced by architectural decisions. The work is not complete when the system goes live. It is complete only when the organisation can operate it without transferring an unacknowledged part of its cost onto the people expected to make it work.

Harder Truths

8.1 The organisation knows what it is

Most organisations assume that somewhere—perhaps in strategy, leadership or enterprise architecture—there is a coherent understanding of the organisation as a whole.

Usually there is not.

Different parts of the organisation carry different, partially overlapping accounts of its purpose, structure, customers, capabilities and obligations. The organisation functions not because these accounts agree, but because people continually reconcile them through conversation, judgement and workaround.

This is uncomfortable because it means the organisation may be less a designed object than a negotiated settlement.

8.2 Leadership sees the whole

Hierarchy creates the impression that information accumulates as it moves upward. Seniority is therefore treated as a wider field of vision.

In practice, information is compressed as it travels. Detail is removed, uncertainty is simplified and disagreement is resolved before it reaches leadership. Senior leaders may possess broader context, but they often have less direct contact with the conditions in which the work is performed.

The uncomfortable truth is that greater authority does not necessarily produce greater understanding.

8
40
8.3 Strategy describes reality

A strategy is often treated as an authoritative description of what the organisation is doing and where it is going.

Frequently it is an argument about what leadership would like the organisation to become. Existing work that does not fit the narrative is omitted, capabilities are described as absent because they are not visible centrally, and uncertainty is converted into confident direction.

The strategy may still be useful. It simply should not be confused with an objective account of the organisation.

The organisation acts as one thing

We speak of organisations as though they possess a single mind:

The organisation decided.

The organisation wants.

The organisation believes.

In reality, decisions emerge from competing interests, partial information, institutional habits and unequal authority. Different groups may be pursuing incompatible outcomes while each believes it is acting on behalf of the whole.

This assumption is comfortable because it hides politics beneath the language of alignment.

8.4 Decisions are made for the reasons recorded

Architecture decisions, programme papers and business cases usually present a rational chain from evidence to conclusion.

The actual decision may also have been shaped by timing, personal credibility, budget cycles, fear, executive preference, vendor relationships or the need to appear decisive. The written rationale is often not false, but it is cleaner than the process that produced it.

The uncomfortable part is that organisational records frequently preserve the justification for a decision rather than its cause.

8.5 Governance reduces risk

Governance is assumed to make decisions safer because more people review them and more evidence is recorded.

Sometimes it does. Sometimes it diffuses accountability, delays corrective action and rewards decisions that are easy to defend rather than decisions that are likely to work. A heavily governed programme may fail with immaculate paperwork.

The difficult truth is that governance can become a way of demonstrating control without exercising it.

41
8.6 Standardisation creates simplicity

Standardisation is often presented as the natural cure for organisational complexity.

It can remove unnecessary variation, but it can also erase meaningful differences and force exceptions into less visible forms. The process appears standard while people create spreadsheets, side systems and informal agreements to restore the distinctions the standard removed.

The organisation then becomes simple in its official model and more complicated in practice.

8.7 Technology is the difficult part

Transformation programmes often assume that the technical implementation is the central challenge and that organisational adoption will follow once the platform is delivered.

Usually the harder questions concern authority, meaning, incentives, ownership and which version of reality will prevail. Technology makes those disagreements concrete, but it does not create them.

This is uncomfortable because technical problems can be delegated. Political and epistemic problems cannot.

8.8 Capability can be purchased

Organisations often behave as though capability is something that can be acquired through a product, supplier or consultancy engagement.

Products provide functions. Consultants provide expertise and temporary capacity. Capability exists only when the organisation can understand, operate, challenge and evolve what has been introduced without permanent dependence on the people who introduced it.

The uncomfortable truth is that an organisation can spend heavily acquiring technology while becoming less capable.

8.9 Data is neutral

Data is commonly treated as a factual substrate beneath organisational interpretation.

Yet every dataset reflects choices about what was considered worth recording, which categories were available, who had authority to define them and which exceptions were discarded. Data can be accurate while still presenting a partial or politically convenient view of reality.

The question is therefore not merely whether the data is correct, but whose world it describes.

8.10 Measurement reveals performance

Metrics are assumed to make performance visible.

42

They also determine what receives attention. What can be counted becomes governable; what cannot be counted becomes anecdotal. Once consequences are attached to a measure, people adapt their behaviour around it and the metric begins reporting the adaptation rather than the underlying condition.

The uncomfortable truth is that measurement does not simply observe the organisation. It reshapes it.

8.11 More visibility creates more control

Leaders often ask for dashboards, reporting and central oversight because visibility appears to increase control.

Visibility may reveal symptoms without exposing causes. It may also encourage local teams to manage the appearance of performance because they know what the centre can see. The organisation becomes more observable while becoming less candid.

Seeing more indicators does not necessarily mean understanding more of the system.

8.12 Silence means agreement

When objections stop, organisations often conclude that alignment has been achieved.

Silence may instead mean that people have learned disagreement is futile, costly or dangerous. They continue doing what is required while withholding knowledge that might complicate the official narrative.

This is one of the most dangerous assumptions because the organisation can lose access to reality while believing resistance has been overcome.

8.13 Resistance is irrational

People who oppose a transformation are often described as attached to the past, fearful of change or protective of their territory.

Sometimes they are. They may also understand dependencies, consequences and historical failures that the programme has not discovered. What appears as resistance may be compressed operational knowledge trying to enter a decision process that has no room for it.

The uncomfortable truth is that resistance is often evidence before it becomes obstruction.

8.14 People are interchangeable

Organisational charts, workforce plans and sourcing models often treat people as units of capacity attached to roles.

But much organisational capability exists as tacit knowledge: who to ask, which definitions are misleading, where the systems disagree, which process matters only once a year and which promise was made long ago to somebody who still depends on it.

When people leave, the organisation does not merely lose labour. It loses portions of its model of itself.

43
8.15 External advice is more objective

Consultants and vendors are often treated as independent because they stand outside the organisation.

They may be less attached to internal history, but they are not free of incentives, assumptions or preferred solution shapes. Distance can produce perspective; it can also remove context. External confidence is easily mistaken for objectivity because the uncertainty has already been edited out of the presentation.

The uncomfortable question is not whether advisers are biased, but whether their biases are more visible than the organisation’s own.

8.16 Procurement transfers risk

A contract can allocate responsibility, specify remedies and make failure commercially expensive for a supplier.

It cannot transfer the operational consequences of failure. The organisation still has to serve its users, meet its obligations and repair the damage. Contractual risk may have moved while existential risk remains where it always was.

Buying from a large supplier often makes a decision easier to defend, not necessarily safer.

8.17 Transformation has an end state

Programmes are built around the idea that the organisation will move from a current state to a target state and then stabilise.

In reality, the target begins ageing before it is reached. People adapt around it, external conditions change and compromises introduced during delivery become the starting point for the next intervention.

The uncomfortable truth is that transformation is not a journey to stability. It is one episode in a continuous process of adaptation.

8.18 Success proves the decision was right

Once something survives, delivers or produces visible benefit, the original decision is often vindicated retrospectively.

But an acceptable outcome may have been produced despite the decision rather than because of it. People may have compensated for a poor design, costs may have been displaced elsewhere, or a cheaper alternative may have produced the same result.

Outcomes matter, but they do not remove the need to examine the quality of the reasoning that preceded them.

8.19 The organisation wants to know the truth
44

Perhaps the hardest assumption is that organisations naturally prefer an accurate account of themselves.

Truth can threaten budgets, careers, strategies, supplier relationships and the legitimacy of past decisions. Organisations often want enough truth to act, but not always enough to destabilise the arrangements through which authority is exercised.

That does not make organisations uniquely dishonest. It makes truth costly.

The most dangerous organisational assumptions are not those nobody has questioned. They are those the organisation cannot afford to question without also reconsidering who has authority, who benefits, and which parts of its own story remain intact.

Typical approaches to remediation

9.1 Outsourcing, Off-shoring & Vendorization

Outsourcing and product purchasing can provide genuine expertise, scale and commodity capability. The larger phenomenon is what happens when external provision substitutes for the organisation’s capacity to understand what it is buying.

The organisation gradually loses the skills needed to specify the work, evaluate supplier claims, judge architectural consequences and take control when conditions change. Supplier dependency then becomes self-reinforcing: the less internal knowledge remains, the riskier it appears to use anyone except the incumbent supplier.

Recent NAO work describes legacy systems becoming “black boxes” whose original suppliers retain the knowledge required to support them. It also identifies weak internal “intelligent client” capability as a major risk in technology procurement. Research on IT outsourcing during systemic disruption similarly found that strong internal capability remained an important means through which client organisations protected performance, even where substantial work had been externalised.

The underlying paradox is:

The organisation outsources technology to reduce its operational burden, then becomes permanently responsible for managing a dependency it no longer understands.

This belongs directly beside your material on consultancy incentives. Capability hollowing is what happens when repeated external intervention removes the organisation’s ability to choose whether it needs the next intervention.

Give an inch, take a mile

10.1 AI productivity

This is an emerging phenomenon, but it may become one of the defining organisational errors of the next decade: confusing faster production of technical artefacts with faster delivery of reliable capability.

Evidence is currently mixed and strongly dependent on task, experience and context. Some controlled and field studies have found substantial gains, especially for bounded tasks and less-experienced developers. Others have found much weaker effects in mature, complex codebases.

A particularly revealing 2025 randomised study involved 16 experienced open-source developers completing 246 real tasks in projects they already knew. The developers expected AI to reduce completion time by 24% and afterwards believed it had made them about 20% faster. In the experiment, access to the AI tools increased completion time by 19%. The study was narrow and should not be generalised to all software work, but the mismatch between perceived and measured productivity is striking.

This is not an argument against AI. It exposes a larger organisational phenomenon:

Tools that make work feel faster can increase output at one stage while moving verification, correction and comprehension costs downstream.

Organisations may measure code produced, prompts answered or local task completion while missing the effect on review queues, maintainability, security and the number of people who genuinely understand the resulting system.

10.2 The productivity measurement trap
10
47

Software delivery is unusually difficult to measure because the visible artefacts—commits, tickets, story points, lines of code, deployments—are weak proxies for value.

When these proxies become management targets, Goodhart’s Law takes hold. Teams split work into more tickets, produce more deployments, reduce reported incident duration or close requests before the underlying problem has been resolved. The numbers improve precisely because the organisation has taught people how the numbers are judged.

DORA’s research has helped shift measurement towards a balance of throughput and stability, including lead time, deployment frequency, failed-deployment recovery and change-failure measures. Even these are intended to describe the performance of a delivery system, not rank individual developers or become isolated objectives.

The larger phenomenon is that technology leadership repeatedly tries to make uncertain intellectual work legible to management. The resulting measurements then alter the work until they no longer describe it accurately.

10.3 Failing to remove

Large organisations are much better at introducing systems than removing them.

A new platform receives a business case, implementation team, executive sponsor and launch date. Decommissioning depends on locating every old dependency, reproducing obscure reports, migrating historical data and persuading remaining users to stop relying on established behaviour. Once the new system is live, the political attention and funding that supported the programme begin to disappear.

The result is layering rather than replacement.

The UK National Audit Office has repeatedly documented this in government technology: new services must be built within estates dominated by legacy systems and data, while transformation programmes often underestimate implementation difficulty. Its work also notes that purchasing new technology can add another layer of complexity and cost rather than removing the systems beneath it.

This is not merely old technology surviving too long. It is a structural asymmetry:

Addition has a sponsor. Removal inherits a backlog.

Over decades, that asymmetry produces much of the estate that later transformation programmes are hired to simplify.

Identity

An organisation is often described in terms of what it possesses.

It has systems, applications, buildings, employees, suppliers, budgets, data and processes. Annual reports, inventories and enterprise architecture repositories are filled with lists of these things because they are tangible and comparatively easy to measure.

Ask a different question, however, and the answer becomes much less certain.

What is this organisation?

That question cannot be answered by pointing at a CRM, an ERP, a department or a balance sheet. Those things are expressions of the organisation, but they are not the organisation itself. Replace every application, reorganise every department and recruit an entirely new workforce over the course of twenty years, and the organisation may still be recognisably the same institution. Something more enduring has survived all of those changes.

That enduring quality is what I mean by identity.

11.1 Identity outlives implementation

Most organisations underestimate how much they change.

Teams merge and divide. Responsibilities move between departments. Systems are retired and replaced. Reporting lines shift. Entire operating models are redesigned. Sometimes the organisation itself changes direction, pursuing work that would have been unimaginable a decade earlier.

Yet despite all of this, people continue talking about the organisation as though it were a continuous thing.

11
49

A university remains the same university despite generations of students, academics and administrators passing through it. A charity remains recognisable despite changing funding models, technologies and leadership. Even organisations that merge, divide or radically reinvent themselves often preserve something that people recognise as their identity.

That continuity does not arise from any individual system.

It arises because the organisation preserves enough shared understanding that today's institution can still be understood as the successor to yesterday's.

Architecture should concern itself with protecting that continuity.

11.2 Identity is more than purpose

Purpose is often described as the answer to why an organisation exists.

Identity is broader.

It includes purpose, but also the concepts the organisation recognises, the relationships it maintains, the responsibilities it accepts, the promises it makes and the language through which it understands its own work.

If purpose explains why an organisation exists, identity explains why it remains recognisably itself.

This distinction matters because organisations frequently change what they do without changing who they are. A research institute may begin funding entirely new scientific disciplines while remaining committed to advancing knowledge. A humanitarian organisation may respond to different crises over time while remaining recognisably the same institution. Their work evolves, but their identity provides continuity across that evolution.

11.3 Identity gives change somewhere to return to

Architecture is often described as managing change.

A more useful way to think about it is that architecture manages continuity through change.

Every significant transformation alters something about the organisation. New systems introduce different ways of working. Teams acquire new responsibilities. Old concepts disappear while new ones emerge. Without some stable understanding of what the organisation is trying to remain, change becomes little more than a sequence of disconnected migrations.

Identity provides the reference point against which those changes can be understood.

It allows the organisation to ask not simply "What has changed?", but "What has remained the same?" and "What must remain the same if we are still to be the organisation we believe ourselves to be?"

Those are architectural questions every bit as much as technical ones.

11.4 Identity must be deliberately preserved

Identity is often assumed to emerge naturally.

50

Sometimes it does, particularly in smaller organisations where people share history and context directly.

As organisations grow, however, identity becomes increasingly distributed. No single individual carries the whole picture. Understanding becomes spread across documents, systems, traditions, conversations and institutional memory.

Without deliberate stewardship, that shared understanding begins to fragment.

Different parts of the organisation develop different interpretations of the same concepts. New systems quietly redefine established language. Responsibilities move while the reasons behind them are forgotten. Eventually the organisation still possesses its history, but no longer possesses a coherent understanding of itself.

Identity has not disappeared.

It has become difficult to recognise.

The work of architecture is not to invent an identity for the organisation. It is to preserve and clarify the one that already exists, allowing it to evolve without becoming disconnected from its own past.

11.5 Identity is observable

Identity is sometimes spoken about as though it were abstract or intangible.

In practice, it leaves evidence everywhere.

It appears in the concepts an organisation chooses to name. In the relationships it considers important enough to record. In the decisions it makes repeatedly across decades. In the promises it refuses to compromise, even when circumstances change.

These are not merely cultural artefacts.

They are architectural ones.

An ontology records aspects of identity through shared concepts. Architectural decision records preserve why important choices were made. Provenance records explain how understanding evolved. Version history captures how concepts changed without losing their continuity. None of these is the organisation's identity, but together they provide evidence of how that identity has persisted through time.

11.6 Identity as an architectureal concern

Architecture has traditionally been concerned with systems.

This book argues that systems are only one expression of something more enduring.

Every architectural decision should therefore be evaluated against a simple question:

Does this help the organisation remain recognisably itself as it changes?

Sometimes the answer will be found in a data model. Sometimes in an ontology, a capability map or an interface. Sometimes in a decision not to change at all.

The important point is that architecture should begin with the identity of the organisation rather than with the technology currently used to express it.

51

Because systems will always change.

Identity is what allows those changes to belong to the same organisation.

  • Ask most organisations what they have, and you'll get an inventory, systems, data, headcount, budget. Ask what they are, and the answer is much harder, and much more durable. The mission survives reorganisations, technology migrations, and complete turnover of staff, in a way no system or dataset ever does; identity is the most permanent thing an organisation has.
  • That's why the question worth asking, constantly, is "what are we", not "what data do we have". Why organisations forget

Organisations don't forget because the people inside of them are careless, or because sombody failed to write enough documentation. They forget because organisations are constantly changing while their understanding of themselves is carried by people, systems, and habits that are all temporary.

A decade after a major decision has been made, the decision often remains long after the reasons behind it have disappeared. Systems still behave in peculiar ways. Reports will disagree. Approval processes still exist. Integrations still run every night.

Aks why, and the answer is usually the same: "We've always done it this way".

That's rarely an explanation, and it's evidence that the explanation has more or less already been lost to time.

Institutional memory doesn't disappear in one dramatic event. It erodes slowly through dozens of different mechanisms, each of them individually reasonable.

Technology organisations routinely lose more than labour when people leave. They lose undocumented relationships between systems, explanations for exceptions, knowledge of failed approaches and the ability to recognise when an apparently strange design is protecting something important.

A systematic review of 91 empirical studies found substantial evidence that employee turnover produces organisational knowledge loss, while the effect depends on the nature of the knowledge, the departing person and the organisation’s retention mechanisms. Software-specific research likewise describes turnover as a threat to code quality and team productivity because contributors hold knowledge that may not exist in repositories or documentation.

The organisation preserved the information, but it wholly lost the meaning.

13.4 Documentation ages

Documentation always feels permanent, but it isn't.

You need to see things like wiki pages, as kicking off a slow descent into rot from the moment that they're saved. They age extremely rapidly.

The organisation changes, the work changes, systems are modified, processes evolve, exceptions accumulate and the document remains exactly where it was, looking just as authoritative as it did when it was actually correct.

Written knowledge doesn't really have a way to announce when it's wrong. It just becomes increasingly incomplete until people stop trusting it, and it's ultimately deleted.

13.5 Success makes assumptions invisible

Some of the most dangerous knowledge disappears precisely because nothing has gone wrong. Weird though huh?

You've built some process that works, or an extremely stable and depdendable integration. But when something is successful no one tends to ask why it was successful. It's enough that it works.

Eventually everyone just assumes that it's simply how things are supposed to work. Any original trade-offs disappear from memory, constraints become invisible, temporary decisions and workarounds become permanent.

Success has a way of hiding the assumptions that made it possible.

13.6 Language drifts
59

Your organisation will more or less continue on using the same words through time, but their meanins may slowly change and drift from the original intent.

One department might use "Application" to mean the funding request, and another might treat the entire lifecycle of that request as an "Application". A third can even inherit both meanings and not even realize they have.

No single person suddenly decides that they're going to redefine a word, it's shaped, by the systems, the processes, by misuse, and by incentives.

It drifts slowly enough that no one really notices, as people arrive and leave, and old contexts disappear.

The problem is that every discussion starts out with a an apparent agreement about what language means, but hides a lot of subtle disagreements in the corners.

We all still speak, and we manage to lumber along, but the organisation at a higher level stops being able to understand itself.Z

13.7 Incentives rewrite history

Not all forgetting is accidental.

Sometimes organisations slowly edit their own memories.

Projects that overran become success stories.

Metrics that once exposed problems quietly disappear.

Reports change definitions because the previous definition produced uncomfortable numbers.

Nobody sets out to falsify history.

People simply respond to incentives.

Over time the organisation remembers the version of events that was easiest to live with rather than the one that was most useful to learn from.

That is a particularly dangerous form of forgetting because it feels like certainty.

13.8 Layers hide intent

Every architectural decision solves a problem.

Eventually another decision solves the problems created by the first.

Then another.

And another.

Years later the organisation sees only the accumulated layers.

The original intention has disappeared beneath them.

The architecture survives.

The reasoning does not.

Eventually nobody knows which parts of the system are fundamental and which exist only to support decisions made long ago.

13.9 Forgetting is the default mode

None of these failure modes require incompetence.

In fact, they are often the consequence of perfectly healthy behaviour.

People move on.

Technology improves.

Teams reorganise.

Businesses change.

Documentation ages.

Language evolves.

The remarkable thing is not that organisations forget.

It is that they remember anything at all.

That is why stewardship matters.

The role of architecture is not simply to design systems.

It is to preserve understanding against all of the forces that naturally erase it.

Because organisations store vast amounts of information.

What they struggle to preserve is the understanding that gives that information meaning.

The chapters that follow build on this idea. If meaning is worth preserving, and forgetting is inevitable, then architecture cannot be judged solely by the systems it produces. It must also be judged by how effectively it allows an organisation to remember itself through change.

14.1 Knowledge Has a Half-Life

We often talk about documentation as though it were permanent.

It isn't.

A runbook is accurate on Tuesday.

The only question is whether anyone notices before somebody depends on it.

A team changes its process.

A regulation changes.

A supplier introduces a new product.

An API gains another field.

A definition evolves.

A business starts operating in another country.

Every one of those changes is perfectly reasonable.

The organisation changes continuously.

The documentation changes occasionally.

The gap between the two is where decay lives.

14.3 Authority Outlives Accuracy

One of the dangerous properties of written knowledge is that it continues looking authoritative long after it stops being correct.

A wiki page written five years ago looks exactly like one written yesterday.

A glossary still defines concepts nobody uses.

The appearance of certainty often survives accuracy by years.

Reports fail.

Then whoever has been there longest.

A runbook that nobody follows.

A glossary that nobody references.

An architecture document that no system depends upon.

The fastest way for knowledge to become outdated is for it to stop being part of everyday work.

Knowledge survives through use far more reliably than through storage.

Organisations treat documentation like concrete.

Every description of "how things currently work" has an expiry date.

Integration instructions.

API contracts.

Operational playbooks.

Healthy organisations expect knowledge to expire.

They build review, revision and replacement into the work itself.

14.7 Stewardship Includes Maintenance

Architecture is often associated with creation.

Designing systems.

Producing models.

Writing standards.

Stewardship is equally concerned with maintenance.

Not simply maintaining software.

Maintaining understanding.

Every description of the organisation should be assumed to be drifting unless there is active evidence that it is not.

That applies equally to glossaries, architectural diagrams, process maps, ontologies and operational documentation.

The question is never

The more useful question is

"Why should we believe it is still correct today?"

14.8 Decay Is Inevitable

Knowledge does not decay because people are careless.

It decays because organisations are alive.

Living things change.

Every change leaves yesterday's understanding slightly less complete than it was before.

The answer is not to document everything.

Nor is it to endlessly rewrite documentation.

Because knowledge that can only become older is already beginning to disappear.

The Work of an Architect

Architecture is an unusual discipline because relatively little of its value comes from producing architecture.

That Architects Craft

Diagrams, models, standards, and decisions are outputs. The work itself happens in conversations, investigations, disagreements, design sessions, code incidents and the hundreds of small decisions through which an organisation changes. A useful architect spends much more time helping other people see a problem clearly than telling them what its solution ought to be.

This part is about that work.

It's about learning how to look at a system without pretending you can see all of it; how to communicate complicated ideas without removing their complexity; how to expose trade-offs that have been hidden beneath confident recommendations; and how to leave the people you work with better able to make the next decision without you.

This book has quite rightly been hard on consultants, governance structures, transformations, vendors, and organisational incentives. At some point it alos needs to turn the lens and say:

Architects are capable of exactly the same sins.

We can create diagrams nobody needs, invent standards to justify our presence, centralise decisions ebcause it makes architecture visible, impose abstractions teams didn't ask fo, confuse sophistication with value and leave behind governance that continues long after the problem that created it has disappeared.

We need to practice the same humility and stewardship we demand of everyone else.

Surface the Trade-Offs

Most difficult architectural decisions exist because several options are reasonable. One design is simpler to operate but harder to change. Another gives teams more independence but creates duplication. A commercial product gets us somewhere quickly but increases dependency on a supplier. Building internally preserve control but commits the organisation to maintaining what it builds. Strong consistency makes some classes of error impossible while making availability or distribution harder. A shared platform removes repeated work while creating another thing everyone has to coordinate around.

The job of the architect isn't to make these tensions disappear. It's to make them visible.

A weak architectural recommendation explains why an option is good. A strong on explains what the organisation receives, what it gives up, which assumptions make the exchange worthwhile, and what would cause us to reconsider it later.

Most poor architectural decisions aren't obviously foolish when they're made. They become foolish because one side of the exchange was visible while the other was hidden away.

19.1 Every Design Makes Something Easier

Every architectural choice makes some things easier and other things harder.

A centralized data platform makes common governance and reuse easier. It may make independent experimentation harder. Distributed ownership can place decisions closer to domain expertise while making organisation-wide consistency more expensive. Microservices can allow parts of a system to evolve independently while introducing networking, deployment, and coordination complexity. A large enterprise product may provide considerable capability immediately while forcing the organisation to work within assumption it didn't choose.

19
72

There is no such thing as a neutral architecture.

Even doing nothing has shape to it. Retaining an existing system preserves investment, familiarity and stability while accepting whatever constraints caused us to consider replacing it.

The first habit of useful architecture is you need to stop asking:

Which option is best?

and instead start to ask:

What does each option make easier, and what does it make harder?

Even that small change in language is surprisingly potent. It removes a lot of the moral weight attached to technology decisions. Centralisation is neither enlightened nor backward. Buying is neither inherently sensible, nor inherently dangerous. Open source is not automatically virtuous. Cloud is not intrinsically modern. Each creates a different distribution of cost, control, knowledge, and dependency.

Once those things are visible, the organisation can decicde which distribution it prefers.

19.2 Find What You're Optimizing For

A lot of architecture discussions become arguments because people are unknowingly optimising for different things.

- An engineer may prefer an approach because it's easier to maintain - A product owner may prefer another one because it can be delivered sooner - Security may prefer a third because it reduces the number of trust boundaries - Procurement swings in with another option where they favour an established supplier because the commercial risk is easier to assess. - Leadership might chime in with a specific option whose cost and delivery can be explained more confidently.

Everyone can be correct within the frame they're using.

The architecture discussion becomes useful when those frames are brought into the same room.

Ask

What are we actually trying to optimise for?

Then make the answer specific.

73
  • Not agility
    • Agility to do what?
  • Not scalability
    • What's expected to scale, by how much, and over what period?
  • Not simplicity
    • Simple for who?
  • Not lower risk
    • Which risk specifically?
  • Not flexibility
    • Which future change are we trying to make inexpensive?

Words like scalable, flexible, standardised, modern, secure, and strategic sound like properties everyone should want. They conceal choices precisely because nobody wants to argue the opposite.

The architect's job is to force the adjective back into the real world.

19.3 Look for Hidden Assumptions

Every design rests on assumptions.

Some are technical:

  • Traffic will stay below some specific level.
  • Events will arrive more than once.
  • This source will always expose an API.

Others are organisational:

  • Finance will remain the owner of this process.
  • These two teams will continue coordinating closely.
  • We'll retain people capable of operating this technology.

Others are commercial:

  • The supplier is going to continue offering this capability
  • Pricing will remain proportionate
  • We will be able to extract our data if we leave.

And some are assumptions about the future:

  • This requirement will stay stable
  • We'll eventually consolidate these systems
  • Adoption will increase
  • AI capability will improve enough to automate this work.

An architecture can be perfectly reasonable if its assumptions hold. The problem is that assumptions often enter designs disguised as facts:

74

Here's a useful question:

What would have to be true for this to be a good decision?

Suppose someone recommends buying a large enterprise data platform.

For that to be the right decision, a few things need to be true:

  • the organisation needs capabilities it can't economically assemble from existing components
  • enough teams will use the platform to justify its cost
  • the platform's model fits the organisation sufficiently well
  • internal capability will remain strong enough to govern it
  • and the cost of the dependency is lower than the benefit of standardisation

Those are now actual things we can investigate.

The conversation has moved on from:

"I think we should use this platform"

to:

"Here are the conditions under which using this platform makes sense."

That's architecture.

19.4 Ask What Could Change the Answer

Another pretty powerful question:

What would make you choose differently?

People are remarkably good at defending a preferred solution. They're less often asked to describe the conditions under which they would abandon it.

An engineering advocating for Kafka might say that an ordinary queue would be sufficient if replay were unnecessary and there were only a handful of consumers.

Someone advocating a commercial product might say they would reconsider if more than a certain amount of organisational logic required customisation.

A team proposing a shared service might admit that decentralisation would make more sense if domains genunely needed to evolve independently.

You have now discovered the bnoundary of the decision.

This is a lot more useful than forcing people to argue harder for their original position.

If there's no imaginable evidence that could change someone's answer, you're no longer discussing an architectural choice. You're discussing a preference, identity or commitment.

Those need a wholly different conversation.

The point isn't to catch people out or put people in their place.

Maybe storage fills before compute becomes constrained. Maybe the vendor's API limit appears before the database becomes a problem. Or possibly the technical system scales comfortably but the support model collapses after the tenth team adopts it.

Knowing where failure begins tells you much more about an architecture than being told that it scales.

19.6 Ask Domain Experts Where Reality Escapes the Model

Technical staff are not the only people who hold architectural knowledge.

They're particularly important when someone proposes standardisation. Variation might be accidental or wasteful. It might also encode an important distinction that the proposed design has failed to understand.

The architect shouldn't preseve every exception, but they also shouldn't remove exceptions they haven't understood.

19.7 Ask Business People About Consequences

A stakeholder might have no meaningful opinion about even streaming, graph databases, or service boundaries. They do have an opinion about what needs to stay possible.

  • Waht could we tolerate being slow?

  • How quickly do we need to be able to change it?
  • Who suffers if this decision is wrong?
  • Which part of this process differentiates us from other organisations?
  • What could we happily buy from the market?
  • Which knowledge would we never want to lose?
  • Which decision has to stay ours?
  • What would happen if this capability was unavailable for a day?
  • What would happen if we couldn't change it for six months?

These questions produce architectural constraints without requiring people to speak architecture.

"We need a flexible system" says almost nothing.

"We change funding schemes several times each year and can't wait six months for a supplier to update the workflow" says a lot.

Architecture becomes easiuer when vague aspirations are converted into observable consequences.

19.8 Ask Vendors Where Their Product Stops Fitting

Vendor demonstrations naturally emphasise the happy path.

  • What do customers commonly try to do that you recommend against?
  • Where do customers usually need customization?
  • Which of our requirements would you solve outside the product?

  • What happens when your service is unavailable?

A vendor who can answer these questions clearly is considerably more useful than one who insists everything fits.

  • Ask which parts of the current organisation they would preserve.
  • Ask where their preferred approach has failed elsewhere.
  • Ask what capability the organisation will posess after they leave that it doesn't have today.

19.9 Follow the Complexity

Complexity is very very rarely removed, it's usually just displaced somewhere else.

A managed service removes operational complexity from internal teams but introduces supplier and integration complexity.

A central platform reduces duplicated infrastrcture but increases coordination around the platform.

A standard enterprise product removes the need to build common functionality while introducing the work required to reconcile the orgasniation with the product's model.

An abstraction makes future substitution easier while creating another layer that sombody must understand and maintain.

Whenever a proposal claims to simplify something, ask:

Where did the complexity go?

Sometimes the answer is excellent.

"We no longer operate databases because the provider does it."

Good. That complexity has moved somewhere the organisation is happy for it to live.

Sometimes the answer is less than comfortable.

"We standardised the process"

But teams now maintain spreadsheets to represent exceptions.

The complexity didn't disappear, it became invisible to the architecture.

This is one of the most important habits you can develop: when something gets simpler in one place, look for the corresponding increase somewhere else.

Complexity behaves a little like pressure. Push it out of one part of th esystem and it crops back up at another boundary.

19.10 Follow the Cost

The is true of cost.

Architecture decisions frequently reduce a visible cost by creating a less visible one.

Outsourcing reduces headcount but introduces supplier management and change costs.

A shared service reduces duplicated engineering but introduces waiting.

A governance process reduces the perceived risk of local decisions but increases coordiantion and lead time.

A low-code product reduces the cost of initial development while potentially increasing licensing, integration, and exit costs.

Automation removes manual work while creating software that must now be understood, monitored, and maintained.

The question isn't:

Does this reduce cost?

It's:

Whose cost does it reduce, whose cost does it increase, and when does each cost occur?

INitial costs receive extraordinary attention because they belong to the decision being made now.. Operational costs belong to somebody else in the future. Exist costs may belong to somebody five or ten years away.

You need advocate for the people who aren't in the room yet, they're the ones who will inherit the decision.

19.11 Beware One-Sided Options

Proposals are often presented using lists of advantages. That should immediately make you suspicious, every meaningful option has disadvantages.

If a proposal contains five benefits, and no meaningful costs, you don't yet understand the proposal.

This doesn't require an elaborate architecture framework. A small table is usually good enough:

It's not there to produce a winner mechanically, it prevents the winner from being presented without the bill.

Sometimes just writing the second column is enough to change the conversation.

19.12 Separate Reverisible Decisions from Commitments

Not all trade-offs deserve the same amount of scrutiny.

Choosing a library that can be replaced next month is different from adopting an enterprise platform that will contain ten years of organisational data.

A decision becomes architecturally important when it constrains future decisions.

Ask:

  • How difficult is this to reverse?
  • What becomes dependent on it?
  • How quickly will switching costs accumulate?
  • What knowledge will grow around it?
  • What would leaving require?
  • Can we learn more before committing?
  • Reversible decisions should often be made quickly and locally.

Irreversible or increasingly expensive decisions deserve broader investigation because the organisation will live with their trade-offs long after the original decision has disappeared from memory.

This is also where architects need restraint.

Keeping every option open is itself expensive. Abstraction, portability and configurability all introduce complexity. An architecture capable of supporting every imaginable future may become too complicated to support the present.

Optionality has a price.

The question is whether the future uncertainty is valuable enough to justify paying it.

19.13 Find the Disagreement Beneath the Technology

Many apparent technology disagreements are disagreements about something else.

"We should use microservices."

"We should use a monolith."

Perhaps the real disagreement is about how independently teams need to deploy.

"We need a central data platform."

"We need domain-owned data."

Perhaps the real disagreement is about where responsibility for data quality should live.

"We should buy this."

"We should build it."

Perhaps the disagreement concerns whether the capability is strategically distinctive.

"We need real-time data."

Perhaps somebody needs a dashboard refreshed every five minutes while someone else has interpreted real-time as millisecond event processing.

When architecture discussions become stuck, move upward.

Ask what each side is trying to preserve.

Often both positions become compatible once the hidden requirement is visible.

The architect does not resolve disagreement by choosing a camp.

They change the shape of the conversation until everyone can see what they were actually disagreeing about.

19.14 Do Not Hide the Political Trade-off

Some trade-offs are not technical at all.

  • Centralising a capability changes authority.
  • Creating a shared platform changes which team controls investment.
  • Introducing self-service changes who can act without permission.
  • A semantic model creates the possibility that somebody's established definition will no longer be authoritative.
  • Automation may remove work that currently gives a team influence.

These consequences should not be dismissed as politics interfering with architecture.

They are part of the architecture.

Organisations distribute authority through systems, processes, data and decision rights. Changing any of those things changes who can act, who must ask, who can see and who is responsible when something goes wrong.

A technically superior design may therefore fail because the organisation was unwilling to accept the redistribution of authority required to operate it.

That is not necessarily irrational.

The trade-off simply remained hidden.

19.15 Be Careful with "Best Practice"

Best practice can prematurely close a trade-off conversation.

If something is described as industry standard or best practice, opposing it begins to sound irresponsible. The design no longer needs to explain what it optimises because the external world appears to have already decided.

But a practice developed under one set of constraints may behave very differently under another.

The useful question is:

What problem does this practice solve, and do we have that problem?

Then:

What does adopting it cost us?

This applies equally to cloud architectures, agile methods, data mesh, zero trust, microservices, product operating models, enterprise platforms and whatever replaces them.

82

Architecture should learn aggressively from other organisations.

It should copy reluctantly.

19.16 Record the Exchange, Not Just the Decision

Architectural decision records frequently document what was chosen and why.

They become much more valuable when they also preserve what was deliberately sacrificed.

Instead of:

We selected Product X because it provides the required functionality and integrates with our existing platform.

record:

We selected Product X because speed of adoption and mature commodity functionality currently outweigh our concerns about supplier dependency. We accept that Product X will constrain workflow design and require translation between its customer model and our organisational model. We will reconsider the decision if customisation exceeds this boundary or if switching costs begin to prevent reasonable alternatives.

That record gives a future architect something valuable.

It tells them when the decision stops being valid.

Without that, architecture decisions acquire an accidental permanence. Future teams preserve them because they remember that considerable thought went into them but no longer remember the conditions under which the reasoning held.

A good decision should carry its expiry conditions with it.

19.17 Know When to Stop

Trade-off analysis can become its own form of avoidance.

There will always be another risk to investigate, another scenario to model and another alternative worth considering.

Architecture is not the elimination of uncertainty.

Eventually there is enough information to make a responsible choice.

The architect should therefore also ask:

Which unanswered question could genuinely change our decision?

If none of the remaining questions would change the answer, stop.

If one could, investigate that one.

This prevents architecture becoming a ceremonial search for certainty that cannot exist.

The goal is not perfect knowledge.

It is sufficient understanding of the exchange being made.

19.18 Leave the Choice Visible

A good architectural conversation should leave people able to describe the decision without referring to the architect.

They should be able to say:

We chose this because these properties matter more to us right now.

We know that means accepting these costs.

These assumptions support the decision.

If those assumptions change, we will reconsider it.

That is a much healthier outcome than:

Architecture told us to use this.

The first organisation has learned something.

The second has acquired a rule.

Architecture earns its value by making consequential choices easier to see before they become expensive to reverse. It does not remove uncertainty, and it cannot make incompatible qualities simultaneously achievable. It gives the organisation enough clarity to decide which compromises it is prepared to inhabit.

Every architecture is a collection of trade-offs.

The dangerous ones are simply the trade-offs nobody realised they were making. // Questions that reveal assumptions, constraints and hidden choices.

Ask Vendors Where Their Incentives Stop Matching Yours

Vendor conversations deserve a different level of scrutiny from internal design discussions because the relationship is structurally different.

An engineer inside the organisation may be wrong, overconfident or attached to a preferred technology, but they usually remain exposed to the long-term consequences of the choice. They will have to operate it, explain it, change it and eventually repair whatever assumptions turn out to be wrong.

A vendor does not necessarily carry those same consequences.

Their responsibility is to sell, implement and retain the use of their product. That is not an accusation of bad faith. It is simply the commercial arrangement. The vendor succeeds when the organisation adopts more of the product, renews the contract, expands its footprint and becomes a reference customer. The organisation succeeds when it solves its problem at an acceptable cost while retaining enough control to change direction later.

Those goals overlap.

They are not identical.

The architect should therefore spend less time establishing whether a product can satisfy a requirement and more time discovering the conditions under which using that product stops serving the organisation.

20.1 Do not ask whether it can do something

Almost every mature enterprise product can be made to do an extraordinary range of things.

The more useful question is whether doing those things is natural to the product.

20
85

There is a large difference between:

Can your platform support this process?

and:

Is this the kind of process your platform was designed to support?

The first question invites a demonstration.

The second invites an architectural answer.

A vendor can often satisfy an unusual requirement through configuration, scripting, custom objects, plugins, professional services or another product in the same suite. Every one of those mechanisms may be legitimate. The danger lies in gradually moving farther from the product's natural shape while continuing to describe the result as an off-the-shelf solution.

Ask:

Which parts of our requirement fit your product poorly?

Where would you advise us not to use it?

Which requirement would make you question whether we are the right customer?

What do customers commonly customise that they later regret?

Which parts of our process would you expect us to change rather than configure the product around?

A supplier that can clearly describe its own limits is usually giving you more useful information than one able to demonstrate another feature.

20.2 Ask them to describe the bad fit

Vendor demonstrations are designed around successful paths.

A customer exists. A workflow starts. An approval is completed. A dashboard updates. Everything proceeds through the state model as intended.

Real organisations spend much of their lives outside those paths.

Ask the vendor to demonstrate the awkward cases.

Show us what happens when the data is incomplete.

Show us how two conflicting records are resolved.

Show us what happens when a process must move backwards.

Show us how we represent an exception that does not fit the standard workflow.

86

Show us how the system behaves when ownership is disputed.

Show us what an administrator has to do when the automation fails halfway through.

Show us how historical meaning is preserved when a definition changes.

The point is not to invent pathological edge cases.

It is to see whether the product has a coherent way of representing the kinds of mess the organisation already knows exist.

A product that only works cleanly after reality has been simplified to fit it may still be appropriate. The organisation should simply understand that the simplification is part of the purchase.

20.3 Ask what gets expensive later

Initial adoption is usually where a vendor is most competitive.

The organisation has not yet accumulated much data, many users, specialist integrations or organisational dependency. Migration costs are still hypothetical. The supplier is competing for the business.

The economics may look very different after five years.

Ask directly:

What causes customers' costs to increase most quickly?

Which usage dimensions affect pricing?

What typically becomes expensive after the first implementation?

Which features require higher licensing tiers?

How is historic data priced?

What happens when we double the number of users?

How does pricing change when more departments adopt the product?

Which capabilities are commonly introduced as add-ons later?

What professional-services work do mature customers still require from you?

Do not evaluate the answer solely as a forecast of licence cost.

You are looking for gravity.

87

The product may become economically difficult to leave because it has become technically useful. That is not necessarily bad. But the organisation should understand how that gravity will accumulate before building the rest of its estate around it.

20.4 Ask where the product creates dependency

Lock-in discussions often remain too superficial.

They focus on whether data can technically be exported.

That is only one form of dependency.

The more important question is:

What will we know only because we use this product?

An organisation may be able to export every row of data and still be unable to reconstruct the system.

  • Business rules may exist in proprietary workflow definitions.
  • Authorisation logic may be encoded in configuration.
  • Relationships may depend on internal identifiers.
  • Historical states may not survive export.
  • Users may have spent years learning product-specific ways of working.
  • Specialist teams may have formed around administering it.
  • Integrations may rely directly on vendor APIs.

The organisation's language itself may have begun adopting the vendor's terminology.

Ask:

What information cannot be represented outside your product without translation?

Which configuration objects are exportable?

Can we reconstruct workflows independently from the system?

Are authorisation rules portable?

Which identifiers are proprietary?

Which APIs or behaviours are unique to your platform?

What documentation would allow another supplier to reproduce our implementation?

Which skills would we need to recruit if we stopped using your professional services?

Dependency is often encoded in knowledge long before it becomes visible in contracts.

20.5 Ask how customers leave
88

One of the strongest questions in a vendor conversation is also one of the simplest:

Tell us about customers who have left.

Not merely whether exit is supported.

Ask how it actually happens.

How long does a typical exit take?

What data is provided?

In what formats?

What is not included?

What help do you provide during transition?

What does that support cost?

Do customers normally run both systems in parallel?

Which parts of the implementation are hardest to recreate elsewhere?

What happens to our data after termination?

How long do we retain access?

What happens if we need extraction after the contract ends?

Which customers have successfully migrated from your platform to another?

A vendor will naturally have many stories about customers arriving.

Architecture should be equally interested in customers leaving.

A product that provides a credible account of departure is easier to treat as a component rather than a permanent commitment.

20.6 Ask which assumptions the product makes about your organisation

Every enterprise product contains an organisational model.

It has opinions about ownership, hierarchy, workflow, identity, status, permissions and what constitutes a valid transaction.

Those opinions may never appear in the feature list.

Ask:

What assumptions does your product make about organisational structure?

89

Can responsibility exist independently of hierarchy?

Can one thing belong to several domains?

How does the system represent changing ownership over time?

What happens when two parts of the organisation legitimately use different definitions?

Does the product require one authoritative record?

How are exceptions represented?

Which concepts are fixed by the platform?

These questions are particularly important for systems that become systems of record.

You are not merely buying software.

You may be adopting a model of how the organisation works.

That model deserves at least as much scrutiny as the technology.

20.7 Distinguish configuration from customisation from integration

These words are routinely blurred during sales conversations.

They should not be.

Configuration uses supported mechanisms intended to vary between customers.

Customisation changes or extends the product to represent behaviour the standard product does not naturally contain.

Integration moves responsibility across the product boundary.

Each has a different architectural consequence.

Ask the supplier to categorise every major requirement explicitly.

Is this native capability?

Is this configuration?

Is this customisation?

Is this integration?

Does it require professional services?

Is it supported during upgrades?

90

Who owns the code?

Who tests it when the platform changes?

A solution that is "80% standard" can sound reassuring until the remaining 20% contains the organisation's most important processes.

The percentage itself tells you almost nothing.

What matters is which twenty percent.

20.8 Ask what upgrades will break

Products change.

This is obvious, but vendor evaluations frequently treat today's feature set as though it were a stable architectural object.

Ask:

Which customisations commonly make upgrades difficult?

Which APIs have changed significantly in the last five years?

What backward-compatibility guarantees do you provide?

What happens when a feature we depend upon is deprecated?

How much notice do customers receive?

Can we defer upgrades?

What happens if we cannot?

Which changes are under your control but would materially affect our implementation?

This exposes an important distinction.

When you build something yourself, you accept the cost of maintaining it.

When you buy something, you accept the cost of somebody else changing it.

Those are different risks, but they are both risks.

Ask how the product fails

20.9 Architecture conversations often concentrate on availability percentages.

Those numbers matter, but they do not tell you how failure is experienced.

Ask:

91

What does partial failure look like?

What happens when authentication works but a downstream service does not?

What happens to writes during an outage?

Can users continue working?

How are retries handled?

Can messages be lost or duplicated?

What happens after restoration?

Who is responsible for reconciliation?

What visibility do we have during an incident?

What information will your support team need from us?

What information can we obtain without opening a support case?

A managed product does not remove failure.

It changes the boundary at which you can respond to failure.

That boundary needs to be understood before the system becomes critical.

20.10 Ask who carries the unresolved work

There is a class of vendor answer that sounds reassuring:

"That's usually handled by the customer."

Listen carefully when you hear it.

Ask who exactly will perform that work.

  • Data cleansing.
  • Exception handling.
  • Migration reconciliation.
  • Permissions administration.
  • Integration monitoring.
  • Semantic mapping.
  • Configuration maintenance.
  • Release testing.
  • User support.
92

The supplier may be providing a very effective product while transferring significant operational work back to the organisation.

That is perfectly acceptable when deliberate.

It becomes dangerous when the organisation believes it has purchased an end-to-end capability and discovers after implementation that it has really purchased a sophisticated component surrounded by new internal responsibilities.

Ask:

What work must we permanently perform to make this product successful?

That is often more revealing than asking what the product does.

20.11 Ask for evidence beyond reference customers

Reference customers are useful.

They are also selected evidence.

Vendors naturally introduce organisations where the implementation went well, the relationship remains healthy and someone is prepared to speak publicly.

Ask for the denominator.

How many customers of our size use this capability?

What proportion have adopted the feature you are recommending?

How many implementations required substantial customisation?

What is the median implementation duration rather than the fastest?

How often do customers exceed the original implementation budget?

What are the most common reasons projects struggle?

What proportion of customers still depend on your professional services after three years?

How many customers have stopped using this capability?

The vendor may not disclose all of this.

That itself tells you something about what evidence is and is not available.

A case study tells you that success is possible.

It does not tell you how probable it is.

20.12 Ask what the salesperson is not measured on

This is a more direct question, but in the right conversation it is incredibly useful.

93

Ask:

What are you personally incentivised to achieve in this engagement?

You may not get a detailed compensation plan, nor should you necessarily expect one.

But the underlying issue can still be discussed.

Is success:

  • selling the licence?
  • increasing adoption?
  • expanding the number of modules?
  • renewal?
  • customer satisfaction?
  • implementation completion?
  • long-term outcome?

The same question should be asked of implementation partners and consultancies.

A reseller may earn more when the product footprint grows.

An implementation partner may earn more when customisation increases.

A consultancy may earn more when the transformation becomes larger.

Again, this does not imply misconduct.

It tells you where independent scrutiny is required.

The architect should understand the commercial machinery surrounding an architectural recommendation just as carefully as the technical machinery.

20.13 Separate the vendor from the adviser

One particularly dangerous arrangement occurs when the organisation asks someone who benefits from a particular solution to define the problem.

A platform vendor performs the discovery.

Unsurprisingly, the resulting problem looks like something the platform solves.

An implementation partner writes the target architecture.

Unsurprisingly, successful delivery requires substantial implementation services.

A consultancy identifies organisational fragmentation.

Unsurprisingly, the solution becomes a transformation programme.

None of these recommendations need be dishonest.

The problem is epistemic.

The organisation has allowed the party selling the answer to determine the question.

Where the decision is strategically significant, architecture should insist on separating these activities enough to provide meaningful challenge.

94

The people evaluating whether a capability should be purchased should have some independence from those rewarded when it is purchased.

20.14 Ask what they would advise you not to buy

This may be the single best test.

After discussing the organisation's needs, ask:

Which parts of your product or service do you think we should not buy?

Then listen.

A supplier that understands the organisation and is prepared to establish a boundary around its own commercial opportunity is signalling something valuable.

The same applies to consultants:

Which parts of this problem do you think we can solve ourselves?

Where should we strengthen internal capability rather than purchase yours?

What should already exist inside the organisation when your engagement ends?

It is not proof of alignment.

But an adviser incapable of recommending less should be treated differently from one capable of explaining where their own involvement should stop.

20.15 Make the trade explicit

At the end of the evaluation, the architecture should be able to describe the commercial trade without promotional language.

Not:

Product X is a market-leading platform that will accelerate our digital transformation.

But:

Product X allows us to avoid building and operating these capabilities ourselves. In exchange, we accept these constraints on our operating model, these forms of supplier dependency, these recurring costs and this estimated cost of exit. We retain ownership of these concepts and capabilities outside the product because they are strategically important to us.

That is a purchasing decision the organisation can reason about.

The purpose of vendor questioning is not to prove that suppliers cannot be trusted.

Trust is not the issue.

Alignment is.

95

The supplier can behave entirely ethically, deliver exactly what was promised and create considerable value while still preferring an outcome that is not optimal for the organisation.

Good architecture assumes that this divergence will exist somewhere.

Its responsibility is to find out where before the organisation becomes too dependent to care.

Communicate for the Decision

Architecture requires talking about the same problem in many different ways.

An engineer may need to understand how a failure occurs. A product manager needs to understand what behaviour it prevents. Someone responsible for a service needs to know what operational burden it creates. A finance colleague may need to understand how the choice changes future cost. A senior leader may need to know whether it constrains an important organisational capability.

None of these people needs a simplified version of the truth.

They need the part of the truth that allows them to make a decision.

That distinction is important. Good architectural communication is not about translating technical language into increasingly vague business language. It is about preserving the causal relationship while changing which parts of it receive attention.

The same skill applies in an organisation of ten people or ten thousand. Responsibility may be distributed across departments in one and across individuals in another. The architect still has to understand what somebody knows, what they are responsible for, and what decision the conversation is trying to support.

21.1 Start With the Decision

Before explaining an architecture, ask what the other person needs to decide.

That sounds obvious, but architecture discussions often begin with what the architect wants to explain.

21
97

A team has investigated an identity problem for three weeks, so the presentation begins with identity models. An engineer has discovered an interesting failure mode, so the meeting begins with queues and retries. A platform team has spent months developing a capability, so the discussion begins with the platform.

The audience may need none of those things yet.

Suppose five systems maintain separate identifiers for the same organisations.

An engineer considering an integration needs to know:

Which identifier should I persist, and how do I resolve the others?

Someone responsible for reporting needs to know:

Can two reports disagree because they identify organisations differently?

A programme considering another CRM needs to know:

Will adopting this product create another independent representation of organisation identity?

Someone deciding where to invest may need to know:

Are we repeatedly paying teams to solve the same organisational problem?

The underlying condition has not changed.

The decision has.

Architecture communication should begin there.

21.2 Preserve the Mechanism

There is a dangerous form of simplification in which the explanation gets shorter by removing the thing that actually causes the problem.

Consider:

We have a data quality problem.

That could mean almost anything.

Compare it with:

Five systems independently identify the organisations we work with. Teams therefore reconstruct the relationships between those identities whenever data is combined, and different mappings can produce different answers.

That explanation is still accessible to someone who has never designed a database.

More importantly, it preserves causality.

They can ask sensible questions:

98
  • Why do five systems maintain identities?
  • Could we establish one shared identity?
  • Which systems would have to change?
  • What happens when two records cannot be reconciled?

The architect has given them something they can reason about.

A useful explanation often has the form:

Because this is true, this keeps happening, and therefore this becomes difficult.

For example:

Because several systems contain their own definition of committed spend, teams repeatedly reconcile those definitions when producing organisation-wide reports. That means reporting remains expensive and different parts of the organisation can legitimately produce different answers to the same question.

You can remove implementation detail without removing the mechanism.

That is the difference between clarity and oversimplification.

21.3 Talk About Consequences

Technology itself is rarely the useful starting point outside a technical design conversation.

Someone considering Kafka does not necessarily need to understand distributed logs before they can participate in the decision.

They may need to understand the trade:

Several consumers need to process the same events independently, and some need to replay events that happened previously. We can introduce infrastructure designed for that purpose, but doing so gives us another distributed system that we must operate and understand permanently.

Now there is a decision.

  • Do we actually need replay?
  • How many consumers are expected?
  • Is the operational burden proportionate?
  • Could something simpler satisfy the requirement?

The technology remains available for deeper discussion when it becomes relevant.

This approach is particularly useful with domain specialists and non-technical colleagues because it does not require them to pretend to have opinions about implementation choices. They can contribute knowledge about consequences, which is usually what the architecture needs from them anyway.

Instead of:

99

Should this be eventually consistent?

ask:

If somebody changes this information here, how long could another part of the organisation safely continue seeing the old value?

Instead of:

Do we need high availability?

ask:

What actually happens if this system is unavailable for an hour?

Instead of:

Should we use event sourcing?

ask:

Do we need to know what we believed at a particular point in the past, or only what we believe now?

The architectural property emerges from the answer.

21.4 Different People See Different Parts of the Problem

People understand systems through their relationship with them.

  • Engineers tend to see mechanisms and failure modes.
  • Domain specialists see distinctions, exceptions and judgement.
  • People operating services see recurring work and fragility.
  • Product staff see outcomes, users and priorities.
  • Finance sees commitments and costs.
  • Security sees exposure and control.
  • Leadership sees organisational capability, dependency and choices that may be difficult to reverse.

These are not increasingly abstract versions of reality.

They are different projections of it.

Architecture becomes useful when those projections can be connected.

  • An engineer may know that a change requires coordinated releases across four services.
  • A delivery manager knows that apparently small changes routinely take six weeks.
  • A leader knows that the organisation cannot respond quickly when policy changes.

Those may all be observations of the same architectural condition.

The architect can connect them:

100

A policy change currently requires coordinated modification of four separate implementations of the same rule. The coordination is why relatively small changes take several weeks. Until that rule is represented once and consumed consistently, changing policy will continue to require coordinated delivery across several teams.

Everyone can now see their part of the problem in the same explanation.

21.5 Do Not Assume Technical and Non-Technical Audiences

The division between technical and non-technical people is often unhelpful.

  • A finance specialist may understand the behaviour of an ERP better than most engineers.
  • A grants officer may understand a data model immediately because they understand the distinctions the model is attempting to represent.
  • A senior executive may have spent twenty years building software.
  • An engineer may know almost nothing about the business process their service implements.

Adjusting communication according to job title is therefore unreliable.

Find out what the person already understands.

  • Ask questions.
  • Draw something.
  • Use their terminology.

Allow them to tell you when you have gone too far into detail or removed too much.

The goal is not to pitch the explanation at an imagined level of technical literacy.

It is to establish enough shared understanding to make progress.

21.6 Use Their Language Without Losing Yours

Every part of an organisation develops language around its work.

Use it.

If someone talks about grants, applicants and funding decisions, begin there rather than translating everything immediately into entities, state transitions and relationships.

If an engineer is already reasoning in terms of transactions and idempotency, there is no value in replacing those precise terms with vague organisational language.

Architecture needs both vocabularies.

The skill is connecting them.

A Grant has one authoritative funding decision.

might become:

That means the API cannot simply accept whichever decision arrives last, because an update has to preserve the authority and history of the decision.

101

The domain statement creates the technical constraint.

Or:

This workflow cannot represent two concurrent approval routes.

may become:

Some applications legitimately undergo two independent review processes, so the current implementation is collapsing two different organisational relationships into a single status.

The technical limitation exposes a problem in the organisational model.

Communication is strongest when people can see the connection between the language they already use and the architectural consequence it creates.

21.7 Be Precise About Uncertainty

Different audiences do not justify different levels of certainty.

If something is unknown, say so.

This becomes particularly important when architectural information passes through several people. Uncertainty tends to disappear as information is summarised.

An engineer says:

We haven't tested this above ten million records, but based on the current access pattern we don't expect a problem.

By the time the statement reaches a programme report it becomes:

The solution scales.

Those are not equivalent statements.

The architect should preserve qualifiers that matter:

We do not currently have evidence that scale will be a constraint, but this has not yet been tested at the expected five-year volume.

Likewise:

The supplier says this is supported.

is different from:

We have demonstrated that it works with our workflow.

And:

We believe this will reduce reconciliation.

is different from:

102

Reconciliation will be eliminated.

Precision is not excessive caution.

It prevents organisational confidence from gradually exceeding the evidence underneath it.

21.8 Explain the Trade, Not Just the Recommendation

Architectural recommendations become more credible when people can see what they are being asked to accept.

Instead of:

We should centralise this capability.

say:

Centralising it means we can define this concept once and stop rebuilding it in each system. In exchange, teams that need the concept will become dependent on the shared service, so its availability and responsiveness become more important.

Instead of:

We should buy this product.

say:

Buying gives us mature commodity functionality quickly, but means accepting the vendor's workflow model and making future change partly dependent on them.

Instead of:

We should build this ourselves.

say:

This capability changes frequently with our own policy and contains organisation-specific judgement. Owning it gives us control over that change, but means accepting permanent responsibility for operating and maintaining it.

People can disagree with these decisions.

That is healthy.

The job of the architect is to make sure they are disagreeing about the real exchange rather than slogans such as build versus buy, centralised versus federated, or modern versus legacy.

21.9 Use Examples Before Abstractions

Architecture tends naturally towards abstraction.

Sometimes the quickest route to shared understanding is the opposite.

103

Start with one real case.

Yesterday, Finance and Grants produced different figures for the same funding scheme.

Then trace it.

  • Why were they different?
  • Where did each number originate?
  • Which definition did each use?
  • Who believed their definition was authoritative?
  • Where did the divergence enter the systems?

Once the concrete case is understood, the broader architectural pattern often becomes obvious:

We don't have a shared operational definition of committed spend.

The abstraction now has evidence underneath it.

This is much easier to discuss than beginning with a conceptual statement about semantic consistency.

Examples also provide a check against architecture becoming detached from actual work. If a proposed principle cannot explain a real case, it may not yet be useful.

21.10 Know What Detail to Leave Out

Good communication requires omission.

A diagram containing every component is usually less useful than one containing the five components relevant to the decision.

An explanation of an integration problem probably does not need to begin with Kubernetes.

A conversation about organisational identity probably does not need to explain the RDF representation used to encode it.

Leaving something out is not dishonest when it does not affect the reasoning.

The useful test is:

If the omitted detail were different, could it change the decision?

If not, leave it out.

If yes, preserve it.

That creates a much more defensible approach to abstraction than trying to guess what somebody is capable of understanding.

21.11 Give People a Way Back Into the Detail

Concise communication should not create a dead end.

Someone hearing:

104

We currently depend on this supplier for a capability we cannot reproduce ourselves.

may reasonably ask:

What specifically would prevent us from replacing them?

The architect should be able to travel back into the evidence.

Perhaps the answer is:

  • the workflows exist only in proprietary configuration;
  • there is no complete export;
  • seven systems consume proprietary identifiers;
  • and no permanent employee currently understands the implementation well enough to reconstruct it.

This is why technical depth and communication ability reinforce one another.

You can be concise with confidence when you know the detail is still available underneath the concise statement.

Without that foundation, concise architectural communication becomes assertion.

21.12 Don't Use Abstraction to Gain Authority

There is a particular kind of architectural language that sounds important because nobody can easily disagree with it.

  • Strategic alignment.
  • Enterprise agility.
  • AI enablement.
  • Data-driven transformation.
  • Future-ready architecture.

These phrases may describe genuine objectives.

On their own they describe almost nothing.

Architecture should become more concrete when challenged, not retreat into increasingly impressive abstraction.

Ask:

What would we actually observe being different?

"We would become more agile."

How?

"Teams would be able to change processes more quickly."

What currently stops them?

"Changes require modifications by an external supplier."

105

Now there is something architectural to work with.

An architect should be suspicious of any statement that cannot eventually be connected to a decision, behaviour, system, constraint or piece of evidence.

21.13 Check That the Message Survives

A useful exercise is to express the same architectural condition in several ways and check that none contradict the others.

For example:

ImplementationThree systems calculate committed spend independently using different rules.
DeliveryChanges to the definition require coordinated updates across several implementations.
ReportingDifferent reports can produce different values for committed spend.
OrganisationWellcome does not currently have one operational definition of an important financial concept.
DecisionUntil that definition is stabilised, organisation-wide reporting will continue requiring reconciliation.

These are not levels of intelligence or hierarchy.

They are views of the same condition for different purposes.

If one explanation cannot be reconciled with another, investigate.

Something has probably been lost in translation.

21.14 Ask What Matters to Them

One of the easiest ways to communicate well is simply to ask.

What decision are you trying to make?

What are you worried about?

Which part of this matters to you?

How much detail would be useful?

What would you need to know to be comfortable with this?

This prevents the architect from guessing what another role ought to care about.

Sometimes a COO wants to understand the database problem in detail.

Sometimes an engineer only needs to know that the organisation has accepted a constraint and can move on.

Sometimes the most technically sophisticated person in the room needs the simplest explanation because the decision they are making has nothing to do with the implementation.

Meet the conversation where it actually is.

106
21.15 Leave Shared Understanding Behind

The measure of good architectural communication is not whether the architect explained something elegantly.

It is whether the people involved can reason about the problem afterwards.

A useful conversation leaves people able to say:

This is the condition we're dealing with.

This is why it happens.

These are the consequences.

These are the choices available to us.

This is what each choice costs.

This is what we still don't know.

The architect should not need to remain in every subsequent conversation to translate the architecture again.

That would make architecture another dependency.

Communication is part of stewardship because organisations cannot preserve decisions they never genuinely understood. The architect's responsibility is therefore not to find the correct level of abstraction, or to learn a special language for executives and another for engineers.

It is much simpler than that.

Understand what the other person is trying to decide.

Preserve the parts of reality that matter to that decision.

And explain them in terms that allow the other person to think for themselves.

Moving from engineer to executive without losing the substance.

Make Other People Smarter

Architecture is often practised as though the architect's value comes from knowing the answer.

A team presents a problem, the architect evaluates it, and eventually returns with a recommendation. The architect's expertise becomes visible because they can see something other people could not. In the short term this can be useful. In the long term it creates exactly the wrong dependency.

If every difficult decision requires the architect to be present, the organisation has not gained architectural capability. It has acquired another bottleneck.

A better measure of architectural work is whether the people involved become better able to make the next decision without you.

That does not mean withholding expertise, pretending not to know things or turning every meeting into a teaching exercise. It means using expertise in a way that improves the judgement of the people around you. Sometimes that means giving an answer. More often it means asking the question that reveals why the answer follows.

23.1 Do Not Rush to Demonstrate That You Know

Experienced architects often recognise patterns quickly.

Someone proposes a microservice and you can already see that the boundary is artificial.

A team proposes Kafka and you suspect they only need a queue.

A programme wants to buy an enterprise platform and you can already see where the vendor model will collide with the organisation.

23
109

The temptation is to say so immediately.

Sometimes you should.

But doing so too quickly has a cost.

You replace the other person's reasoning with your own.

The engineer learns that the architect dislikes this design. They do not necessarily learn what made the design weak. The product manager learns that architecture rejected the product. They do not necessarily understand what property of the product created the concern.

The next time the same pattern appears, they still need you.

Instead of:

We don't need Kafka here.

try:

What property of this problem requires us to retain and replay the event history?

Instead of:

This should not be a microservice.

try:

Which part of this needs to change independently from the rest?

Instead of:

That abstraction isn't buying us anything.

try:

Which future change are we expecting this abstraction to protect us from?

The question contains much of the reasoning without giving away the conclusion.

It also creates the possibility that the other person has an answer you had not considered.

That matters.

23.2 Ask Questions That Contain a Model

The best architectural questions are rarely generic.

"What do you think?" can be useful, but it does not necessarily help someone reason differently.

A stronger question introduces a useful distinction.

What becomes easier if we choose this, and what becomes harder?

110

introduces trade-offs.

What would have to be true for this to remain a good decision?

introduces assumptions.

What fails first?

introduces failure boundaries.

What would make us choose differently?

introduces falsifiability.

Which part of this is specific to our organisation?

introduces the distinction between commodity and strategic capability.

Which decision must remain ours if we buy this?

introduces dependency.

The question is doing more than eliciting information.

It is teaching a way of looking at the problem.

Once people become accustomed to these questions, they begin asking them before the architect arrives.

That is the outcome you want.

23.3 Let People Finish the Thought

Sometimes the architect already knows where a conversation is going.

Do not always finish it for people.

Suppose someone says:

If we put all of this logic in Salesforce, we'd get the workflow functionality quickly, but every time the process changes we'd need to...

Leave the silence.

They may finish:

...change the Salesforce configuration.

Then ask:

Who currently has the ability to do that?

Now they may discover the dependency themselves.

This is a subtle skill.

People tend to remember conclusions they reached much more strongly than conclusions delivered to them. More importantly, they also understand the reasoning that produced the conclusion.

The architect's role is sometimes to create enough structure around a problem that the answer becomes difficult not to see.

23.4 Ask for the Other Option

People become attached to designs partly because the design they are discussing becomes the whole problem space.

Ask for another option.

Not necessarily a better one.

Just another.

What is the simplest version of this?

What would we do if we weren't allowed to buy anything?

What would we do if we had to buy rather than build?

What would this look like if there were only one team?

What would it look like if ten teams had to use it?

What would we do if this system disappeared tomorrow?

Creating a second option forces comparison.

Once there are two plausible approaches, assumptions begin becoming visible.

The goal is not to create endless architecture alternatives. Two or three are usually enough to make the trade-offs concrete.

An architect who repeatedly asks for alternatives also teaches people that the first workable solution is not automatically the architecture.

23.5 Ask People to Explain the Constraint

When someone tells you that something "has to" be done a particular way, ask why.

Not aggressively.

Just follow the constraint.

Why does that need to be synchronous?

Why must that live in the CRM?

Why does one team need to own all of this?

112

Why does the customer identifier need to come from this system?

Why does the data need to be real-time?

Why must we use the same product across every department?

Often the first answer merely moves the constraint:

Because Finance needs it immediately.

What does immediately mean?

Before the morning report.

Now "real-time" has become "available by 8am".

That may result in a radically different architecture.

The useful habit is to keep moving until the constraint reaches something real: a legal obligation, a user need, a physical limitation, an economic boundary or an explicit organisational choice.

A surprising amount of architecture is built around constraints that nobody remembers choosing.

23.6 Ask Engineers to Teach You

Making other people smarter does not imply that the architect occupies the teacher's role.

Often the most useful thing you can do is ask someone to explain their area to you.

Show me why that is difficult.

Walk me through what happens when this fails.

Where does the data actually come from?

Why did you structure it this way?

What have we tried before?

This does several things at once.

It gives you access to detail that architecture documents rarely contain.

It forces the person explaining the system to organise their own mental model.

It shows respect for expertise.

And it creates a conversation rather than an inspection.

Sometimes the act of explaining exposes the problem without the architect saying anything.

113

An engineer describing a deployment process may suddenly realise that three manual steps exist only because two teams do not trust the same automation.

A domain specialist explaining a reporting discrepancy may recognise that two departments are using different definitions of the same word.

The architect did not solve the problem.

They created the conditions in which it became visible.

23.7 Do Not Pretend Not to Know

There is an opposite failure mode.

Socratic questioning can become irritating very quickly when everyone knows the architect already has an opinion.

People can tell when they are being led through a sequence of questions whose answer has already been decided.

That is not collaboration.

It is a quiz.

If you know something important, say it.

I think there is a problem with this boundary because these two components always change together. Before we decide that, though, can you show me why you separated them?

That is much more respectful than pretending to discover the concern together.

The principle is not "never give answers".

It is:

Give enough of your reasoning that people can challenge it.

Architecture should not operate through riddles.

23.8 Expose Your Own Reasoning

When you make a recommendation, show the structure underneath it.

Instead of:

I would keep this capability internal.

say:

I would keep this internal because the rules change frequently, they encode organisation-specific judgement, and we do not have a clean way to specify that judgement contractually. If those conditions changed, buying it might make more sense.

Now the other person has learned something reusable.

They can apply the same criteria elsewhere.

114

They can also challenge you:

The rules don't actually change very often.

Good.

Now the architectural disagreement has become about evidence rather than authority.

The strongest architects are not those whose conclusions are hardest to challenge.

They are those whose reasoning is easiest to inspect.

23.9 Turn Preferences Into Principles

People naturally develop preferences.

  • Postgres.
  • Kafka.
  • Kubernetes.
  • AWS.
  • REST.
  • GraphQL.
  • Monoliths.
  • Microservices.
  • Open source.
  • Commercial products.

These preferences usually have history behind them. Someone has had success with a technology or been badly burned by another.

The architect can help turn those experiences into something more transferable.

If an engineer says:

I hate ORMs.

Ask:

What problems have they caused you?

Perhaps the actual concern is hidden query behaviour and difficulty predicting performance.

Now the lesson is not:

Don't use ORMs.

It is:

We need database access behaviour to remain observable and predictable where query performance matters.

That principle can survive the next tool.

115

Likewise:

We should never buy enterprise software.

may conceal:

We have repeatedly lost control when organisation-specific process logic became trapped inside proprietary platforms.

That is a useful architectural concern.

The preference was only its rough outer shell.

23.10 Teach People to Separate Facts, Assumptions and Choices

Many architecture discussions become confused because different types of statement are mixed together.

Consider:

We need to use the vendor's customer identifier because Salesforce is our CRM.

There may be three different things hidden inside that sentence.

A fact:

Salesforce currently assigns a customer identifier.

An assumption:

Salesforce will remain our CRM for the foreseeable future.

A choice:

Other systems should adopt that identifier as the organisation-wide identity.

Those are not the same thing.

Teach people to separate them.

Ask:

Which part of that is a fact today?

Which part are we assuming will continue?

Which part are we choosing?

This is an extraordinarily useful discipline because choices disguised as facts tend to become permanent.

The same technique works with programme decisions:

We have to migrate everything this year.

116

Why?

Perhaps the supplier contract expires this year.

That is a fact.

Perhaps leadership believes extending it would be unacceptable.

That is a choice.

Perhaps the team assumes another extension cannot be negotiated.

That is an assumption.

Now the decision space is visible again.

23.11 Let People Be Right

Sometimes an architect asks a question expecting one answer and gets a better one.

Take the loss gracefully.

An engineer may have considered the failure mode you thought they missed.

A business specialist may explain why the apparently unnecessary exception is essential.

A vendor may have a stronger portability story than you expected.

Do not move the goalposts because your challenge was answered.

Say:

Yes, that addresses the concern.

This is one of the simplest ways to build trust.

People become much more willing to expose unfinished thinking when they know architecture is genuinely evaluating the answer rather than searching for a reason to reject it.

The objective is not for the architect to be right.

The objective is for the design to become better understood.

23.12 Make It Safe to Say "I Don't Know"

Architecture deals constantly with uncertainty.

People should be able to say:

I don't know.

We haven't tested that.

I assumed that.

I hadn't thought about that case.

117

Those are useful statements.

They tell you where investigation is required.

If architectural review punishes uncertainty, people learn to hide it.

  • Answers become more confident.
  • Diagrams become cleaner.
  • Risk registers become greener.
  • The organisation becomes less informed.

Architects contribute to this more than they often realise. A review style built around catching mistakes teaches people to arrive with defensible answers rather than honest questions.

A healthier response is:

We don't know yet. Is that something we need to know before making this decision?

Sometimes the answer is no.

That is equally useful.

23.13 Praise Good Reasoning, Not Agreement

When someone changes your mind, make that visible.

That's a better constraint than the one I was using.

I hadn't considered that dependency.

That changes my view.

Your explanation of the failure case makes this option much more reasonable.

This does more than create a pleasant working environment.

It teaches what architecture values.

  • Careful reasoning.
  • Evidence.
  • Explicit trade-offs.
  • Changing position when the evidence changes.

If people receive approval only when they agree with the architect, they will learn to optimise for agreement.

If they receive recognition for improving the reasoning, they will improve the reasoning.

23.14 Give the Pen Away

Collaborative modelling is a particularly good place to practise this.

If you have been drawing for ten minutes, hand the marker to someone else.

118

Ask:

Can you show me where I've got this wrong?

Or:

Draw the bit I'm missing.

The drawing changes immediately.

People add boundaries you did not know existed.

They use different words.

They place two things close together that you assumed were unrelated.

They cross out something you thought was important.

The act of drawing externalises their mental model.

You now have something shared that everyone can challenge.

This is far more powerful than presenting a completed architecture diagram and asking for comments.

A finished diagram invites approval.

An unfinished sketch invites participation.

23.15 Leave Behind Questions

A useful architect gradually builds a vocabulary of questions within the teams they work with.

After enough conversations you begin hearing people ask one another:

What are we optimising for?

Where did the complexity go?

What would make this choice wrong?

Is that a real constraint or one we've inherited?

Which part of this is specific to us?

What happens when it fails?

How do we leave?

At that point architectural judgement has started spreading.

This is much more scalable than central architecture.

119

A small architecture function can influence a large organisation if good questions reproduce themselves.

A large architecture function can still become a bottleneck if every question has to return to it for an answer.

23.16 Give People Tools, Not Just Answers

Questions are one tool.

Others include simple decision records, trade-off tables, modelling conventions, architectural fitness tests and small shared vocabularies.

The aim is always the same: make good reasoning easier to reproduce.

A team deciding whether to buy or build should not need the original architect present if it has a useful way to examine strategic specificity, market maturity, dependency, internal capability and exit.

A team designing an integration should not need an architecture board to remind it to consider ownership, semantics, retries and failure.

A team evaluating a new technology should know to ask what problem it solves, what complexity it introduces, how it fails and how difficult it will be to remove.

Architecture becomes organisational capability when its methods survive the architect.

23.17 Do Not Create Learned Helplessness

There is a subtle danger in highly centralised architecture.

Teams gradually stop making architectural decisions.

Why bother?

  • Architecture will review it.
  • Architecture will tell us which pattern to use.
  • Architecture owns the standards.
  • Architecture decides whether exceptions are allowed.

Over time, the teams most familiar with the systems become less confident exercising judgement about them.

The central architecture function then observes inconsistent decision-making and concludes that even more governance is required.

The dependency reinforces itself.

Good architects push decisions outward whenever the consequences are sufficiently local.

They provide principles, evidence and support.

They intervene where decisions cross important boundaries or create long-lived organisational consequences.

The goal is not architectural consistency at all costs.

120

It is widespread architectural competence.

23.18 Know When to Give the Answer

There are moments when teaching through questions is inappropriate.

An incident is unfolding.

A security vulnerability needs immediate remediation.

A regulatory requirement is explicit.

A junior engineer is stuck on something well understood.

A decision has already been investigated thoroughly and delay now costs more than further learning provides.

Give the answer.

Architecture should not become pedagogical theatre.

The objective is not to force every person to independently rediscover decades of engineering knowledge.

Expertise exists precisely so that we can borrow it.

The distinction is between transferring knowledge and creating dependency.

Sometimes the best thing you can say is:

We've seen this problem before. Use this pattern. I'll explain why afterwards.

That is still making people smarter.

23.19 Teach the Reason, Preserve the Freedom

Standards are another opportunity.

A weak standard says:

Services must communicate using asynchronous messaging.

A useful standard says:

Where work does not require an immediate response, prefer asynchronous communication because it reduces temporal coupling between independently operated services. Synchronous communication remains appropriate where the caller genuinely requires the result before it can continue.

Now somebody can understand when the rule applies.

More importantly, they can recognise when it does not.

Rules without reasoning create compliance.

Rules with reasoning create judgement.

The second scales much better.

23.20 Work Yourself Out of the Conversation

There is a useful test for architectural effectiveness.

Return to a team you worked with six months ago.

  • Are they asking better questions?
  • Are their decision records clearer?
  • Do they expose trade-offs before review?
  • Are they identifying architectural concerns without being prompted?
  • Can they disagree with you using the same reasoning tools you helped establish?

If so, architecture worked.

If they still need you to repeat the same advice, perhaps the intervention solved the immediate design but did not improve the organisation.

Architecture should leave residue.

Not more committees or documents.

  • Judgement.
  • Vocabulary.
  • Questions.
  • Confidence.

The ability to recognise a familiar problem in an unfamiliar form.

23.21 The Architect Is Not the Smartest Person in the Room

They should not need to be.

The domain specialist knows things about the organisation you will never know.

The engineer working on the service understands details you will not hold in your head.

The security specialist sees threats you will miss.

The finance colleague understands consequences you did not realise existed.

The architect's advantage is different.

You spend a disproportionate amount of time looking across boundaries, comparing patterns, following consequences and asking how today's local decision becomes tomorrow's organisational constraint.

Use that perspective to connect other people's intelligence rather than compete with it.

A good architectural conversation should not end with everyone impressed by the architect.

It should end with the room understanding the problem better than it did before.

Eventually somebody else should ask the question you were about to ask.

That is not a loss of relevance.

That is the work succeeding.

Question-led architecture, teaching without lecturing, and creating independent judgement.

Keep Your Hands in the Work

Architecture creates distance from implementation. Some distance is necessary. An architect cannot spend every day buried in one repository and still maintain a useful view across systems, teams and organisational boundaries. The problem begins when that distance becomes permanent.

It's surprisingly easy for an architect to remain conversant in technology while becoming progressively less technical. You still know which databases are fashionable, which cloud services exist, which vendors occupy which parts of the market and which architectural patterns are currently considered modern. You can compare Snowflake with Databricks, Kubernetes with ECS, Kafka with managed queues, or the latest generation of AI platforms. You can speak confidently about event-driven architecture, semantic layers, lakehouses and platform engineering.

None of that necessarily means you understand how any of them work.

There's a difference between being familiar with the technology landscape and possessing technical depth. Architecture needs both.

24.1 Knowing the Market isn't the Same as Knowing the Technology

An architect should understand what can be bought.

Commercial products, managed services and open-source projects all change what is sensible to build internally. Ignoring the market leads to needless reinvention. A modern architect should know broadly what capability exists, where mature products are available and where the market is still immature.

But product knowledge is shallow knowledge by default.

24
124

A vendor can tell you that a platform provides governance, lineage, streaming, semantic modelling or AI-assisted analytics. A product matrix can tell you which boxes contain ticks. Neither tells you how those capabilities behave under pressure, where their abstractions leak, how difficult they are to integrate or which assumptions they impose on the architecture around them.

You learn those things by getting closer.

  • Install the database.
  • Run the query.
  • Build the pipeline.
  • Break the consumer.
  • Read the execution plan.
  • Look at the generated files.
  • Inspect the protocol.
  • Follow the authentication flow.
  • Try the migration.
  • Read the source code when something behaves strangely.

Until you have done some of that work, much of what you know about the technology is still somebody else's description of it.

24.2 Practise

Technical judgement is perishable.

You don't maintain it by remembering what you knew ten years ago. Languages change. Database engines acquire new execution models. Infrastructure becomes programmable in different ways. Distributed systems that once required specialist teams become managed services. Things that were impractical five years ago become ordinary, while approaches that were once considered best practice become historical curiosities.

An architect needs some form of continuing technical practice.

That doesn't mean taking ownership of production services or spending half the week clearing engineering tickets. It means continuing to make things often enough that the resistance of the material remains familiar.

  • Write some code.
  • Build a small service.
  • Stand up a cluster.
  • Create an event stream.
  • Model a domain.
  • Write an infrastructure definition.
  • Push a few million rows through a new data engine.
  • Try the API everyone is proposing.
  • Build the smallest possible version of the architecture being discussed.

There's a quality of understanding that only appears when you actually attempt something.

125

A diagram might make a technology look interchangeable with another. Twenty minutes of implementation may expose a completely different programming model.

A product may describe itself as open. Trying to extract its configuration may tell you what that claim really means.

An API may appear simple until you discover pagination, throttling, historical updates and eventual consistency.

Practice turns architectural vocabulary back into engineering knowledge.

24.3 Go Deep Sometimes

Breadth is one of architecture's great advantages.

You encounter databases, security, networks, application design, data modelling, integration, infrastructure, operating systems, cloud services, commercial products and organisational processes. Over time you develop enough familiarity to recognise patterns across all of them.

Breadth without depth, however, becomes superficial very quickly.

An architect should periodically go deep.

Pick a technology relevant to the work and learn it substantially beyond the level required to describe it in a presentation. Understand its execution model. Learn how it stores data. Understand its consistency guarantees. Learn where state lives. Find out what happens during failure. Read its documentation beyond the getting-started guide. Understand what its operators complain about.

You don't need that depth everywhere.

You need enough experience of depth to recognise what genuine understanding feels like.

That matters enormously when evaluating other people's claims. Once you have learned a few systems deeply, you become much less impressed by feature names. You know that "supports transactions", "scales horizontally", "event driven", "serverless" or "AI powered" each conceal an enormous number of consequential details.

Depth gives you resistance to architecture by brochure.

24.4 Learn Mechanisms, Not Brands

Technologies change faster than the ideas underneath them.

Someone who learned "Oracle" may eventually become dated.

Someone who learned transactions, query planning, indexing, concurrency control, replication and storage engines can move between Oracle, Postgres, SQL Server, DuckDB and whatever comes next.

The same applies elsewhere.

Learn message delivery, ordering, replay and backpressure rather than simply learning Kafka.

126

Learn scheduling, isolation, service discovery and resource allocation rather than merely learning Kubernetes.

Learn authentication, authorisation, delegation and trust boundaries rather than memorising one identity platform.

Learn columnar storage, predicate pushdown, partitioning and vectorised execution rather than attaching your understanding to a particular lakehouse product.

Learn graph traversal, identity and relationship semantics rather than associating graph architecture with a particular database vendor.

Products are implementations of mechanisms.

Architects who understand mechanisms can evaluate unfamiliar products quickly because they have something stable to compare them against.

Architects who know only products are repeatedly reset when the market changes.

24.5 Build Enough to Understand the Abstraction

Modern technology is composed almost entirely of abstractions.

That is a good thing. Very few organisations should implement their own schedulers, storage engines, encryption libraries or distributed consensus algorithms.

But abstraction creates a particular architectural danger: people begin making consequential decisions about mechanisms they have never encountered beneath the abstraction.

You don't need to implement Raft to use a distributed database.

It's useful to understand why a network partition creates difficult choices.

You don't need to build a database engine.

It's useful to understand why indexes accelerate one access pattern while making another more expensive.

You don't need to write a container runtime.

It's useful to understand what a process, namespace, filesystem and network boundary actually are.

Every now and then, go one level lower than your normal work requires.

That depth pays for itself later because abstractions stop appearing magical.

And architecture should be deeply suspicious of magic.

24.6 Read Source Code

Open source has given modern architects an extraordinary opportunity that previous generations rarely had.

Much of the infrastructure on which organisations depend can simply be inspected.

When documentation is ambiguous, look.

When a system behaves unexpectedly, search the implementation.

127

When evaluating an open-source project, examine how it is structured, how frequently it changes, how errors are handled, what dependencies it carries and how maintainers discuss difficult design decisions.

You don't need to understand the entire codebase.

Even small amounts of source reading improve your sense of what the software really is.

A product described as a lightweight abstraction may turn out to contain an enormous machinery of adapters and edge cases.

A supposedly complicated system may reveal a surprisingly simple internal model.

Architecture is partly the ability to see through the marketing category into the engineering underneath.

Source code is unusually honest in that regard.

24.7 Build with New Technologies Before Recommending Them

Architectural enthusiasm should come after experimentation, not before it.

When a new database, framework, AI tool or platform appears attractive, use it for something small.

Not a proof-of-concept designed to prove that it works.

Something deliberately intended to expose its shape.

  • Try a real data model.
  • Use awkward data.
  • Run an upgrade.
  • Change the schema.
  • Introduce failure.
  • Integrate it with something that doesn't naturally fit.
  • See what the operational experience looks like.

A technology often reveals its philosophy through the things that feel easy and the things that feel awkward.

That philosophy matters because adopting a technology means accepting some of its worldview.

A good architect should know enough of that worldview before asking an organisation to live inside it.

24.8 Learn Technologies You Don't Expect to Use

Staying current doesn't only mean becoming better at the existing estate.

Periodically learn something outside it.

If your organisation uses relational databases, spend some time with graph databases.

If your systems are request-response heavy, build something with a log or event stream.

128

If you mostly use Python, write something substantial in Rust or Go.

If everything is cloud hosted, understand what modern local-first or embedded systems can do.

If your organisation buys large analytics platforms, spend time with DuckDB, Polars, DataFusion or other lower-level components and see how much capability is available without the platform.

This isn't technology tourism.

It expands the set of architectures you can imagine.

One of the dangers of working inside a mature organisation is that the existing estate quietly defines what appears possible. People solve problems using the tools already approved because those are the tools they know.

Experimentation reminds you that the current architecture is only one point in a much larger design space.

24.9 Understand What You Are Buying

Technical depth becomes particularly important when evaluating commercial products.

A commercial platform may contain excellent engineering. It may also package ordinary technical capabilities behind proprietary interfaces and charge substantially for convenience, integration and support.

Without technical understanding, it is difficult to distinguish between the two.

If you understand how object storage, Parquet, distributed compute and query engines work, you can evaluate what a commercial data platform is genuinely adding.

If you understand identity protocols, policy evaluation and directory services, you can distinguish commodity IAM capability from product-specific packaging.

If you understand retrieval, embeddings, tool invocation and language models, you can evaluate whether an "enterprise AI platform" provides something technically distinctive or mainly assembles existing components.

This doesn't imply that you should build the equivalent yourself.

Convenience, operational support and integration are valuable.

It means you can price those things consciously.

A technically shallow organisation often pays enormous premiums because it cannot tell which parts of a product are difficult and which parts are merely unfamiliar.

24.10 Stay Capable of Building the Small Version

There's a useful threshold for architectural competence:

Could you build the small version?

Not the hyperscale version.

129

Not the regulated enterprise product.

Not the thing with twenty-four-hour support and five years of accumulated features.

The small version that demonstrates the mechanism.

  • Could you build a basic event consumer?
  • Could you create a small REST service?
  • Could you model a handful of concepts in RDF?
  • Could you implement authentication between two services?
  • Could you write a pipeline that turns raw files into partitioned Parquet?
  • Could you deploy something to a container platform?
  • Could you build the crude version of the semantic lookup service you are recommending?

If the answer is always no, the distance between architecture and engineering is becoming too large.

You may still recommend buying the capability.

In fact, after building the small version you may have considerably more respect for why buying it is sensible.

The point isn't independence from technology suppliers.

It's independence of judgement.

24.11 Learn Operations

A great deal of architecture is conceived during implementation and disproved during operation.

Architects should therefore understand what operating technology actually involves.

  • Deployments.
  • Backups.
  • Upgrades.
  • Monitoring.
  • Capacity.
  • Credentials.
  • Certificates.
  • Incidents.
  • Cost.
  • Support.
  • Data recovery.
  • Dependency updates.

Operational knowledge changes how you evaluate designs.

A system composed of twelve elegant services looks different after you have been responsible for twelve deployment pipelines, twelve sets of alerts, twelve dependency graphs and twelve failure modes.

130

A managed product looks different after you have experienced a vendor incident for which your only operational control was opening a ticket.

A database design looks different after performing a real migration against a dataset large enough that rolling back is no longer trivial.

Operation makes permanence visible.

Architects who never operate anything can accidentally design as though implementation is the expensive part and ownership is free.

24.12 Learn Performance by Measuring It

Technical intuition is useful.

It's also frequently wrong.

Modern hardware and software are sufficiently complicated that performance should increasingly be learned by experiment.

  • Run benchmarks.
  • Read query plans.
  • Measure serialisation.
  • Look at network behaviour.
  • Watch memory consumption.
  • Compare file layouts.
  • Measure cold starts.
  • Test concurrency.
  • Use realistic volumes occasionally.

This doesn't require becoming a performance engineer.

It prevents architecture from being built around folklore.

Claims such as:

  • relational databases cannot scale,
  • Python is too slow,
  • JSON is inefficient,
  • serverless will be cheaper,
  • graph databases are faster for relationships,
  • this needs Spark,
  • are not architectural facts.

They are hypotheses about particular workloads.

Technical practice teaches architects to recognise the difference.

24.13 Learn Enough Mathematics and Computer Science to See Through Fashion

A surprising amount of supposedly new technology is an old idea appearing under a new product category.

131

Distributed systems continue dealing with coordination, state, failure and partial knowledge.

Databases continue dealing with storage, indexing, consistency and query execution.

Data platforms continue dealing with representation, movement, transformation and computation.

AI systems still consume compute, memory, data and probability.

Architects benefit enormously from learning some of the foundations beneath these technologies.

  • Algorithms.
  • Probability.
  • Distributed systems.
  • Database theory.
  • Networking.
  • Operating systems.
  • Programming language concepts.
  • Security models.
  • Information theory where relevant.

Not because architecture should become academic.

Because fundamentals decay much more slowly than products do.

The better you understand the foundations, the easier it becomes to recognise when a supposedly revolutionary technology is merely moving an old trade-off somewhere new.

24.14 Maintain More Than One Technical Home

It's useful to have areas where your technical depth is particularly strong.

It's dangerous to have only one.

Someone whose entire career has centred on enterprise Java applications may naturally interpret architecture through application platforms.

A career database specialist may see information modelling as the centre of every system.

A cloud infrastructure specialist may treat deployment architecture as architecture itself.

Broaden deliberately.

An architect might maintain serious competence in two or three different areas: perhaps software development, data systems and infrastructure; or security, networking and distributed systems.

The exact combination matters less than having enough different perspectives that none becomes your entire model of technology.

Architecture is often about recognising the boundaries between disciplines.

You have to know enough of both sides to see the boundary.

24.15 Don't Confuse Seniority with Distance

There's a persistent career model in technology where progression means leaving implementation behind.

  • Engineer.
  • Senior engineer.
  • Lead.
  • Architect.
  • Manager.

Eventually the person who once understood systems deeply spends their days producing documents about systems built by other people.

That progression isn't inevitable.

Seniority can mean increasing the range across which technical knowledge is applied rather than abandoning technical practice.

  • You may write less production code.
  • You may operate fewer services.
  • You may spend much more time on organisational questions.

But some portion of your professional life should still involve direct technical learning and experimentation.

Otherwise your expertise slowly becomes historical.

You remain confident because you once knew deeply.

Technology doesn't care.

24.16 Make Learning Part of the Job

Staying technically current cannot depend entirely on evenings and weekends.

An organisation that expects architects to understand modern technology should expect them to spend time learning it.

That might mean regularly experimenting with emerging tools, attending engineering reviews, reading technical papers, building prototypes, pairing with teams or taking responsibility for small exploratory pieces of work.

This should not be treated as time away from architecture.

It's architecture.

A profession that depends on judgement has to continually renew the experience from which that judgement is formed.

A surgeon who stopped learning new surgical techniques twenty years ago would not be considered more strategic because they now only attended planning meetings.

Technology should not accept a lower standard.

24.17 Know When Your Knowledge Is Old

Experience is valuable.

Experience also creates traps.

Something genuinely may have been a terrible idea fifteen years ago and become perfectly reasonable today.

  • Networks changed.
  • Languages changed.
  • Compilers changed.
  • Cloud economics changed.
  • Open-source ecosystems matured.
  • Products absorbed enormous amounts of complexity.

An architect has to periodically revisit conclusions formed earlier in their career.

"Never build distributed systems unless absolutely necessary."

"Open source isn't suitable for enterprise use."

"Build is more expensive than buy."

"Relational databases cannot handle this."

"Python can't be used for serious systems."

These kinds of beliefs often contain experience, but experience has a timestamp.

24.18 Stay Curious About the Small Things

Technical relevance is maintained as much through curiosity as discipline.

24.19 Technical Depth Protects Independence

There's a wider organisational reason for all of this.

An organisation whose architects understand only commercial offerings becomes dependent on the people selling those offerings to explain what technology can do.

  • The consultancy defines what good looks like.
  • The analyst report defines the shortlist.
  • The market defines what architecture appears possible.

That independence is extremely valuable.

It's one of the reasons organisations need technically capable architects rather than technology procurement specialists with architecture titles.

30.2 Time poverty

Time poverty is what happens when an organisation consumes so much of people's available attention that there is no room left to think.

It is easy to confuse this with being busy. Busy organisations can still have slack, reflection and space to improve. Time-poor organisations cannot. Every hour is already committed to delivery, meetings, reporting, operational support, planning, governance or the next programme of change. People may be working hard and producing a great deal, while simultaneously losing the capacity to question whether the work should exist at all.

This matters because improvement itself requires time.

You need time to understand why a system keeps failing rather than repairing it again. Time to automate the repetitive task. Time to simplify a process. Time to learn a technology properly. Time to sit with another team and understand their problem. Time to remove something that has outlived its usefulness. Time to write down what everybody currently carries in their heads.

When all available capacity is consumed by today's work, tomorrow's work becomes progressively harder.

Utilisation Is Not Productivity

Organisations often treat unused capacity as inefficiency.

30
143

A team operating at seventy percent apparent utilisation looks as though thirty percent of its capacity is being wasted. Someone eventually asks whether another project could be added, another service absorbed or the team reduced.

This works tolerably well for some forms of repetitive work.

It works badly for knowledge work.

Engineering, architecture, analysis and design contain uncertainty. Problems arrive unevenly. Some turn out to be trivial. Others consume several days because the thing that looked simple exposes a dependency nobody knew existed. Incidents appear unexpectedly. Someone needs help. A supplier changes an API. A new requirement invalidates an assumption.

When every person's time has already been allocated, there is nowhere for this variability to go.

  • Work waits.
  • People multitask.
  • Small problems interrupt large ones.
  • Teams begin scheduling conversations days or weeks into the future because nobody has an hour available today.

The organisation may still report very high utilisation.

It has simply converted spare capacity into queues.

Slack Is Capacity to Adapt

Slack has acquired an unfortunate reputation in management.

It sounds like waste.

In adaptive organisations it is closer to reserve capacity.

A team with some uncommitted time can investigate an incident properly rather than patching it. It can absorb an unexpected priority without abandoning three other commitments. It can improve the systems it owns while continuing to operate them. It can help another team without requiring a programme to fund the interaction.

Slack is also where learning happens.

An engineer experimenting with an unfamiliar database may discover an approach useful six months later. An analyst investigating an inconsistency may uncover a definition that has diverged across the organisation. Someone cleaning up an awkward deployment may remove a source of failure that has quietly consumed hundreds of hours over several years.

These activities are difficult to justify individually against an immediate delivery target.

Collectively they are how capability grows.

An organisation that removes all slack does not merely become busier. It gradually loses the ability to improve itself.

Improvement Work Is Usually the First Thing to Lose

When demand exceeds capacity, organisations prioritise visible obligations.

144
  • The regulatory report still has to be produced.
  • The application still has to remain available.
  • The programme milestone still has a date.
  • The executive request still arrives marked urgent.

The work that gets deferred is usually the work whose consequences are less immediate.

  • Refactoring.
  • Documentation.
  • Automation.
  • Removing obsolete systems.
  • Improving observability.
  • Learning.
  • Reducing duplicated processes.
  • Cleaning data.
  • Understanding why the workaround exists.

None of these failures is dramatic enough to win against today's deadline.

So they move to next quarter.

Then next year.

Eventually the workaround has become the architecture.

This is one of the mechanisms through which organisational accretion happens. People usually know what should be improved. They simply never receive enough uninterrupted time to improve it.

Time Poverty Creates Short-Term Thinking

When people are chronically short of time, the horizon of their decisions contracts.

The useful question becomes:

What gets me through this week?

rather than:

What leaves this easier next year?

A manual export beats an automated interface because it can be done today.

A spreadsheet beats fixing the source system.

Adding another approval beats resolving the uncertainty that caused the approval to be requested.

Copying the data beats understanding why the two systems disagree.

Buying another product beats investigating whether an existing capability can be extended.

Each decision may be entirely rational within the time available.

The accumulated architecture may be terrible.

145

This is why telling teams to "think strategically" accomplishes very little when their immediate environment gives them no capacity to do so. Long-term thinking is not merely a mindset. It requires enough time to consider consequences that are not yet urgent.

Fragmented Time Is Often Worse Than No Time

Not all available hours are equally useful.

Three free half-hours scattered across a day are not equivalent to ninety uninterrupted minutes.

Some work requires a mental model to be assembled before useful progress begins. Architecture, software design, analysis, writing and debugging all have this characteristic. People need time to reconstruct where they were, remember the constraints and hold several interacting ideas in mind at once.

Constant interruption destroys that state.

A calendar can therefore contain apparent free time while offering almost no usable thinking time.

This is particularly common for experienced people.

As someone becomes more knowledgeable, more teams ask for their help. They acquire more recurring meetings. They become the person invited to reviews, incidents and planning sessions. Their expertise makes them increasingly valuable, and the organisation responds by fragmenting their attention until they can barely use that expertise for sustained work.

Eventually the most experienced people become coordination mechanisms.

They spend their days discussing work rather than doing or deeply understanding it.

That should concern architecture.

Meetings Consume the Same Resource as Thinking

Meetings are not inherently wasteful.

Complex organisations require coordination.

The problem is that meeting time and thinking time are usually budgeted separately in people's heads even though they consume the same finite resource.

A one-hour architecture review is not merely one hour.

It may divide two blocks of concentrated work. It may require preparation. It may generate follow-up. For someone attending six meetings across a day, the remaining fragments may be practically unusable for difficult technical work.

Recurring meetings are particularly dangerous because their cost becomes invisible.

A weekly one-hour meeting involving twelve people sounds small.

It consumes more than six person-weeks over a year before preparation or follow-up is considered.

The organisation should occasionally ask of recurring coordination:

What happens if we stop doing this?

146

If nobody knows, stop once and find out.

Useful governance should continuously justify the time it consumes.

Time Poverty Changes Behaviour

People adapt to persistent shortage.

  • They stop reading long documents.
  • They skim.
  • They answer whichever messages look most urgent.
  • They avoid asking questions likely to create additional work.
  • They accept plausible answers because investigating them would take too long.
  • They stop offering help outside their immediate responsibilities.
  • They stop attending optional conversations where useful knowledge once travelled informally.
  • They become reluctant to experiment because failed experiments consume time they do not possess.

Eventually they may stop raising structural problems because experience has taught them that nobody has capacity to address them.

This is not laziness or disengagement.

It's adaptation to scarcity.

The organisation then observes these behaviours and may conclude that people need greater accountability, clearer objectives or more management attention.

Each response consumes a little more time.

Urgency Becomes Self-Reinforcing

Time-poor organisations are often dominated by urgency.

Everything is important because everything has been deferred until it became important.

  • A neglected maintenance problem becomes an incident.
  • An undocumented process becomes urgent when its owner leaves.
  • A delayed procurement becomes critical because a contract is expiring.
  • An integration that should have been replaced becomes a blocker for another programme.

The resulting emergency consumes the time that might otherwise have prevented the next emergency.

This creates a reinforcing cycle:

  • shortage of time produces deferred maintenance;
  • deferred maintenance produces incidents and workarounds;
  • incidents consume more time;
  • less time remains for improvement;
  • more work is deferred.
147

Eventually firefighting becomes part of organisational identity.

People become valued for rescuing difficult situations.

Quietly preventing those situations receives much less recognition because nothing visibly happened.

A healthy culture has to reverse that incentive.

Transformation Can Make Time Poverty Worse

Transformation programmes frequently claim to create future efficiency while consuming substantial present capacity.

The organisation must continue operating while also designing, testing and adopting its replacement.

The same experienced people are often required for both.

  • They maintain the old system because they understand it.
  • They support the programme because they understand the domain.
  • They test the new system because they know how the old one behaves.
  • They help colleagues migrate because they understand the differences.
  • They then inherit whatever remains unfinished when the programme closes.

From the programme's perspective, this may appear as stakeholder engagement.

From the individual's perspective, it is another workload layered over the job they already had.

This is one reason transformations can leave organisations less capable even when they deliver their stated outputs. The programme consumes the slack through which existing teams learned, improved and maintained their systems.

Once that capacity is gone, ordinary work begins accumulating debt.

Time Is an Architectural Resource

Architecture usually models compute, storage, network capacity, licensing and money.

It should also model human time.

  • Every dependency consumes coordination.
  • Every hand-off consumes attention.
  • Every approval consumes waiting.
  • Every duplicated representation consumes reconciliation.
  • Every bespoke integration consumes maintenance.
  • Every additional system consumes some amount of learning, administration, security review, patching and support.

Individually these costs often appear negligible.

Across an estate they can consume an enormous proportion of the organisation's available capacity.

148

This gives architecture another useful question:

How much human attention does this design require to remain true?

A system requiring constant reconciliation is expensive even if its infrastructure bill is tiny.

A governance process requiring six specialists to meet every week has an operating cost.

A platform that requires every team to learn a proprietary model consumes organisational learning capacity.

Human attention is finite.

Architectures compete for it.

Protect Deep Work Explicitly

If uninterrupted time is important, leaving it to emerge accidentally is unlikely to work.

Organisations can deliberately protect it.

Not every team needs the same arrangement. A support function and a research engineering team operate under different kinds of demand. The principle is simply to recognise concentrated attention as something worth scheduling and defending.

  • Reduce recurring meetings.
  • Batch routine requests.
  • Create predictable windows for consultation.
  • Avoid spreading people across too many initiatives.
  • Allow teams to finish work before assigning more.
  • Give specialists some days where they are not expected to be continuously reachable.

Do not interpret an empty calendar as unused capacity waiting to be filled.

Perhaps most importantly, leaders need to model this behaviour themselves. A culture cannot claim to value thoughtful work while senior staff continually manufacture urgency and expect immediate responses.

Limit Work in Progress

Many organisations attempt to solve insufficient capacity by starting more things.

The logic is understandable. Several priorities matter, so progress should begin on all of them.

The result is usually slower progress on everything.

People divide attention between initiatives. Dependencies multiply. Meetings increase because more work must be coordinated. Every project has periods where it waits for somebody assigned simultaneously to several others.

Reducing work in progress can feel uncomfortable because some important work remains visibly unstarted.

That visibility is useful.

149

It exposes the actual constraint.

Starting everything hides scarcity inside queues and partially completed work.

A culture that accepts limited capacity can make explicit choices about what matters most.

A culture unwilling to make those choices distributes scarcity across everyone.

Make Space for Maintenance

Sustainment cannot be something teams perform after delivery work is finished.

Delivery work is never finished.

Capacity for maintenance, learning and improvement must therefore be treated as part of operating the capability.

A team responsible for a system should reasonably spend some of its time making that system easier to own.

That may involve removing code rather than adding it.

  • Replacing a workaround.
  • Improving testing.
  • Reducing alerts.
  • Updating documentation.
  • Eliminating a manual step.
  • Upgrading a dependency before the upgrade becomes an emergency.

None of this should require constructing an artificial transformation programme merely to obtain permission to maintain the thing responsibly.

An organisation that funds construction but not maintenance eventually spends most of its money reconstructing things it failed to maintain.

Give People Enough Time to Care

There is also a cultural consequence to relentless time pressure.

Quality requires care.

Not perfectionism.

Care.

  • Enough time to notice that a name is misleading.
  • Enough time to make the interface understandable.
  • Enough time to write the error message properly.
  • Enough time to ask why the data does not reconcile.
  • Enough time to leave something slightly better for the next person.

When every task is performed under immediate pressure, those small acts disappear.

People still deliver.

They simply stop tending.

150

Over years, the absence of tending becomes visible everywhere: confusing systems, brittle integrations, abandoned documentation, duplicated concepts and processes everybody dislikes but nobody has time to change.

Culture is partly what people do when nobody explicitly asks them to do it.

If there is never any time available for those discretionary acts, the organisation should not be surprised when its culture becomes transactional.

Do Not Fill Every Gap

When a team becomes more efficient, organisations often immediately reclaim the time.

  • Automation saves half a day each week.
  • Another responsibility is assigned.
  • A process is simplified.
  • The team receives another project.
  • A vacancy is removed because output remained stable.

This makes individual optimisation rational while making organisational improvement strangely unrewarding.

  • Why automate your work if successful automation merely earns you more work?
  • Why simplify a process if the recovered capacity disappears immediately?

Some efficiency gains should remain with the people who created them.

That time becomes the capacity through which the next improvement happens.

Without it, continuous improvement becomes an extraction mechanism: every improvement increases utilisation until there is no room left to improve.

Leaders Create Time

One of the most valuable things leadership can provide is not direction.

It is space.

  • Removing a project.
  • Cancelling a meeting.
  • Narrowing a priority.
  • Stopping a report nobody reads.
  • Protecting a team from another "small" request.

Accepting that something worthwhile will take longer because doing it properly will reduce work later.

These decisions rarely feel as visible as launching something new.

They may create more organisational capability than another initiative ever could.

Leadership has considerable power over how fragmented an organisation becomes. Every priority communicated without removing another priority pushes the cost downward. People resolve the contradiction by working longer, lowering quality, delaying hidden work or attempting several things simultaneously.

A priority without a corresponding decision about what will not be done is usually just additional demand.

Time Poverty Is a Choice

There will always be periods of genuine pressure.

Deadlines exist.

Incidents happen.

Unexpected events require extraordinary effort.

A resilient organisation can absorb these periods partly because they are exceptional.

The problem is when exceptional pressure becomes the ordinary operating model.

At that point time poverty is no longer something happening to the organisation.

It is something the organisation is choosing through the amount of work it starts, the coordination it demands, the capacity it removes and the attention it continually fragments.

A culture of sustainment treats human attention as a scarce resource.

It leaves enough space for people to understand the systems they operate, improve them, learn new things and respond intelligently when circumstances change.

That space can look inefficient when viewed through utilisation.

It looks very different when viewed through adaptability.

An organisation with no spare time has no spare capacity to change.

And an organisation with no capacity to change is already borrowing from its future.

Borrowing complexity

Organisations often describe buying technology as a way of avoiding complexity.

A supplier has already built the product, employed the specialists, solved the difficult technical problems and spread the cost across many customers. The organisation can purchase the result rather than reproduce the effort. In the right circumstances, this is exactly what it should do.

The mistake is assuming that complexity has disappeared.

It has usually moved.

The product contains decisions the organisation did not make, dependencies it does not control and assumptions formed in other organisations. The supplier carries much of the complexity involved in creating and operating it, but the buyer inherits the complexity of fitting it into its own work, governing the relationship, understanding the limits and responding when the product changes direction.

Buying technology is therefore better understood as borrowing complexity. The organisation avoids owning one kind of complexity by accepting another. Good architecture does not insist that everything be built internally. It decides deliberately which complexity the organisation should own, which it should borrow and where the boundary between them must remain visible.

31.1 Complexity doesn't disappear
31
153

A payroll platform may save an organisation from implementing tax rules, statutory reporting, pension calculations and payment processing. That is a sensible use of an external product. The complexity has not vanished, however. The organisation still needs to understand who counts as an employee, which forms of compensation exist, how organisational changes affect payroll and what must happen when data arrives late or incorrectly.

The supplier can calculate payroll. It cannot decide what employment means within the organisation.

The distinction is easy to miss because products arrive with confident language. They offer standard processes, predefined entities, established practices and integrated workflows. The organisation appears to be purchasing a completed capability. In reality, it is purchasing a particular interpretation of that capability and accepting the work required to reconcile that interpretation with its own.

This may still be much cheaper and safer than building everything internally. The purpose of architecture is not to prevent the purchase. It is to identify what has been transferred, what remains and what new dependency has been created.

Every buy decision should therefore begin with a more honest question:

Which complexity are we choosing not to own, and which complexity are we accepting instead?

31.2 The make-or-buy decision is older than technology

The boundary between what an organisation produces internally and what it obtains from the market is one of the oldest questions in organisational economics.

Ronald Coase argued that markets are not free coordination mechanisms. Discovering suppliers, negotiating contracts, specifying requirements, monitoring delivery and adapting agreements all carry costs. Firms exist partly because, under some conditions, coordinating activity internally costs less than arranging every exchange through the market. The question is not whether markets or internal organisations are inherently superior, but which arrangement is better suited to the particular activity.

Later work developed this into transaction-cost economics. Activities become harder to purchase cleanly when they require highly organisation-specific knowledge or investment, when needs are uncertain, when performance is difficult to measure, or when contracts must continually adapt to changing circumstances. Markets often have advantages of scale and specialisation; internal organisations may have advantages where close coordination and adaptation matter more.

Research specifically examining information services reached a similarly untidy conclusion. Poppo and Zenger studied nine information services across 152 companies and found that make-or-buy choices could not be explained by production cost alone. Transaction costs, organisation-specific knowledge and the difficulty of measuring performance all mattered. They also warned that make and buy are not always clean opposites, but points within a wider range of contractual and organisational arrangements.

The practical lesson is that price comparison is only a small part of the decision. The organisation is choosing where knowledge, authority, adaptation and dependency will live.

31.3 Buying and building aren't opposing forces
154

Technology discussions often turn make-or-buy into a question of ideology.

One side argues that organisations should purchase established products rather than waste effort rebuilding solved problems. The other argues that purchased technology creates lock-in, poor fit and dependence on suppliers. Both positions contain truth, and both become dangerous when applied universally.

Building internally does not guarantee independence. An internal platform can become just as rigid, obscure and unresponsive as a commercial product. Its maintainers may become the only people able to change it. Other teams may have no meaningful influence over its direction. The organisation may carry all the costs of a vendor relationship without the discipline of a contract or the option of choosing another provider.

Buying does not necessarily mean surrendering control. A well-bounded product with stable interfaces, portable data and several viable alternatives may reduce complexity without materially constraining the organisation. Open standards, modular architecture and a credible exit route can allow external capability to be used without allowing it to define the organisation around itself.

Most real arrangements are hybrid. An organisation might buy cloud infrastructure, databases, identity services and workflow components while building the models and services through which it expresses its own knowledge. It might purchase an enterprise platform but retain internal ownership of integration, data meaning and user experience. It might use consultants to accelerate delivery while ensuring permanent staff direct the architecture and acquire the knowledge needed to sustain it.

The important question is not simply whether something is bought or built.

It is where the boundary is placed, who controls it and what must cross it.

31.4 Buy commodity capability

Commodity capability is capability whose essential form is broadly shared across organisations.

Email delivery, document storage, commodity compute, certificate management and basic identity protocols are not usually where an organisation expresses its distinctive purpose. They may still be technically difficult, important and subject to rigorous requirements. Difficulty does not make something strategic.

Buying commodity capability allows the organisation to benefit from scale, specialist expertise and experience accumulated across many customers. Rebuilding these functions internally can consume scarce attention without improving the organisation’s ability to fulfil its mission.

The word commodity must be used carefully, however. A capability is not a commodity merely because several products claim to provide it. Nor is it universally a commodity across every organisation. Payment processing may be supporting infrastructure for a charity but central strategic capability for a financial-services provider. Geospatial analysis may be an occasional reporting tool in one organisation and the basis of another organisation’s operating model.

Commodity status is contextual.

155

A useful test is whether the organisation would become meaningfully less itself if the capability were replaced by another conforming implementation. Changing an email provider may be inconvenient, but it should not alter the organisation’s identity. Changing the way a research funder understands grants, applications, institutions and scientific relationships may alter the organisation profoundly.

Buy what can change without changing what the organisation is.

31.5 Build where meaning and judgement live

The strongest case for building internally is not technical novelty. It is organisational specificity.

Some capabilities encode the organisation’s particular language, history, judgement and relationships. They determine how it distinguishes one case from another, what it considers important and how it fulfils responsibilities that cannot be delegated cleanly.

A commercial workflow engine may be a sensible purchase. The definition of the stages, evidence and decisions within a specialist funding process may not be.

A graph database may be a commodity product. The ontology through which an organisation represents its concepts is not.

An AI model may be purchased as a service. The rules governing which conclusions the organisation is prepared to act upon remain its own responsibility.

This does not mean every line of software touching strategic meaning must be written internally. The underlying components can still be bought. The organisation must retain ownership of the model that makes them relevant.

That ownership requires more than holding copyright or possessing a repository. The organisation must have people who understand the capability, authority to change it and enough direct experience to judge whether an external component continues to serve it.

A capability is not truly owned when the organisation must ask a supplier what it means.

31.6 Asset specificity

One of the most useful concepts from transaction-cost economics is asset specificity.

An asset is specific when its value depends heavily on a particular relationship or organisational context. That asset may be a customised system, a specialist data model, an integration, a body of knowledge or the expertise of people who have learned how the organisation actually works.

A standard document-management service has relatively low specificity. A deeply customised platform containing years of organisational terminology, workflows and exceptions has much higher specificity. The more specific the capability becomes, the harder it is to replace the supplier, transfer the work or write a complete contract describing how it must behave.

Specificity creates dependency from both directions. The organisation depends upon the supplier’s knowledge of the implementation. The supplier may also become embedded in the organisation’s decisions and operations. Renewals are no longer ordinary purchasing choices because the cost of leaving includes rediscovering knowledge, rebuilding interfaces, migrating data and changing established work.

156

This is why initial price is such a poor measure of a buy decision. A product may be inexpensive to adopt and extremely expensive to leave. A bespoke internal service may cost more initially but preserve flexibility around a strategically important capability. Neither conclusion follows automatically. The relevant question is how specificity will change over time.

Before purchasing, ask:

Will using this product make our organisation-specific knowledge easier to preserve and move, or will that knowledge gradually become inseparable from the supplier?

31.7 You also borrow the vendor's model

Every significant product contains a model of the world.

An HR platform contains assumptions about workers, positions, organisations, contracts and reporting relationships. A CRM contains assumptions about customers, interactions, opportunities and ownership. A project-management product contains assumptions about work, priority, progress and accountability.

These assumptions are not necessarily wrong. They are the reason the product can be sold repeatedly. The vendor has created a general model that fits enough organisations well enough to sustain a market.

The danger begins when the general model is treated as more authoritative than the organisation’s reality.

Terms are redefined to match the product. Important distinctions are collapsed because no field exists for them. Processes are changed not because the new process is better, but because the software makes the previous one difficult. Exceptions migrate into notes, spreadsheets and conversations beyond the platform’s view.

The organisation then claims it has standardised.

What it has often done is move its real variation outside the official system.

This is not an argument for unlimited customisation. Products can reveal unnecessary local habits and force valuable simplification. Some differences genuinely should be removed. The decision must be based on understanding what the difference represents, not on the convenience of configuration.

A useful product challenges accidental variation.

A harmful product erases meaningful variation and calls the result best practice.

31.8 The interface determines how much you borrow

The safest place to buy is behind a clear boundary.

Modular systems allow components to change independently when their responsibilities and interfaces are sufficiently stable. Baldwin and Clark’s work on modularity showed how explicit design rules and interfaces create options: modules can be improved, substituted or developed separately without redesigning the whole system.

This principle applies to organisational sourcing as much as software design.

157

A purchased capability is easier to govern when the organisation controls the contract at its boundary:

the meaning of the information crossing it; the service levels required; the security and policy constraints; the behaviour expected when the service fails; the format in which data can be recovered; and the conditions under which another implementation can replace it.

The more internal details of the product leak into the rest of the organisation, the more complexity has been borrowed. Vendor-specific identifiers spread through downstream systems. Proprietary workflow states become organisational language. Internal reports query undocumented database structures. Teams build directly against features that no alternative supplier provides.

What began as a product becomes part of the organisation’s anatomy.

Good architecture creates an anti-corruption boundary around externally sourced capability. It translates between the product’s model and the organisation’s model, allowing each to change without silently redefining the other. That boundary carries a cost, but it is the cost of preserving choice.

An interface is not merely technical integration.

It is where the organisation decides which assumptions it is prepared to inherit.

31.9 Cheap adoption can produce expensive dependency

Buying decisions are often made at the moment of entry.

The organisation compares licence prices, implementation estimates, delivery dates and apparent feature coverage. The product that appears to satisfy the requirement with the least immediate effort becomes the rational choice.

Dependency develops later.

Data volumes grow. Workflows become embedded. Users learn the product. Integrations multiply. Specialist staff are recruited. Procurement negotiates enterprise pricing based on broader adoption. The cost of leaving increases with every successful expansion.

This is sometimes described as lock-in, but the term can be too simplistic. All useful capability creates some switching cost. Internal systems also accumulate specialised knowledge and dependencies. The concern is not that switching has a cost. It is that the organisation may not understand or govern that cost as it grows.

A healthy dependency is visible, proportionate and reversible at a cost the organisation has consciously accepted.

An unhealthy dependency is discovered only when the organisation attempts to change direction.

The National Audit Office has found that large public organisations frequently lack the internal digital and commercial capability needed to manage these relationships effectively. It argues that an intelligent client must understand the technology, product lifecycle, supply chain and commercial strategy even when technical delivery sits with a supplier. It has also found that organisations often fail to decide clearly which skills should remain internal and which should be sourced externally.

Outsourcing execution does not outsource responsibility.

158

The organisation still owns the consequences.

31.10 Retain the capability to buy well

An organisation cannot make intelligent buy decisions if it has already outsourced the knowledge needed to evaluate them.

This creates a common cycle. Internal expertise is reduced because a supplier now delivers the capability. When the contract must be renewed or replaced, the organisation lacks enough knowledge to challenge the incumbent, assess alternatives or specify what it requires. It therefore returns to the same supplier or hires another external adviser to explain the dependency created by the first decision.

The supplier may have performed perfectly well. The failure lies in assuming that buying the service removed the need to understand it.

The buyer must retain enough capability to:

understand the organisational need; evaluate product and supplier claims; define the architectural boundary; monitor performance and consequences; integrate the service coherently; make informed trade-offs; and leave when the relationship no longer serves the organisation.

This is sometimes called the intelligent-client function. It should not be interpreted as a thin contract-management layer. It requires real technical, commercial and domain knowledge. The NAO has linked weak intelligent-client capability to poor digital-change outcomes and has specifically warned against procurement processes in which technical specialists are involved too late or not at all.

A team does not need to be capable of rebuilding every purchased product. It does need to understand the capability well enough to recognise when the product is no longer a sensible way of obtaining it.

Without that knowledge, the organisation is not buying.

It is consenting.

31.11 Don't outsource the ability to change

A capability may be operationally ordinary and strategically important because of how frequently it must change.

This distinction matters. Organisations sometimes outsource a system because its present operation is well understood, then discover that every future organisational change requires negotiation with the supplier. New policies become change requests. New terminology becomes configuration work. New integrations become commercial discussions.

The system itself may be stable. The boundary around it becomes a toll gate through which change must pass.

Where a capability is expected to evolve continuously with the organisation, adaptation cost should carry as much weight as implementation cost. The best provider is not necessarily the one that can deliver today’s requirement most cheaply. It may be the arrangement that allows tomorrow’s requirement to be discovered and implemented without renegotiating the organisation’s freedom to act.

159

This is closely related to the idea of dynamic capability: an organisation’s ability to integrate, reconfigure and renew its resources as conditions change. A sourcing decision that produces an efficient current state while weakening that ability may optimise the organisation into immobility.

Do not merely ask whether a supplier can deliver the capability.

Ask whether the relationship preserves the organisation’s ability to change the capability.

31.12 Building also has a cost

The argument for internal capability can become romantic very quickly.

Internal teams are not automatically closer to users, more responsive or better at preserving organisational meaning. They can build products nobody asked for, invent proprietary frameworks, become attached to their own solutions and underinvest in documentation, security and support. A system may remain internal long after the organisation has ceased to possess the skills required to maintain it responsibly.

Building creates permanent obligations.

Someone must operate the service, respond to incidents, patch dependencies, maintain compliance, support users, evolve the design and eventually replace it. The decision to build is not a one-time development estimate. It is a commitment to sustain a capability for as long as the organisation depends upon it.

Internal work also carries opportunity cost. Engineers building a commodity messaging service are not improving a capability more closely related to the organisation’s purpose. Technical autonomy can become an excuse to spend scarce attention on interesting problems that mature products already solve adequately.

The discipline applied to purchasing must therefore also be applied to building:

Why must this capability be ours?

Which organisational knowledge does internal ownership protect?

Are we willing to sustain it, not merely create it?

What will we stop doing in order to own it well?

A weak reason to buy is that building sounds difficult.

A weak reason to build is that building sounds satisfying.

31.13 Buy the product, not the dependency

The best sourcing arrangements are often deliberately asymmetrical.

The organisation buys substantial external capability but retains control over a small number of critical things.

160

It may buy a data platform while retaining ownership of its conceptual model, transformation logic and data contracts. It may buy a customer-management system while keeping customer identity and relationship definitions outside the vendor’s proprietary structures. It may use a cloud provider extensively while ensuring that data, automation and service boundaries can be reconstructed elsewhere.

This does not eliminate migration cost. Portability is never free. It prevents the product from becoming the only place where the organisation can understand itself.

The pattern is:

Buy the machinery. Own the meaning.

Buy the scale. Own the decisions.

Buy the specialist operation. Own the architectural boundary.

The organisation does not need complete independence from every supplier. That would be prohibitively expensive and usually pointless. It needs independence in the places where losing choice would threaten its ability to fulfil its purpose.

31.14 Use more than one test

No single rule can settle make-or-buy decisions. “Buy commodity, build strategic” is useful, but strategic importance is not the only concern. A capability may be strategic but better obtained through a mature market. A commodity product may create disproportionate risk because of data sensitivity or integration depth.

31.15 Define the boundary before selecting the product
161

Many organisations select a product and only then discover the boundary it imposes.

The sequence should be reversed.

Before approaching the market, define what the organisation must continue to own. Identify the concepts, data, decisions, policies and interfaces that cannot be allowed to disappear into a supplier’s implementation. Establish what must remain portable and which forms of customisation would make replacement impractical.

This changes procurement from a feature comparison into an architectural decision.

The requirements should include more than what the product must do. They should describe:

  • what it must not redefine;
  • which information must remain recoverable;
  • which standards it must support;
  • how integrations will be insulated from product internals;
  • what knowledge must transfer to permanent staff;
  • how changes will be priced and governed;
  • and how the service will be exited.

These are not secondary contractual protections. They determine whether the organisation is buying a useful component or reorganising itself around a supplier.

31.16 Design the exit at the beginning

Exit is where the real shape of dependency becomes visible.

Ask what would need to happen if the supplier stopped operating, raised prices substantially, changed strategic direction, degraded the service or simply ceased to fit the organisation’s needs. Identify which data would be extracted, which interfaces would change, which knowledge would be missing and how long replacement would reasonably take.

Backsourcing research shows that organisations commonly bring outsourced work back inside to regain control, improve quality or reduce cost. A systematic review of reported cases found that doing so required not merely contract termination but competence building, organisational reconstruction, ownership transfer and vendor-relationship management.

That is the point. Exit is not a clause.

It is a capability.

An organisation that cannot operate during a supplier transition has no meaningful exit, regardless of what the contract says. An organisation that no longer understands the service cannot verify whether the transferred assets are complete. An organisation without permanent staff capable of receiving the work is not leaving a supplier; it is moving dependence from one supplier to another.

Designing exit does not indicate distrust. It allows the relationship to remain a choice.

31.17 Revisit the boundary

Make-or-buy decisions are not permanent truths.

162

Markets mature. Internal skills develop. Products change. Strategic priorities move. A capability that once required internal development may later become a well-served commodity. A product that once fitted neatly may accumulate enough organisational specificity that the boundary needs to be reconsidered.

The decision should therefore be reviewed through the life of the capability.

The review should ask whether:

the product remains interchangeable; organisation-specific logic is leaking into it; exit costs are rising; internal knowledge is weakening; supplier performance remains measurable; the market still provides credible alternatives; and the original reason for buying or building remains true.

This is particularly important after success. A product that delivers value tends to spread. Every new team and integration increases both its benefit and the cost of leaving it. Expansion should not happen automatically merely because an enterprise agreement makes the next adoption appear inexpensive.

The marginal licence may be cheap.

The marginal dependency may not be.

31.18 Borrow deliberately

There is no virtue in owning all complexity.

An organisation that insists on building everything will exhaust itself maintaining capabilities that others can provide better and more cheaply. It will reduce the attention available for the work that only it can do.

There is also no virtue in buying everything.

An organisation that outsources every difficult capability eventually loses the knowledge required to understand what it has bought, why it exists and how it should change. It becomes surrounded by products while growing less capable of acting without them.

Architecture should hold the boundary between these extremes.

It should identify commodity capability and use the market confidently. It should recognise organisation-specific meaning and retain ownership where dependency would constrain identity, judgement or adaptation. It should build interfaces that prevent purchased products from silently becoming the organisation’s model of itself. It should preserve enough internal capability to remain an informed buyer and design every important supplier relationship with a credible route out.

Buying is not the absence of architecture.

It is architecture conducted across an organisational boundary.

Every purchase borrows someone else’s complexity. The responsibility is to know exactly how much has been borrowed, where it will surface, and whether the organisation can return it when it no longer serves.

Humility

Architecture depends on models. We use them to reduce a difficult organisation into something that can be discussed, compared and built upon. A model names the things that appear important, draws boundaries around them and describes the relationships that seem to matter. Without that reduction, the organisation remains too large and too changeable to reason about as a whole.

The reduction is useful precisely because it leaves things out.

That is also where the danger begins.

32.1 Every model is incomplete

No model contains the whole organisation. An ontology does not capture reality; it records a particular understanding of reality at a particular time. It reflects the questions people were asking, the distinctions they considered important and the evidence available when the model was made.

This is not a defect that can be corrected with more detail. A complete model of an organisation would have to be as complicated as the organisation itself, and would stop being useful as a model. The work therefore involves choosing what to preserve and what to omit.

Those choices should be treated as provisional. A concept appears in the model because the organisation currently believes it matters enough to name. An attribute appears because it helps distinguish or describe that concept. A relationship appears because it has proved useful to make explicit. None of this establishes that the model has found the only correct account of the organisation.

It establishes that the model is adequate for the work currently being asked of it.

32
164
32.2 The map begins to replace the territory

The longer a model is used, the easier it becomes to forget that it was constructed.

Its categories enter reports, systems and everyday language. New work is designed to fit them. People become familiar with the boundaries the model draws and begin treating those boundaries as though they belonged to reality itself.

This is how an abstraction becomes an authority.

A customer describes a situation that does not fit the available categories. Instead of reconsidering the categories, the organisation asks the customer to choose the nearest one. A new kind of work appears, but because the system has no place for it, people force it into an existing concept. The resulting record may be internally valid while describing the situation badly.

The model has remained consistent. The organisation has become less accurate.

This failure is easy to misdiagnose as poor data quality, weak training or inconsistent process. Sometimes it is. At other times the data is difficult because reality has produced something the model was not designed to express.

Architecture needs a way to tell the difference.

32.3 Exceptions are evidence

When something does not fit the model, the first response should not be to make it fit.

The discrepancy may reveal a misunderstanding, an edge case, a new distinction or a change in the organisation itself. Treating it only as an error removes the evidence before anyone has had the chance to learn from it.

This does not mean every exception deserves a new concept. Models would become unusable if every unusual case immediately changed their structure. It means exceptions need somewhere to remain visible while the organisation decides what they signify.

A practical model should therefore record unresolved cases rather than hiding them. It should allow people to explain why the existing representation is inadequate, how often the problem occurs and what consequences follow from forcing the case into the current structure. Repeated exceptions can then be reviewed together. Some will prove to be misunderstandings. Others will show that the model no longer describes the work faithfully.

The important point is that the decision is made deliberately.

32.4 Build revision into the model

Humility cannot depend on architects remembering to remain humble. It needs to be supported by the way the architecture is managed.

Models should be versioned. Changes should record what was altered, why it was altered and which evidence justified the change. Earlier versions should remain available so that people can understand how the organisation's interpretation evolved rather than seeing only the latest result.

165

Concepts and relationships should also have clear ownership, but ownership should not imply permanence. The owner is responsible for maintaining the definition, listening for evidence that it no longer works and convening the people needed to revise it. They are not responsible for defending it against change.

A model should also distinguish between established meaning and provisional meaning. Some concepts are stable enough to support many systems. Others are still being explored. Treating both with the same degree of certainty encourages premature commitment and makes later correction unnecessarily expensive.

The architecture should make that uncertainty visible.

32.5 Separate validation from conformity

Validation is useful. Organisations need to know whether records contain the required information, whether values come from agreed vocabularies and whether relationships satisfy known constraints.

The difficulty begins when validation is used to force reality into the model rather than to test whether the representation is adequate.

A failed validation may mean the record is wrong. It may also mean that the model has encountered a legitimate case it cannot express. Those outcomes require different responses.

A useful validation process therefore retains context. It records which rule failed, what the person or system was trying to represent and whether similar failures have occurred before. This turns validation into a source of architectural feedback rather than a gate that merely rejects inconvenient data.

The purpose is not to weaken standards. It is to prevent standards from becoming insulated from evidence.

32.6 Keep meaning close to use

Models decay most quickly when they are maintained separately from the work they describe.

A glossary reviewed once a year will not notice that a term has changed meaning in daily practice. An ontology maintained only by specialists may remain elegant while the organisation develops workarounds around it. A reference model that nothing operational depends upon can become increasingly inaccurate without producing any visible failure.

Meaning should therefore be exercised through use. Reports, interfaces, permissions and integrations should refer back to the shared model wherever practical. When the model changes, the effects should become visible. When operational work no longer fits the model, that discrepancy should return as feedback rather than being absorbed silently in local logic.

This creates a useful tension. The model influences the systems, and the systems provide evidence about the model.

Neither is allowed to become unquestionable.

32.7 Review what the model excludes

Most architecture reviews ask whether a model is internally consistent. Fewer ask whether it still describes the organisation well.

166

Both questions matter, but they are not the same.

A regular model review should examine the places where reality has resisted representation. Look at recurring exceptions, local extensions, free-text fields used to bypass controlled values, mappings that require increasingly complicated transformations and concepts whose definitions need frequent caveats. These are often signs that the model is carrying more certainty than the organisation has earned.

The review should not begin by deciding how to eliminate those deviations. It should begin by asking what they are trying to say.

A useful set of questions is:

Missing distinctions or weak shared meaning Which mappings require the most exceptions? Misalignment between concept and implementation Which concepts are rarely used? Unnecessary abstraction or obsolete meaning Which definitions need repeated explanation? Ambiguity hidden behind familiar language What evidence would cause us to revise this model? Whether revision is genuinely possible

32.8 What good looks like

A humble architecture is not vague or unwilling to decide. It still defines concepts, establishes constraints and expects systems to use them consistently. The difference is that it treats those decisions as maintained understanding rather than permanent truth.

You should be able to show when a concept was introduced, what problem it addressed and how its definition has changed. People should have a clear route for raising cases that do not fit. Exceptions should be reviewed as possible evidence, not merely suppressed as non-conformance. The model should state where confidence is high and where the organisation is still learning.

Most importantly, the organisation should be able to answer a simple question:

What is the most recent thing we learned because reality did not fit our model?

An organisation that cannot answer may have built a model that is too distant from use, or a culture in which contradiction is corrected before it can be examined.

Build Trust

One of the more subtle responsibilities of architecture is deciding where decisions are made.

Many organisations assume that important decisions should naturally move upwards. Work is proposed by the people closest to it, reviewed by successive layers of management and governance, and eventually approved by someone sufficiently senior to authorise it. This appears to reduce risk. More people have examined the decision, more signatures have been collected and more opportunities have existed to prevent mistakes.

The difficulty is that complexity rarely waits for permission.

Trust is often treated as a cultural concern, something built through good leadership or strong interpersonal relationships. Those things matter, but they are only part of the story. Trust is also an architectural property. People trust what they can understand, observe and predict. When goals are clear, language is shared, ownership is visible and feedback arrives quickly, organisations require remarkably less permission because uncertainty has been reduced. Conversely, when understanding is fragmented, approvals multiply. Committees, governance boards and sign-off processes often emerge not because they improve decisions, but because they compensate for information that the organisation has failed to make available elsewhere.

33.1 Let's talk about trust... baby
There is a persistent trust gap between leaders and employees

Perhaps the most famous is the Edelman, which has measured institutional trust for over two decades. One of its recurring findings is that employees consistently report trusting their immediate employer more than business or government generally, but that trust drops sharply as leaders become more distant. Employees tend to trust "my employer" and "my manager" significantly more than CEOs, government leaders or media.

That distinction is interesting architecturally because it suggests trust is fundamentally local, not organisational. People trust people they know and can observe.

Trust predicts organisational performance

Amy Edmondson's work on psychological safety showed that teams where people feel safe to speak up, admit mistakes and challenge assumptions consistently outperform teams where they cannot. The mechanism isn't that people become nicer. It is that the organisation learns faster because bad news travels more easily.

That's almost exactly the argument you're making about architecture.

33.3 Trust enables movement

Modern militaries confronted this problem many decades ago. Complex operations had become too fast, too uncertain and too distributed for every decision to return to a central command post. Rather than attempting to direct every movement, command increasingly focused on communicating intent, establishing doctrine and ensuring that people had the training and resources to act independently when circumstances inevitably changed.

The centre did not become less important.

Its responsibility changed.

Instead of controlling every decision, it became responsible for creating the conditions in which good decisions could be made without constant intervention.

Organisations face much the same challenge. The people closest to the work usually possess the richest understanding of the immediate problem. They see details that cannot easily be communicated upwards, particularly when the situation is changing quickly. Requiring every significant decision to pass through a distant approval process often delays the work without materially improving the quality of the decision itself.

Architecture should therefore aim to support informed local decision-making rather than concentrating every decision at the centre.

33.4 Permission is not trust

Permission and trust are often treated as though they were interchangeable.

They are not.

Permission means that somebody else accepted responsibility for allowing an action to proceed. Trust means believing that the people closest to the work possess the judgement to make that decision themselves within agreed boundaries.

The distinction matters because permission scales poorly.

Every additional approval introduces waiting. Context must be transferred from the people doing the work to people reviewing it. Questions are answered, meetings are scheduled and priorities compete for attention. None of this necessarily improves the outcome. Much of it simply compensates for a lack of confidence elsewhere in the organisation.

Trust requires more investment.

People need clear intent. Shared language. Good architectural principles. Access to information. Training. Feedback when decisions produce unexpected consequences. These things are harder to build than approval processes, but they reduce the need for those approval processes in the first place.

171

Architecture should make trust easier, not permission more frequent.

33.5 Approval often hides missing knowledge

Approval processes have a habit of accumulating over time.

A review board is introduced after a difficult incident. Another committee appears following an audit. Additional sign-offs are required after a failed project. Individually, each decision appears reasonable. Collectively, they produce organisations where relatively straightforward changes require approval from people several steps removed from the work itself.

It is worth asking why those approvals exist.

Sometimes they genuinely reduce risk.

Often they compensate for something else.

An architecture review may exist because architectural principles were never made explicit. A security approval may exist because secure defaults were never built into the platform. A governance meeting may exist because ownership remains unclear. An executive sign-off may exist because success has never been defined well enough for teams to judge it independently.

The approval is solving a symptom.

The architecture should address the underlying cause.

33.6 Replace pmerimssion with evidence

One useful question to ask of any approval process is this:

What evidence would allow this decision to be made without requiring permission?

If a deployment requires manual approval every time, perhaps automated testing and continuous delivery provide stronger evidence than another signature.

If a data sharing request requires multiple committees, perhaps clearer data classifications and policy-driven access controls remove much of the uncertainty.

If every architectural decision must be reviewed centrally, perhaps the organisation has not yet expressed its architectural principles clearly enough for teams to apply them consistently themselves.

Evidence scales.

Permission rarely does.

That does not mean every decision should become autonomous. Some decisions carry consequences large enough that broad discussion remains entirely appropriate. The point is that approvals should exist because they genuinely add judgement, not because the organisation has failed to build confidence elsewhere.

33.7 Architecture creates confidence

Trust is often described as a cultural concern.

It is also an architectural one.

172

Shared language allows people to communicate accurately. Common concepts reduce misunderstanding. Good observability provides rapid feedback. Fitness tests demonstrate that important properties continue to hold. Decision records explain why previous choices were made. Stable interfaces reduce the consequences of independent change.

These are not merely technical practices.

They are mechanisms through which organisations become confident enough to distribute decision-making safely.

An organisation that cannot trust its own people will eventually attempt to replace trust with governance.

An organisation that invests in shared understanding finds it needs remarkably less governance than it once imagined.

33.8 What good looks like

A healthy organisation does not remove every approval process.

It removes the approvals that exist only because something more fundamental is missing.

People understand the intent behind the architecture rather than simply complying with its rules. Decisions are made as close to the work as practical because the organisation has invested in the language, principles, feedback and evidence needed to support them. Central architecture spends less time granting permission and more time improving the conditions under which everyone else can succeed.

That is a very different role.

The architect becomes less like a gatekeeper and more like a steward—preserving shared understanding, building confidence and ensuring that trust rests on evidence rather than optimism. When those things are in place, architecture no longer directs every movement. It provides the conditions in which good movement becomes the natural outcome.

  • Advanced militaries learned decades ago that complex operations can't wait for every decision to flow back through a central command post, so the centre's job changed to providing intent, doctrine, and support rather than control. Good architecture works the same way, sustaining movement instead of directing every movement.
  • Architecture defaults to permission because permission is easy to audit and trust is not, so approval steps accumulate quietly until decisions need sign-off from people who aren't accountable for the outcome. Somewhere in your organisation, an approval step is standing in for evidence nobody's bothered to collect.

Fix the incentives

We've gone over a lot of ground here, there's a lot of observations and information floating here, but we haven't really answered the question of how an organisation accretes all these things.

The important thing to note is that these incentives are not really diametrically opposed. They are locally rational and mutually reinforcing. Each actor can make a defensible decision according to the way their role is measured while collectively producing an organisation that is more dependent, more complicated and less able to change.

  • Consultants are rewarded for transformation
  • Vendors are rewarded for selling products
  • Procurement is rewarded for buying established products
  • Management is rewarded for reducing perceived risk
  • Boards are rewarded for visible governance

And finally:

No one is rewarded for quietly building internal capability over time.

The incentives are all aligned to make sure that the organisation is always in motion, and that the people who are supposed to be helping it adapt, are instead spending their time defending themselves.

34.1 How we get here
34
174

We have described many of the things that accumulate inside an organisation: systems, integrations, committees, controls, exceptions, contracts, duplicated data and abandoned ways of working. We have not yet answered the more important question of how an organisation acquires them.

It rarely happens through a single reckless decision. No one sets out to create an estate that is expensive to operate and almost impossible to change. Complexity accumulates through a succession of decisions that were reasonable within the narrow boundaries in which they were made.

A programme needs to demonstrate progress. Procurement needs to demonstrate that a supplier was selected fairly. Management needs to demonstrate that risk is being controlled. The board needs evidence that governance is in place. Vendors need to sell products and services. Consultants need a defined engagement through which they can provide advice and delivery.

Each decision can be justified. The combined result may still be disastrous.

This is a familiar problem in organisational economics. Principal–agent theory describes situations in which authority, information, incentives and consequences are distributed between different actors. Each participant acts according to the information and rewards available to them, but their interests do not necessarily produce the outcome wanted by the organisation as a whole.

The organisation does not arrive here because everyone behaved irrationally. It arrives here because everyone behaved rationally inside a system that rewarded the wrong things.

34.2 Complexity is usually locally rational

Consider a large procurement.

The programme wants certainty, so it asks for detailed requirements. Procurement wants comparable bids, so it turns those requirements into a specification. Suppliers protect themselves by pricing the uncertainty the specification cannot remove. Governance asks for additional assurance because the resulting contract is expensive. The eventual system struggles to accommodate changing needs, so the organisation creates exceptions, manual processes and additional integrations around it.

At every stage, someone did what their role expected of them.

The programme reduced visible uncertainty. Procurement followed an auditable process. The supplier remained within the contract. Governance received its papers. The board saw evidence that risks had been considered.

The organisation nevertheless acquired another rigid system and a new collection of dependencies.

Complexity accretes in the space between these locally successful decisions. The costs are displaced from the decision being measured into the future operation of the organisation. A procurement can be completed successfully while producing a system that is difficult to change. A programme can go live successfully while leaving years of reconciliation and remedial work behind it. A governance process can operate exactly as designed while making good decisions harder to reach.

This is why examining individual competence is rarely enough. The question is not merely whether people performed their roles correctly. It is whether the roles, incentives and decision boundaries could collectively produce a sustainable outcome.

34.3 Consultants are rewarded for intervention

Consultants can bring specialist knowledge, independent challenge and experience that the organisation genuinely lacks. The problem is not that consultants exist, or that their advice is necessarily poor. It is that the natural unit of consulting work is a bounded engagement: a review, a strategy, a transformation or an implementation.

Quietly improving an organisation's judgement over ten years is difficult to package as an engagement. A transformation programme has a title, a budget, a leadership structure and a visible end state. It can be commissioned, presented and governed. Gradual capability building is slower, less dramatic and harder to attribute to any one intervention.

Research into management fashions helps explain the resulting market. Eric Abrahamson described consultants, management writers, business schools and professional media as “fashion setters” that identify managerial anxieties, develop techniques presented as the frontier of progress and disseminate them back into organisations. The theory does not claim that every new technique is useless. It explains why the market continually needs new techniques to appear progressive and why apparently settled organisations remain receptive to the next wave of transformation.

The consultancy does not need to invent a problem. Real problems already exist. It needs to frame those problems in a form that can be addressed through an engagement. Fragmented processes become an operating-model transformation. Inconsistent data becomes a platform programme. Weak coordination becomes organisational redesign. The framing may be legitimate, but it also determines what kinds of solutions can subsequently be sold.

The danger appears when the engagement substitutes for organisational learning. The consultants diagnose the problem, formulate the strategy, choose the method and sometimes deliver the implementation. Internal staff provide context and operate the result, but the organisation does not necessarily acquire the ability to repeat the work for itself.

The UK National Audit Office now explicitly recommends that knowledge transfer be designed into consultancy contracts and carried out throughout the engagement, rather than reduced to a handover of documents at the end. Its guidance says internal staff must learn enough to continue the initiative after the consultants leave and, where possible, avoid repeatedly purchasing the same capability.

That advice exists because knowledge transfer does not occur automatically. A completed engagement can leave behind deliverables without leaving behind capability.

34.4 Vendors are rewarded for production adoption

A vendor sees an organisation through the product it sells.

A customer relationship platform vendor sees relationships that can be represented through its platform. An enterprise resource planning vendor sees processes that can be configured within its modules. A cloud provider sees workloads that can be moved into its services. None of this requires dishonesty. A product company naturally understands problems through the capabilities of its product.

The difficulty begins when the organisation adopts the same view.

176

The product's categories become the organisation's categories. Its workflows become the approved way of working. Its limitations are reframed as standardisation. Where reality does not fit, the organisation changes its process, purchases another module, commissions customisation or builds an integration around the boundary.

The product becomes larger than the problem it was bought to solve.

Contemporary technology markets make this dependence more pronounced. The National Audit Office notes that digital services are increasingly subscription-based, controlled by suppliers rather than owned by the customer, and concentrated among a small number of very large providers. It also describes legacy systems that have become “black boxes” supported by the suppliers that originally provided them, making them unattractive or impractical for alternative suppliers to take over.

A vendor may deliver exactly what it promised and still increase the organisation's long-term dependency. The contractual outcome and the architectural outcome are not the same thing.

This is not an argument against buying products. Commodity capability should usually be bought. The mistake is failing to distinguish between capability that is genuinely common and capability through which the organisation expresses its particular identity, knowledge or advantage.

When a product is placed over something strategically distinctive, the organisation must either constrain itself to the product or spend indefinitely adapting the product to itself.

34.5 Procurement is rewarded for a defensible purchase

Procurement exists for good reasons. It protects fairness, competition, financial control and legal compliance. Those responsibilities also shape what procurement regards as a good decision.

An established supplier is easier to assess than an unfamiliar one. A standard framework is easier to defend than a novel commercial arrangement. A product with many reference customers appears safer than a capability that must be developed internally. When outcomes are uncertain, evidence that others have already bought something becomes a substitute for evidence that it fits this organisation.

Institutional theory calls part of this mimetic isomorphism. DiMaggio and Powell observed that uncertainty encourages organisations to imitate other organisations, particularly those perceived as successful or legitimate. Copying a recognised peer does not eliminate risk, but it makes the decision easier to explain.

Modern procurement research continues to identify this pattern. OECD work describes risk aversion as a barrier to innovation procurement, while recent European Commission analysis found that public buyers often prefer established firms able to provide previous customer references, even where delivering a genuinely new solution makes those references a weak predictor of success.

Procurement therefore tends to favour what is already legible to procurement.

177

This becomes particularly dangerous when commercial specialists are separated from the technical and operational knowledge needed to evaluate the purchase. The National Audit Office found that commercial teams frequently led digital procurements without sufficient use of digital expertise. It warned that shortages of combined commercial and technical skill can lead organisations to rely on generic frameworks, and even to shape specifications around the procurement mechanisms officials know how to use rather than the nature of the problem being solved.

Procurement has not necessarily failed in these cases. It may have succeeded according to every criterion applied to it.

The criteria were incomplete.

34.6 Management is rewarded for reducing perceived risk

A manager proposing an unfamiliar internal approach carries a particular kind of exposure. If it fails, the decision is theirs. They selected the team, accepted the uncertainty and chose not to follow the established route.

Buying a familiar product from a major supplier changes the narrative. The decision can be supported by market position, reference customers, procurement assurance and external advice. Failure remains possible, but it becomes easier to describe as an unforeseeable delivery problem rather than an irresponsible choice.

This creates a distinction between reducing risk and making risk defensible.

Externalisation often achieves the second more reliably than the first.

A contract can specify liabilities, deliverables and escalation routes, but it cannot transfer the organisation's ultimate responsibility for the outcome. The National Audit Office found that attempts to push excessive risk onto technology suppliers can lead to over-specified contracts, fewer bidders and services that technically satisfy a contract without meeting the organisation's actual need. Its conclusion is blunt: the buyer remains accountable when the service fails, regardless of how the contract allocated responsibility.

The risk has not disappeared. It has moved.

It moves from the visible moment of decision into integration, operation, renewal, switching and eventual replacement. By the time those costs appear, the original decision-makers may have moved roles and the programme may have been declared complete.

Management is therefore encouraged to prefer decisions that are safe to approve over systems that are safe to inhabit

34.7 Boards are rewarded for visible governance

Boards cannot observe an organisation directly. They depend on reports, risk registers, assurance statements, programme dashboards and the testimony of leadership. These artefacts are necessary, but they create a bias towards risks that can be represented through governance processes.

A committee can see whether an architecture review occurred. It is much harder to see whether the review improved the architecture. It can see whether a risk has an owner, a rating and a mitigation. It is harder to see whether the organisation understands the risk well enough to act when circumstances change.

178

The distinction is between evidence of governance and evidence of control.

Research into audit committees in UK higher education found that oversight could become fixated on the process of risk management and over-reliant on risk registers. The researchers identified both substantive and symbolic governance activity and questioned whether the resulting arrangements enabled committees to monitor operational risk effectively.

That does not mean governance is merely theatre. It means governance can become theatrical when the artefacts become the objective.

Current UK government guidance recognises this danger. It says assurance should be tested against reliable evidence, should identify gaps and duplication, and should explicitly examine whether risks are being over-controlled, consuming resources that could be used elsewhere.

Visible governance is attractive because it can be counted. Meetings happened. Papers were submitted. Gates were passed. Risks were recorded. These things demonstrate activity, but activity does not necessarily demonstrate understanding.

When boards reward the production of assurance more consistently than the reduction of dependency, the organisation acquires more governance without necessarily becoming more governable.

34.8 Internal capability loses the presentation contest

Internal capability grows quietly.

People learn the organisation's history. Teams discover where concepts do not align. Engineers develop tools that remove recurring work. Analysts learn which figures are misleading. Managers develop enough technical judgement to distinguish a difficult problem from an expensive sales proposition.

There is rarely a single launch date for this capability. Its value appears in incidents avoided, contracts not required, decisions made more quickly and changes completed without a major programme. Those outcomes are difficult to place on a slide because many of them are counterfactual: the expensive thing that did not need to happen.

External expenditure is easier to classify as investment. Internal expertise is more likely to appear as headcount.

A transformation programme receives a business case because it promises a future state. The people gradually constructing that future state through ordinary work remain an operating cost. The organisation can therefore become willing to spend millions purchasing a capability while remaining reluctant to fund the smaller permanent team needed to understand, operate and evolve it.

This is not merely a theoretical concern. The National Audit Office found that the UK government had not clearly decided which digital skills it should retain internally and which should be sourced externally. It connected weak “intelligent client” capability—the ability to understand needs, markets, technologies and suppliers—to poor outcomes in digital change.

Other governments have begun responding directly. Australia's 2023 Strategic Commissioning Framework was explicitly introduced to reduce dependence on consultants and contractors and rebuild internal public-service capability, particularly for work considered core to the state.

179

Capability does not mean doing everything internally. It means retaining enough knowledge and authority to decide what should be bought, judge whether it is working and change direction without requiring permission from the supplier.

Without that capability, procurement ceases to be a choice. It becomes dependency purchasing further dependency.

34.9 Motion creates more motion

These incentives form a reinforcing cycle.

An organisation lacks internal capacity, so it commissions external advice. The advice recommends a programme. The programme procures products and implementation services. The new system introduces dependencies and transitional work. Internal teams spend their time integrating, reconciling and supporting it. Because they are occupied maintaining the result, they have less time to develop the capability that might have provided an alternative.

The next problem therefore arrives in an organisation even less able to solve it internally.

External intervention creates the conditions for further external intervention.

A system introduced to simplify the estate may coexist with its predecessor for years. A product selected to standardise work may require local tools to handle the exceptions. A governance mechanism introduced after one failure may remain after its purpose is forgotten. None of these additions is large enough to trigger a reconsideration of the whole estate. They settle around what is already there.

The organisation becomes busier without becoming more adaptive.

The National Audit Office has warned explicitly that new technology procurements can add another layer of complexity and cost over existing legacy systems, particularly where organisations lack the combined digital and commercial capability to understand the transition.

The movement itself then becomes evidence that the organisation is responding.

Strategies are launched. Partners are appointed. Programmes are mobilised. Governance is established. The absence of movement begins to look like the absence of leadership, even where the most responsible action would be to continue building, learning or removing things slowly.

Transformation stops being an exceptional response to a particular need.

It becomes the organisation's normal operating condition.

34.10 The people who could help become defendants

The people closest to the work often see these problems first.

They know which dependencies are hidden behind the programme plan. They know why the existing process contains apparent irregularities. They know which migration assumptions are unlikely to survive contact with real data. They also know that explaining these things can make them appear negative, territorial or resistant to change.

The programme is rewarded for movement. They are supplying reasons to slow down.

The vendor is rewarded for adoption. They are describing where the product does not fit.

180

Leadership is presenting confidence. They are introducing uncertainty.

Their knowledge is necessary, but its immediate effect is inconvenient. The organisation begins treating the people carrying the most context as obstacles to the future rather than evidence about the present.

Over time, people adapt. They qualify their concerns. They wait to see whether somebody more senior will raise the issue. They record objections defensively or stop raising them altogether.

Research describes organisational silence as the point at which people collectively believe that speaking about problems is futile or dangerous. Once that belief spreads, withholding information becomes normal rather than exceptional.

Related research into organisational defensive routines describes patterns through which people and institutions avoid embarrassment or threat. These routines protect individuals in the immediate situation while preventing the organisation from exposing errors, learning from them and changing the conditions that produced them.

This is how the people who are supposed to help the organisation adapt end up spending their time defending themselves.

They collect evidence that they warned the programme. They document decisions for personal protection. They learn to translate operational reality into language that governance will accept. Eventually, more effort goes into surviving the decision process than improving the decision.

The organisation then interprets the absence of candid disagreement as alignment.

34.11 Ask hard questions

The purpose of tracing these incentives is not to discover a villain. It is to identify who benefits from the current arrangement, who carries its consequences and which information remains absent from the decision.

Begin with the question already implied by this chapter:

Who benefits if the system stays complicated?

Then ask whether those are the same people advising on its simplification, extending its contracts or selling its replacement.

That question should not be treated as an accusation. Complexity can be commercially beneficial without anyone deliberately creating it. A supplier may earn legitimate revenue supporting a system that has become difficult to leave. A consultancy may be repeatedly engaged because the organisation has never retained the capability transferred through earlier engagements. A governance function may expand because every failure appears to justify another control.

The incentive matters even where the intent is good.

A useful examination follows several lines of inquiry:

34.12 Reward stewardship

Accretion will continue while the organisation rewards addition more visibly than removal.

Preventing it requires more than asking people to behave differently. The incentives themselves have to change.

Consultancy engagements should be measured partly by the capability they leave behind. Contracts should identify the knowledge, skills and decision authority that will transfer into the organisation, not simply the documents that will be delivered.

Vendors should be evaluated against whole-life dependency, interoperability, data portability and exit cost, not only implementation price and feature coverage.

Procurement should involve technical and operational expertise before the solution and contract structure have hardened. Its success should include the quality of the operating outcome, not merely compliance with the buying process.

Management should be rewarded for sustained capability, reduced dependency and the removal of old systems—not simply the launch of new programmes.

Boards should ask for evidence of operability, adaptability and internal understanding alongside evidence of governance. A programme that passes every gate but leaves the organisation unable to alter its own systems has not reduced risk.

Architecture must make these long-term effects visible while there is still time to influence the decision. That means tracing incentives, dependencies, knowledge and exit from the beginning rather than recording them after the contract has been signed.

Organisational accretion is not mysterious. It is the residue left by incentives.

The organisation asks each participant to optimise one visible part of the problem, then inherits the combined consequences. It continually rewards motion, acquisition and assurance while treating continuity, removal and patient capability building as ordinary costs.

No one is rewarded for quietly making the organisation better able to understand and change itself over time.

Let engineers drive capability

This sections pretty near and dear to my heart, so we're going to spend some time here.

I think people outside of software engineering have been sort of hoodwinked over the last twenty years. They've been sold the idea that building software is the expensive and difficult part. It isn't. Building understanding is. Modern software engineering has accumulated decades of abstractions, frameworks, patterns, infrastructure and hard-earned lessons that mean a handful of capable engineers can produce systems in weeks that would once have required entire departments. The bottleneck is infrequently typing actual code. It's understanding what should exist in the first place.

And a few IN software engineering as well if I'm honest.

So when you buy a product which provides some functionality, and you evaluate it solely on the functionality it provides, you're not saving yourself time in terms of cost or effort for engineers to write code and build applications which approximate that functionality. You're alleviating yourself of the effort and responsibility of understanding what the organisation actually needs, and subsequently robbing your internal teams of the opportunity to build deep understanding, and solve the problem in ways which align directly with your operational needs instead of the prescribed needs of a vendor.

Aside: Go look how many project management platforms are out there, go look, google "project management software", I'll wait. There's so many that it should be abundantly clear that building them has not been hard since the late 90s. We can build them fast, and at volume, and at a level of quality that's sufficient for most organisations, and then sell them.

38.1 The spectre of Shadow IT

Shadow IT is usually described as uncontrolled technology: spreadsheets, local databases, unofficial SaaS products and scripts created outside central oversight.

38
189

Research presents a more interesting picture. Shadow systems often emerge because official systems do not support the work people are accountable for performing. They are both a risk and a form of adaptation. Research on shadow IT has documented the security and governance problems it introduces, while studies of workarounds during major enterprise implementations show that unofficial practices may also preserve essential distinctions and operational flexibility that formal systems removed.

This creates a recurring cycle:

The organisation standardises a process. The standard does not represent important local conditions. People construct workarounds. The workarounds fragment data and control. Leadership responds with more standardisation and governance.

The shadow estate is then blamed on user behaviour, even though it contains evidence about where the official architecture does not fit reality.

This is an interesting phenomena book because it connects architecture, meaning, truth and organisational adaptation. Unofficial technology is often the organisation attempting to restore information that the official model discarded.

38.2 Software is easier to build than people think

If I want to expose an HTTP API today, here's what that actually looks like:

use axum::{routing::get, Router};

#[tokio::main]
async fn main() {

    let listener = tokio::net::TcpListener::bind("0.0.0.0:3000").await.unwrap();
    axum::serve(listener, app).await.unwrap();
}

The mechanics of building software have become more or less trivial, it's the difficult questions that come after this bit of code that are harder:

  • What should the endpoint expose?
  • Who owns it?
  • What concepts does it represent?
  • How does it evolve?

Those aren't programming questions, they're core organisational ones.

38.3 Software engineering is theory building

Peter Naur famously described programming as Theory Building. I've always thought that was a better description of software engineering than "writing code". Engineers spend most of their time constructing a coherent theory of an organisation: its language, concepts, constraints, relationships and behaviours. The source code is really just the artefact left behind once that understanding is sufficiently complete.

190

Writing the actual software has become progressively cheaper over time, and not because engineering salaries have gone down. Modern languages, frameworks, cloud infrastructure, open-source, AI-assisted development, and decades of accumulated knowledge and balanced abstractions mean that producing a service, an API, a workflow engine, or an entire web, desktop or mobile application is often measured in weeks rather than years. For an experienced engineer, implementation is rarely the limiting factor.

38.4 Commercial software represents somebody else's theory

In order to be adaptive, you need to have a deep understanding of your organisation, it's people, it's processes, it's funding, it's partnerships. If you buy commercial software to solve a problem that is distinct to your organisation, you are effectively outsourcing that understanding to a vendor whose incentives are to bend your organisation to fit their theory of how organisations should operate and what they find meaningful.

Commercial software is literally just theory that has accumulated somewhere else. Years of solving one or a handful of organisation's problems eventually became abstract enough to package and sell. Every ERP, CRM, HR platform and project management tool holds thousands of assumptions about how work should happen.

You should be <i>very</i> careful about whose organisations processes you're buying. Because when they're misaligned, it's extremely costly in time, money and resource, and in extreme cases, sometimes impossible to change.

Organisations consistently misunderstand what it is they're actually paying for, they think that they're buying software because software is expensive to build. What they're really buying is someone else's theory because they aren't willing to invest in building their own.

38.5 How a rational organisation goes awry

You don't arrive at commercial software accidentally. You're pushed toward it from multiple directions. Consultants recommend it, procurement processes actually reward it, vendors market it, analysts rank it, boards recognize names. It's a subtle set of pressures, but it's unbelievably relentless.

All the arguments you hear sound great, in isolation.

"Dont't reinvent the wheel"

This is a pretty common one, it implies that writing software is where the cost is, and the simple and most straightforward solution is that buying software must be cheaper than building it.

Buying a product doesn't remove the work of understanding your organisation, it just shifts that work into configuration, integrations, customisation, governance, and organisational compromise.

You are <strong>not</strong> buying less complexity, you're buying complexity that lives somewhere else.

"It's industry standard!"

Standards are incredibly useful when the problem itself is pretty standard. Things like Payroll, Identity, Email, Accounting.

Those are domains where thousands of organisations have already discovered remarkably similar solutions, but the mistake is assuming your differentiating capabilities have also become standard.

Research isn't standard, humanitarian response isn't standard, scientific funding isn't standard. Your competitive advantage almost certainly isn't standard either.

"It's more secure"

This is one of the more persuasive arguments. Large vendors have security teams, penetration testing, compliance certifications, and dedicated incident response.

Those are real advantage, but security is only a single quality of an overarching systems. Adaptability, replaceability, organisational fit are also qualities that matter.

Buying security while simultaneously making your organisation less capable of changing is still a trade-off, it isn't a free improvement.

"Thousands of organisations already use it"

This sounds compelling, but actually think about what that means under the hood.

It means the product has accumulated requirements from thousands of organisations. It wasn't built for yours.

The larger the customer base becomes, the more the software optimizes for the statistical average organisation.

If your organisations exists precisely because it isn't average, that's probably worth mulling over a bit.

"It's cheaper"

Sometimes, sometimes not, depends on how you measure cost in most cases.

Procurement compares licensing against development, but almost nobody compares licensing, plus configuration, plus integration, plus training, plus administration, plus consultants, plus migration lock-ins, plus eventual replacement.

Those are engineering costs too, they're just usually hidden in different budgets masking the real cost of the decision.

"Nobody gets fired for buying X"

This is probably one of the strongest pressures, and it's really that commercial software transfers responsibility.

The cumulative effect of all this pressure is subtle, you slowly begin to believe that software itself is where value lives when it really isn't. The actual value is in understanding the organisation, and software is just an artefact of that understanding.

38.7 Invest in people, not products

Investing in engineering has very different economics to enterprise software. A good engineering team doesn't just produce software, it continually refines its understanding of the organisation. The cost of changing your mind

When organisations decide to replace a system or adopt a new way of working, the discussion usually begins with technology. Attention naturally focuses on migration plans, implementation effort, integration work and project budgets because those things are visible. They can be estimated, costed and planned for.

They are also only part of the cost.

The larger cost often sits elsewhere, quietly accumulated over years of people adapting themselves to the existing system. Processes have been shaped around its limitations. Teams have developed ways of working that assume its behaviour. Reports, operational procedures and even the language people use have gradually settled into forms that make sense within the current environment. None of those things appears on the project plan because they are no longer recognised as consequences of the system. They have simply become "how we work."

By the time the organisation decides to change direction, it is no longer replacing a piece of software. It is asking the organisation to stop behaving in ways it has spent years learning.

44.1 Every Decision Accumulates Consequences

Architectural decisions rarely remain isolated.

A decision made today becomes part of the environment in which tomorrow's decisions are made. New work naturally builds on what already exists because that is usually the cheapest and least disruptive option. Over time the original decision stops being one decision among many and instead becomes part of the background that every new piece of work quietly assumes.

44
200

This is why changing direction becomes progressively more expensive. The cost does not come from undoing the original decision itself. It comes from undoing everything that has grown around it since.

An organisation may discover that a particular product is no longer the right fit, but replacing the product also means revisiting years of reports, integrations, operational procedures and training that were written assuming that product would remain. None of those artefacts was created to make future change difficult. They simply reflect the organisation's understanding at the time they were produced.

44.2 Learning Is Cheap. Acting On It Is Not.

Most organisations continue learning throughout their lives. They gain a better understanding of their customers, discover more useful ways of organising work and refine the language they use to describe themselves. Learning is rarely the problem.

The difficulty begins when that improved understanding conflicts with decisions that have already become deeply embedded.

Suppose an organisation realises that what it has always treated as a single business capability is actually several distinct capabilities that should evolve independently. The insight may arrive in a single workshop. Acting on it may take years.

Not because the idea is complicated, but because every system, report, interface and process built around the earlier assumption now has to be reconsidered. The organisation is not paying for the new idea. It is paying for the continued success of the old one.

44.3 Why The Cost Is Often Hidden

One reason organisations repeatedly underestimate the cost of change is that the cost is distributed.

  • No individual team sees the whole picture.
  • Engineering estimates the migration.
  • Operations estimates new procedures.
  • Finance estimates licensing.
  • Training estimates onboarding.

Each estimate is reasonable within its own boundary, yet none captures the cumulative effect of asking an entire organisation to replace one set of assumptions with another.

By the time those costs are visible, the decision to change has usually already been made. Projects then appear to overrun, not because they were badly managed, but because the organisation discovered that it had spent years building around assumptions it no longer wished to keep.

This is one reason major transformations so often feel unexpectedly expensive. The organisation is not simply building something new; it is unwinding years of adaptation that nobody had been consciously maintaining.

The Cost of Rediscovering Knowledge

Changing a long-established system almost always begins with a period of rediscovery.

Before anything can be redesigned, the organisation first has to understand what it already has. Why does this process exist? Why are these two systems inconsistent? Which reports depend on this behaviour? Which parts of the current design are deliberate and which are simply historical accidents?

These questions are rarely difficult because the answers are technically complex. They are difficult because the reasoning has gradually disappeared. The organisation still possesses the software, but it no longer possesses the understanding that produced it.

Large programmes often spend months rediscovering knowledge that the organisation once held perfectly well. That effort is not creating new value. It is paying the cost of previous forgetting.

The Cost of Unwinding Adaptation

Software does not exist in isolation. Given enough time, people adapt themselves to it.

Work is organised around its limitations. Reports reflect the questions it can answer. Processes evolve to accommodate the behaviours it encourages. None of these adaptations feels particularly significant while it is happening. Each one is simply the easiest way of getting work done.

Years later those adaptations have become indistinguishable from the organisation itself.

Replacing the software therefore means unwinding years of learned behaviour before the organisation can begin learning something new. This is often one of the largest costs of change, yet it rarely appears explicitly in project estimates because there is no single piece of work called "undo ten years of adaptation."

The Cost of Coordination

Foundational decisions rarely affect only one team.

As organisations grow, more and more work comes to depend on the same assumptions. A change that appears straightforward within one department may require agreement from many others before it can proceed. The effort is no longer spent solving the technical problem. It is spent helping different parts of the organisation move together.

This coordination cost increases naturally over time. The more successful a system becomes, the more parts of the organisation build upon it. Success therefore creates its own inertia. A capability that serves the whole organisation is usually far more difficult to change than one used by a single team, regardless of the quality of its implementation.

The Cost of Opportunity

Every major change consumes attention.

Engineers spend time migrating instead of building. Operational teams support the transition instead of improving services. Subject matter experts explain the past instead of shaping the future. Leaders devote months to governance and decision-making that could otherwise have been spent addressing new opportunities.

202

These costs are difficult to quantify because they are measured in things that never happened. The capability that was never built. The improvement that was postponed. The opportunity that was missed because the organisation was occupied preserving or replacing yesterday's decisions.

Opportunity cost rarely appears in architecture discussions, yet it is often the largest cost of all.

The Cost of Confidence

Major architectural change asks people to abandon ways of working that have become familiar. Even when the current system is widely recognised as inadequate, it is at least understood. People know where its edges are and how to work around its weaknesses.

Replacing that certainty with something unfamiliar creates hesitation. Teams become more cautious. Decision-making slows. Productivity often falls before it rises again.

None of this reflects resistance to change. It reflects the simple reality that confidence takes time to rebuild.

Architects should expect this and design transitions that minimise unnecessary disruption rather than assuming new technology alone will create immediate improvements.

Cost of professional discounting
  • People who have built up experience through specialisation in a given system have invariably built up knowledge about how the organisation works, it's processes and dark corners.
  • Because the system they're specialised in is being replaced, their knowledge is often discounted, and they are treated as if they are no longer relevant to the organisation's future.
44.5 Designing For Change

None of this suggests that architects should avoid making decisions. Every system embodies assumptions, and every architecture reflects the best understanding available at the time.

The responsibility is to recognise that those assumptions will eventually change.

When making an architectural decision, it is worth asking not only whether it solves today's problem, but what would have to change if the organisation later discovered that the decision was based on an incomplete understanding.

That question shifts the focus of architecture. Instead of asking only whether a design is correct, it asks whether the organisation will be able to recover gracefully when its understanding improves.

44.6 A Practical Test

Choose one significant architectural decision in your organisation that nobody would make today.

203

Rather than asking how difficult it would be to replace the technology, trace the consequences that have accumulated around it. Look for the reports that depend upon it, the operational practices that assume it, the language that grew from it and the teams whose work has adapted to its behaviour.

The exercise is not intended to criticise the original decision. It is intended to make visible the cost that normally remains hidden. Once those consequences can be seen, they become something architecture can actively work to reduce in future decisions.

44.7 Decisions outlive their reasons
  • We talk endlessly about coupling between systems, and almost never about coupling across time. If changing today's organisation requires re-litigating decisions made fifteen years ago, for reasons nobody currently working there was present for, your architecture is coupled to history just as tightly as it could ever be coupled to another system.
  • Every architectural decision is made in a context, a set of priorities, a technology landscape, a set of assumptions about the future, that was reasonable when it was made. The problem isn't that those decisions were wrong, it's that organisations keep negotiating with their own past long after the context that justified it has gone; chapter 9 covered why the reasons behind those decisions get forgotten, this is what that forgetting costs architecturally.
  • The test: for your most painful system, ask how much it would cost to change your mind about one foundational decision made when it was built, not to patch around it, to actually reverse it. Great architecture is measured by how cheap that answer is, because the alternative is an organisation that can only ever move in the direction its old decisions already committed it to.

Define invariants

Every organisation changes.

Departments are reorganised. Systems are replaced. Processes evolve. Teams grow, shrink and merge. None of those things is unusual, and none of them is necessarily a problem. The difficulty begins when the organisation has no stable reference point against which those changes can be understood.

That reference point is what I call an invariant.

An invariant is something the organisation believes will remain true regardless of how it chooses to organise itself or which technology happens to support it. It is not a description of today's implementation. It is a description of the organisation itself.

Every other architectural decision depends upon identifying these first.

45.1 Recognising the problem

The easiest way to recognise that invariants have never been defined is to listen to how people explain the organisation.

Ask someone what a Grant is.

If the answer begins with:

"It's the thing in Salesforce..."

or

"It's that record in Oracle..."

or

"It's whatever this table represents..."

then the organisation has confused its systems with itself.

The same exercise works for almost any important concept.

Ask three different teams what an Application, Award, Employee, Project or Organisation is.

If the answers differ, or if everyone eventually points at a different system, then the organisation has no stable definition independent of its technology.

That is the first problem to solve.

45.2 What makes something an invariant?

Not everything deserves to be an invariant.

An invariant should continue making sense even if every current system disappeared tomorrow.

For most organisations, the list is surprisingly small.

People exist independently of HR systems.

Organisations exist independently of CRMs.

Money exists independently of finance systems.

Authority exists independently of approval workflows.

Relationships exist independently of databases.

Provenance exists independently of audit logs.

These things describe reality.

The software merely records observations about them.

A useful test is to imagine replacing every major platform in the organisation.

If the concept would still exist afterwards, it is a good candidate for an invariant.

If the concept disappears with the software, it probably describes an implementation rather than the organisation.

45.3 Writing them down

Most organisations only need between five and eight invariants.

More than that usually indicates you're describing implementation rather than identity.

Write each invariant as a short paragraph in plain English.

Avoid technical language.

Avoid product names.

Avoid organisational structure.

No mention of Oracle.

They are not part of the invariant.

A useful set of invariants has several properties.

Property Description Independent Can be understood without referring to any system. Stable Expected to remain valid through organisational change. Shared Every team understands the definition in the same way. Simple Describes the concept, not the implementation. Testable People can identify whether something belongs to the concept or not.

If an invariant cannot satisfy these properties, it probably needs simplifying.

45.5 Common mistakes

The most common mistake is confusing today's organisation with the organisation itself.

For example:

"An Application is something submitted through Salesforce."

That definition fails immediately if Salesforce is replaced.

Likewise,

"A Grant is something approved by the Grants Operations team."

Teams change.

Responsibilities move.

The definition has become coupled to today's operating model.

A stronger definition describes the thing itself rather than the current mechanism through which it happens.

The second common mistake is attempting to define everything.

The purpose of invariants is not to describe the entire organisation.

It is to identify the handful of concepts that remain stable while everything else changes around them.

This exercise usually takes less than two hours.

  • Ask each person to independently write down the ten most important concepts in the organisation.
  • Compare the lists.
  • Group concepts that clearly describe the same underlying thing.
  • Remove concepts that only exist because of particular systems or processes.
  • Continue until only five to eight concepts remain.
  • Write one paragraph describing each concept without mentioning any system, product, department or process.

The discussion is usually more valuable than the document it produces.

You are not finished when everybody agrees.

You are finished when somebody unfamiliar with your systems can read the definitions and understand the organisation without needing any additional explanation.

The document should fit comfortably on a single page.

If it requires twenty pages of caveats and exceptions, you are almost certainly documenting implementations rather than invariants.

Return to the concepts and simplify them.

Give meaning a language

The previous chapter asked you to identify the handful of things that remain true regardless of how your organisation changes. Those invariants describe what the organisation is. They do not yet describe what the organisation knows about those things or how they relate to one another.

That is the purpose of this chapter.

Every organisation already has a language. People speak about applications, grants, projects, people and organisations every day. The problem is not that the language doesn't exist. The problem is that it usually exists only in conversation. It changes slightly from team to team, acquires different meanings in different systems and is rarely written down in a form that everyone can agree upon.

The goal is not to invent a new language.

It is to make the existing one explicit.

46.1 Recognising the problem

A useful way to test whether your organisation has a shared language is to ask different teams to describe the same concept without referring to a system.

Ask what an Application is.

One team may describe a request for funding. Another may describe a software application. A third may begin explaining how Salesforce stores the record rather than what the concept actually represents.

None of these people are necessarily wrong.

46
211

They are simply describing different things using the same word.

These differences often remain invisible because each team spends most of its time talking to itself. The disagreement only becomes visible when systems need to exchange information or reports need to combine data from different parts of the organisation.

By then the language problem has become a technology problem.

46.2 Describe the organisation, not the database

A common mistake when designing a data model is to begin with tables.

The database already contains columns, identifiers and foreign keys, so it feels natural to organise discussions around those structures. The result is usually a model that faithfully describes one particular implementation while saying very little about the organisation itself.

Reverse the process.

Describe the organisation first.

What are the important concepts?

What do we know about each one?

How are they related?

Only after those questions have been answered should you consider how a particular database, CRM or ERP might choose to represent them.

The distinction matters because systems are implementations.

They are not reality.

A CRM does not create a grant. An ERP does not define an employee. A database does not determine whether two organisations are related. These systems simply record observations about things that already exist.

The map is not the territory.

An ontology exists because the map changes far more often than the territory beneath it.

46.3 Describe concepts

Begin with the invariants identified in the previous chapter.

Each invariant becomes a concept within your shared language.

Keep the description short and independent of any implementation.

For example:

Concept Description Grant A commitment by the organisation to provide funding under agreed conditions. Person An individual who participates in the organisation's work in one or more roles. Organisation A legal or informal body that interacts with the organisation.

At this stage you are not designing databases.

212

You are creating a vocabulary that allows people to describe the organisation consistently.

46.4 Describe attributes

Once the concepts are clear, identify the information that belongs to each one.

Attributes describe the concept.

They do not describe how a particular system happens to store it.

For a Grant this might include:

Attribute Description Identifier A stable identifier for the grant. Title A human-readable description. Awarded Amount The agreed funding value. Award Date The date on which the commitment was made.

Notice that these descriptions avoid implementation details.

Whether one system stores a date as a timestamp and another as text is irrelevant.

Those are implementation choices.

The attribute itself belongs to the organisation, not the software.

46.5 Describe relationships

Relationships are often the first thing lost when organisations rely entirely on databases to describe themselves.

A foreign key tells one system how two rows are connected.

It says very little about what that connection means.

Make the relationship explicit.

Instead of saying

grant.organisation_id

say

A Grant is awarded to one or more Organisations.

Instead of

application.person_id

say

An Application is submitted by one or more People.

Once relationships are described in the language of the organisation they become understandable to people as well as software.

They also become reusable across every system that represents the same concepts.

46.6 Give values meaning
213

Many attributes can only take one value from a known set.

  • Status
  • Country
  • Currency
  • Funding Type

These should be treated as controlled vocabularies rather than free text.

The purpose is not consistency for its own sake.

It is preserving meaning.

If one system records Awarded, another records Approved and a third records Successful, somebody eventually has to decide whether those values represent the same thing.

Making that decision once, deliberately, is far cheaper than making it independently in every report, integration and migration.

46.7 Organise the language

As the number of concepts grows, they naturally begin clustering around different areas of the organisation.

  • People
  • Funding
  • Finance
  • Research
  • Governance
  • Technology

These are domains.

Domains exist to make the language easier to navigate.

They should not become barriers.

A concept belongs to one domain because that is where it is primarily understood, not because other domains are forbidden from using it.

The organisation should still have one shared language.

Domains simply provide structure as that language grows.

46.8 Avoid reflecting your systems

The strongest temptation during this work is to allow an existing system to define the model.

This usually happens without anyone intending it.

The first CRM becomes the definition of a customer.

The finance platform becomes the definition of money.

The HR system becomes the definition of a person.

214

Once that happens every future system is expected to match the implementation choices of the first.

The organisation gradually forgets that the concepts existed long before the software did.

Always model the organisation.

Never model the product.

46.9 Deliverable

At the end of this exercise you should have a document that describes your organisation independently of its technology.

It should contain:

  • The core concepts that describe the organisation.
  • The attributes that belong to each concept.
  • The relationships between those concepts.
  • Controlled vocabularies for values with agreed meanings.
  • A small number of domains that organise the model as it grows.

The entire document should be understandable by someone who has never seen your systems.

If it reads like a database schema, you are describing an implementation.

If it reads like a description of the organisation itself, you are describing meaning.

Design the exit

Here's a super obvious in hindsight, but often overlooked aspect of enterprise architecture. We spend enormous effort and time planning how a system will be introduced, and almost no time at all thinking about how we eventually remove it.

We build out loads of artefacts, and content around implementation plans, key dates, migration plans, and support plans (and sometimes not). All these things have governance, risk registers and acceptance criteria. An enormous amount of effort goes into hedging our bets and ensuring that the system is introduced successfully into the organisation. At no point in that process do we ever as the question:

How do we remove it?

That, in my opinion, is a huge oversight because every system is going to eventually leave the organisation. It may be replaced, absorbed into another capability, retired because the business has changed, or simply become obsolete. We never really know the timing exactly, but we know the outcome, experience has taught us, bit us extremely hard on that front.

If an architecture has no credible, or even considered exist strategy for some component or application, then the organisation isn't in control of that capability. It's at the mercy of the system itself, and the people who maintain it. That's not a good place to be, but it doesn't seem to show up in risk registers.

49.1 Recognizing the problem

A simple exercise reveals whether an organisation has designed an exit.

Choose one of your major systems and ask a small group of engineers, architects and operational staff how it would be removed.

49
218

If the discussion quickly becomes a conversation about risk, disruption, years of work and countless unknown dependencies, the organisation has not designed an exit.

More often than not, nobody can answer the question with confidence because the answer has never been considered. The system was designed to be introduced, not to be replaced.

That is surprisingly common.

49.2 Every system is temporary

One of the themes running throughout this book is that organisations outlive their technology.

The systems you use today are temporary observations of a much longer-lived organisation. They exist to support the organisation, not define it.

That means every major system should be viewed as replaceable from the day it enters production.

This is not an argument that systems should be easy to throw away or that organisations should constantly chase new technology. Quite the opposite. Stable systems are often desirable.

The point is that stability should come from continuing usefulness, not from the absence of any practical escape.

49.3 What does the organisation need to keep?

An exit strategy begins by separating the organisation from the implementation.

If the system disappeared tomorrow, what would the organisation actually need to preserve?

Usually it is not everything.

Some data has enduring value because it represents the organisation's memory. Other information exists only to support the operation of that particular product. Some integrations describe business capabilities. Others exist only because of the software's internal design.

These distinctions are rarely documented because they are unnecessary while the system remains in service.

They become essential the moment the organisation wants to leave.

Without them, every migration becomes an attempt to carry everything forward, simply because nobody knows what can safely be left behind.

49.4 Make dependencies visible

Systems rarely become difficult to replace because of their size.

They become difficult to replace because nobody knows who depends upon them.

219

Dependencies accumulate quietly over time. A report begins reading directly from a database. An operational process starts relying on a particular export. Another application discovers an API that happens to solve its immediate problem. None of these decisions is unreasonable, but together they create a web of relationships that often remains invisible until someone proposes removing the system.

By then the organisation discovers that it is not replacing one application.

It is changing dozens of pieces of work that quietly grew around it.

The exit becomes expensive because the dependencies were never made visible while they were being created.

49.5 Treat exit as a design constraint

Exit strategies are often written long after a system has entered production, if they are written at all.

By then most of the important architectural decisions have already been made.

A better approach is to treat the exit as a design constraint from the beginning.

Before a major system is approved, ask questions such as:

What information belongs to the organisation rather than the product? What interfaces would another system need to replace? Which business capabilities would be disrupted if this system disappeared? How would we migrate away if a better option became available in five years?

These questions rarely prevent a design.

They simply prevent the organisation accidentally becoming trapped by it.

49.6 Make it part of delivery

An exit strategy should not be an aspirational document produced when budgets allow.

It should be part of the definition of done.

Every major system should leave behind a short document answering three practical questions.

What would we keep?

Identify the information and capabilities that belong to the organisation rather than the product.

What would break?

Identify the systems, processes and teams that currently depend upon it.

How would we leave?

Describe, at a high level, the sequence of work required to replace the capability.

None of these documents needs to be exhaustive.

They need only be accurate enough that the organisation can make informed decisions when change eventually arrives.

Graphs & Ontologies

We typically draw large sets of complicated diagrams that box things into frozen sets of categories or relationships.

Architectural diagrams can be extremely hard to follow, and can require a large amount of maintenance and effort to keep relevant and accurate, diagrams can link to diagrams, form sets, views, and layers, but they are still fundamentally a static representation of a dynamic system.

Most enterprise architecture frameworks start with applications, databases or infrastructure and work upwards. We want to start with language. The technology stack then becomes a sequence of increasingly formal representations of the same understanding.

There aren't really any off-the-shelf tools that on their own support this kind of approach, lucky for us though, there's some really great research, outcomes, frameworks, and tools that we can leverage together to build something that can represent the organisation in a way that is more meaningful.

55.1 Upper Ontologies

An upper ontology is a set of concepts that are shared across multiple domains. It's a way to describe the workd in a way that's independent of any particular domain or context.

Utilizing upper ontologies allows us to create a shared understanding of the world that can be used across multiple domains. This is particularly useful in enterprise architecture, where we need to describe complex systems and their relationships in a way that promotes understanding, and can elide the tight coupling between the architecture and the implementation.

55

UML and other languages are upper ontologies, but they aren't the only ones. There are may other types of upper ontologies, some of which can be used together to describe the world and provide outputs which are much more inspectable and understandable from a human perspective and don't require the architect or stakeholders to hold the entirety of the system in their head to understand it.

A good starting point for upper ontologies is the [Basic Formal Ontology](https://basic-formal-ontology.org/), which is a small, but very useful upper ontology that can be used to describe the world. You can then branch off into things like OWL, which is a much more expressive upper ontology that can be used to describe complex systems and their relationships.

55.2 Turtle language

The turtle programming language is a simple, but very useful language for describing ontologies. It's a subset of the RDF specification and is used to describe the entities (classes), their properties, and the relationships between them. Turtle is a very human-readable language compared to standard RDF syntax, and it's just plaintext which makes it eminently inspectable, versionable, and easy to work with.

55.5 What this gives you that UML doesn't

UML is heavily opinionated about presentation. It forces you to use a particular set of diagrams, and a specific set of relationships and encodings of information. It doesn't give you a way to describe the world in a way that is independent of any particular domain or context.

As well, it doesn't accommodate meaning, and the encoding of decisions without a lot of extra effort. In an ontology you can describe the world in ways that align with your organisations understanding of itself, and you can extend your upper ontology to encode things that are important, decision which fed into the architecture, constraints on the architecture and organisation.

We're not just modelling what exists we're modeling stewardship.

Getting Fit

Most organisations treat architecture as something that is reviewed periodically. A design is produced, discussed, approved and eventually forgotten until the next major project begins.

Fitness testing takes a different approach.

Instead of describing architectural principles in documents and hoping people continue following them, those principles are turned into executable tests that run continuously. If an architectural property matters, it should be possible to verify that it still holds. When it no longer does, the architecture should report that fact in the same way a failing unit test reports a defect in software.

This changes architecture from something that is occasionally reviewed into something that is continuously observed.

57.1 What should be tested?

Not everything can be expressed as a fitness test, but many architectural characteristics can.

Some tests relate to software.

  • Services may only communicate through approved interfaces.
  • No application may connect directly to a production database.
  • Every API must expose authentication and observability.

Others relate to data.

57
241
  • Every Grant must have exactly one recipient.
  • Every Person must have a persistent identifier.
  • Every controlled vocabulary must contain only approved values.

Others relate to the organisation itself.

  • Every business capability must have an owner.
  • Every strategic system must have an exit plan.
  • Every ontology concept must belong to a domain and have a steward.

The important distinction is that these are no longer aspirations. They are conditions the organisation expects to remain true.

57.2 Fitness tests should reflect architectural principles

A useful fitness test begins with an architectural principle rather than a technology.

Suppose one of your principles is that strategic business concepts should never be defined by individual applications.

That principle can become a test.

  • Every business concept must exist within the shared ontology.
  • Every application must map its local representation to that concept.
  • No application may introduce a conflicting definition.

Likewise, if one of your principles is that commodity systems must remain replaceable, another test naturally follows.

  • Every strategic system must have an identified exit strategy.
  • Dependencies must be documented.
  • Replacement effort must be reviewed annually.

The test exists because the principle matters, not because the technology makes it easy to measure.

57.3 Implementing fitness tests

In a large organisation, fitness tests should exist at several different levels.

Level Example Source code Dependency rules, API conventions, security requirements Infrastructure Network policies, encryption, backup verification Data SHACL validation, controlled vocabularies, lineage completeness Architecture Capability ownership, ontology coverage, interface standards Organisation Exit plans, stewardship, review cadence, operational measures

Each level reinforces the others. A failed architectural fitness test should be treated no differently from a failed build. Both indicate that something the organisation depends upon is no longer true.

57.4 Start small
242

One of the most common mistakes is attempting to automate every architectural rule.

Begin with the handful of principles that would genuinely concern you if they stopped being true.

For example:

Principle Fitness Test Systems must be replaceable Every strategic system has an exit profile. Meaning is shared Every application maps to the organisational ontology. Data remains trustworthy Every published dataset has complete lineage. Waiting is visible Queue and hand-off times are continuously measured. Ownership is explicit Every capability has a named steward.

Five reliable tests are usually worth more than hundreds of rules nobody reviews.

57.5 Make failure visible

A fitness test only becomes useful when somebody notices it has failed.

The results should therefore be visible alongside the organisation's existing operational measures. They should appear in build pipelines, dashboards and engineering reports, rather than hidden inside architecture repositories.

Architectural drift is much easier to correct when it is discovered early. A missing owner, an undocumented dependency or an unmapped concept can often be resolved in minutes when identified immediately. Left unnoticed for several years, the same issue may require a substantial programme of work.

Fitness tests shorten that feedback loop.

57.6 Review the tests themselves

Fitness tests are built on assumptions about what the organisation values.

As those values evolve, the tests should evolve as well.

A fitness test that no longer reflects an important architectural principle should be retired. A newly adopted principle should quickly acquire a corresponding test. The architecture therefore develops alongside the organisation rather than becoming another source of outdated governance.

Like every model described in this book, the tests exist to serve the organisation. They should be revised whenever they stop doing so.

57.7 Deliverable

Identify five architectural principles that your organisation considers non-negotiable.

For each one, define:

Question Example What principle are we protecting? Every strategic capability remains replaceable. How can it be measured? Exit profile exists and has been reviewed within the last year. Where is the evidence? Architecture repository, CMDB, ontology, CI/CD pipeline. How often should it be checked? Every build, daily, weekly or quarterly. Who responds when it fails? System steward or architecture owner.

If a principle cannot be measured, discuss whether it needs to be made more precise. If it can be measured but nobody acts when it fails, it is not yet functioning as governance.

Recovering a lost function

Everything so far assumes a reader with some capacity to act. Most organisations picking up this book don't have that yet, they have an engineering function that's already exhausted, already distrusted, and already carrying the accumulated weight of every chapter in Part IV. This Part is about that starting condition specifically.

Recognising a struggling function

  • A struggling function isn't just behind, behind is normal, everyone's behind. A struggling function has already tried to fix itself and failed publicly enough that the rest of the organisation has stopped believing it can. The tell isn't the backlog, it's that nobody on the team volunteers information anymore, because volunteering information has cost people before.
  • Burnout, high turnover, and a quiet culture of blame usually travel together, and by the time leadership notices, the function has already learned that the safest move is to look busy and say as little as possible. None of the moves in Part V will work on a team operating like that, they'll just look like one more initiative to survive.
  • The test: ask the team, directly, what happened the last time someone tried to change something significant here. If the honest answer involves someone being blamed, sidelined, or quietly leaving, you're not starting from Part IV's problem, you're starting from this one.

Trust comes before method

  • Part II argued that culture and trust have to exist before the moves in Part V land. In a struggling function, that's not a sequencing note, it's the entire first phase of work, and skipping it is the single most common way transformation attempts fail twice in the same organisation.
  • You cannot credibly ask a team to name invariants, or admit where the god objects are, if the last person who asked those questions used the answers to justify a reorganisation.
