Architecture is Stewardship

My formal title doesn't include the term "architect", take from that what you will. Although I don't have the title, I've been doing the work of an architect for a long time, and through that time, and the longer period of being in software engineering, design, and operations, I've come to a few conclusions about what architecture really is, what people think it is, and what it should actually be.

Live long enough in engineering and eventually you'll become involved with various architects, and leading roles that are often described as "architectural". You'll see people with those titles treated with a kind of reverence, and in some cases even a certain amount of fear.

But as the architect in any organisation, your job isn't really to be the smartest guy in the room at all times. Architects aren't there to be a font of wisdom and knowledge, the purveyors of some infinite wisdom that only they have ascended to levels capable of tapping from the wider cosmos.

1.1 Stewardship

Being an architect is a stewardship operation. The architect is the custodian of the organisation's understanding of itself, and of the systems built to express that understanding. Their responsibility isn't just to design technology, but to ensure that the organisation's identity, language, and accumulated knowledge survive the departure of individuals, teh replacement of systems, organisational upheaval, and the passage of time itself.

You're not here to produce diagrams or approve technology choices. You're here to preserve the organisation's ability to understand itself.

Organisations

We're going to talk about organisations, what they are, how they work, and how they don't work. How organisations forget, and struggle to learn lessons from past experience.

What is an Organisation?

Organisations are funny things. You can call just about anything an organisation these days. A group of people who get together to play a game, even a book club is an organisation. But when we talk about organisations in the context of business, we're talking about something a bit more specific.

2.1 Stripping it back

When we strip away all the bits that we commonly, and misguidedly, think of as an organisation:

  • A building
  • Tax Status
  • Legal structures
  • the org chart

you're left with a collection of people maintining a shared understanding while pursuing a shared purpose. All these other pieces that orbit around that, are really only support structures put into place to help the people in the orgaqnisation achieve that shared purpose.

2.2 Defining an Organisation

We need to take the definition of an organisation seriously, because most of what this book argues over it's many parts and chapters follows on from how we define the organisation.

Because if an organisation is fundamentally a shared understanding, then losing that understanding is essentially losing the organisation itself, not just its documentation, and protecting it it becomes the actual job of architecture, not a side effect of it.

Two kinds of organisations

  • Some organisations succeed by executing the same thing more efficiently each year, and for them stability is a genuine asset. Others, funders, humanitarian responders, universities, succeed by continually becoming something they weren't before, and treating that as dysfunction misreads what the organisation is actually for.
  • Most architecture practice was built inside the first kind of organisation and imported wholesale into the second, without anyone checking whether the assumptions travelled. Worth working out, honestly, which kind of organisation you're actually in before adopting anyone's playbook, including this book's.

Why meaning has to come first

People talk about Applications, Grants, Awards, Employees, Projects, Customers and Partners as though the meaning of those words were absolutely obvious. Most of the time they're obvious. Right up until someone from another department joins the conversation. Suddenly the same word means something slightly different depending on who's speaking, or two different words turn out to describe the exact same thing.

These disagreements rarely announce themselves as disagreements about language. They arrive disguised as reporting problems, failed integrations, awkward meetings and endless debates about which system is correct. By the time technology becomes invovled, the misunderstanding has usually exited for years if not decades.

Most organisations that believe they have a technology problem are actually dealing with a language problem.

Technology can't solve that ambiguity because technology has no opinin about meaning. A database happily stores two records labelled "Grant" without caring whether they represent the same concept. An API will faithfully return whatever values it was given. A reporting platform aggregates numbers regardless of whether the people reading them agree on what htose numbers actually mean. Storage systems are designed to preserve data, not understanding.

Data isn't reality, it's a representation of reality.

A customer record isn't a customer. A grant record isn't a grant. They're observations made by a particular system, for a particular purpose, at an even more particular point in time. Mistaking the existence of data for the existence of shared understanding is where many architectural problems start.

The orgasnisation itself exists independently of whatever system happens to be recording it this year.

5
8

Long before a CRM existed there were customers, long after today's ERP is replaced there will still be invoices, budgets, and suppliers. Systems come and go because they're tools. The organisation is the thing those tools are attempting to describe.

That means the most valuable thing an organisation possesses isn't its software, or databases, or even the information stored inside of them.

It's the shared understanding that allows thousands of people to look at the same thing, same word, and mean the same thing.

Everything else changes. Everything else is replaced, or reorganised, rewritten. Vendors disappear, technology evolves. Shared meaning is the one thing that has to survive every one of those changes, because without it the organisation gradually loses the ability to describe itself consistently.

Archiecture really begins much earlier than the technology.

Before deciding how information should be stored, transferred, or secured, and organisation has to decide what the informaiton actually means. That understanding can't remain implicit, living in conversations and institutional memory. It has to become something the organisation can point to, discuss, refine and preserve.

Only then can technology begin to faithfully representing the organisation instead of continually redefining it.

  • Every organisation has its own language, "Grant," "Application," "Employee," and it sounds obvious until two departments turn out to mean different things by the same word, or use different words for the same thing. Most organisations that think they have a technology problem actually have a language problem, and the technology just inherits whatever ambiguity was already there.
  • Data isn't reality, it's a representation, and storage systems are deliberately indifferent to what the values they hold actually mean; mistaking the existence of data for the existence of shared understanding is where most of this goes wrong. Consensus has to be built deliberately and drawn out into the open, because software, and increasingly AI, will confidently guess at meaning the moment nobody's agreed on it; chapters 28 and 29 turn this into a concrete method.
  • Meaning is the only real asset in any of this, everything else, the org chart, the technology, the process, changes constantly; meaning is the thing you're actually trying to preserve across all of that change. The organisation itself exists independently of whatever systems happen to be recording it this year, the systems are temporary observations of something real, not the real thing.

Truth

In the previous chapter we argued that architecture begins with meaning. Before an organisation can build software, integrate systems or produce reports, it first has to agree on waht the things inside it actually are.

Truth is an important problem.

Meaning asks whether everyone is talking about the same thing.

Trust asks whether what they are saying about it is still honest.

Those two ideas are often confused beacuse they fail in similar ways. An organisation might agree perfectly on waht an Application or a Grant is while slowly losing the ability to describe what's actually happening to eithe of them. The language remains stable, but reality quietly drifts away from the story being told about it.

That separation rarely happens deliberately, and usually not all at once, but it does begin with small incentives.

A metric becomes a target, and the moment it does people begin optimising for the number instead of the thing the number was supposed to measure. Reports become political because poor results have consequences for whoever owns them. Dashboards stop asking what is happening and start presenting whichever version of events is least likely to cause uncomfortable conversations.

Definitions drift for similar reasons. A report that once measured one thing gradually measures something slightly different ebcasue the old definition no longer tells a flattering story. Exceptions accumulate. Caveats multiple. Eventually two reports disagree, not because either contains incorrect data, but because they're no longer attempting to describe the same reality.

6
10

Absolutely none of this requires people to be dishonest or to have done this wholly out of malice.

Most of this emerges from perfectly reasonable people responding perfectly understandable incentives. That's precisely why it's so difficult to notive it while it's happening. There's usually moments though when it all becomes visible. Generally when your meetings start with "Which numbers are right?".

Again, it's easy to chock this up as a technical problem, but it's really not.

People stop trusting data long before they stop collecting it. Once confidence has been lost, every report becomes negotiable, every dahboard becomes suspect, and every decision begins with an argument about evidence rather than a decision itself. An organsisation can continue functioning like this for years, even decades, but it's no longer navigating by observation. It's navigating by competing interpretations of reality.

There's a profound lesson hiding inside that pattern, and it's that our architecture isn't just how we organise our systems.

It's the discipline of preserbing an honest description of reality, even when that reality is inconvenient or unflattering.

LIneage, provenance, audit trails, semantic models, and goverance are often justified in terms of compliance, regulation, and operational efficiency. Those are worthwhile outcomes, but they're secondary. Their deeper purpose is much simpler: they help an organisation remain capable of telling itself the truth.

That truth will sometimes be uncomfortable.

  • A programme is failing.
  • A migration is behind schedule
  • A process no longer works
  • A metric was measuring the wrong thing all along

An architecture that can't represent those realities is honeslty not protecting the organisation. It's protecting the organisation from itself. Every organisation eventually becomes separated from reality.

Processes become ritual. Metrics become performance. Systems become assumptions. Documentation becomes mythology.

The distance grows slowly enough that nobody notices from one month to the next, yet over years it becomes enormous.

The work of architecture is not to eliminate that distance completely—that is impossible.

It is to shorten it.

To build systems that make reality visible again.

To preserve not only shared meaning, but shared truth.

Because an organisation that can no longer describe itself honestly has already lost something far more valuable than accurate reports.

It has lost the ability to learn.

Identity

  • Ask most organisations what they have, and you'll get an inventory, systems, data, headcount, budget. Ask what they are, and the answer is much harder, and much more durable. The mission survives reorganisations, technology migrations, and complete turnover of staff, in a way no system or dataset ever does; identity is the most permanent thing an organisation has.
  • That's why the question worth asking, constantly, is "what are we", not "what data do we have". Meaning, from chapter 5, explains what that identity actually consists of, the concepts, the language, the things that matter. Time, from chapter 8, explains how that identity persists and changes, state tracked over time is really identity being observed as it moves. Identity survives change, but only if something is actually tracking what changed and why.

Time is architectures real material

Building are made of steel and concrete. Software is made of code. Architecture, the kind this book is actually about, is made of time.

Every architectural decision spends tomorrow.

Sometimes deliberately, more often than not, accidentally.

The systems we inherit are less a snapshot of engineering than a fossil record of every compromise, assumption, and shortcut that survived long enough to become normal. What we call architecture is really the accumulated relationships between an organisation and time itself.

This chapter argues that time is architecture's true material. Everything else is simply the medium through which it acts.

8.1 Buildings stand still, organisations don't

A bridge does not wake up one morning and decide it's not a hospital.

An organisation can.

Hospitals become research institutes, universities become global charities, companies discover new markets, governments change policy, and crises appear without any sort of formal invitation.

Architecture is therefore can't simply describe what exists.

8
14

It needs to allow the organisation to become something that doesn't exist yet.

That's why architecture is measured less by how well today's systems fit totday's organisation, and more by how cheaply tomorrow's organisation can emerge.

8.2 Every decision purchases a future

Architectural decisions are often described as "investments".

They're closer to a mortgage than anything else. Every choice commits future engineers to living with its consequences.

Some of those decision will make tomorrow cheaper, others though will quietly make every future change slightly more expensive until nobody remembers where the cost came from.

Good architecture isn't the absence of future work, it's choosing which future work you're willing to pay for 1.

1

Technical debt is only one form of debt.

Organisations also accumulate semantic debt, organisational debt, governance debt, and historical debt.

8.3 Stat is time made explicit

Most systems never model state, they infer it, a checkbox, status field, date. Maybe three booleans that are supposed to agree.

Somebody writes documentation explaining how to recontruct the real state from the combination of values.

That documentation immediately begins to age.

State isn't simply a field, it's identity observed through time. Every meaningful thing in an organisaiton moves through recognizable states.

  • An Application is drafted
  • Submitted
  • Reviewed
  • Awarded
  • Closed

The transitions between those states are important, just as important as the states themselves. Without explicit transitions, two people can legitimately disagree abhout what is happening while looking at the exact same record.

That disagreement is fundamentally architectural, not technical.

If two ssytesm disagree about state, ask which transition they disagree about.

The answer is usually more revealing than comparing the data itself.

8.4 The cost of yesterday

One of the strangest properties of organisations is that they negotiate with people who are no longer actually in the organisation.

A schema exists because sombody once chose it, and approval exisets because somebnody once worried.

Years latery, none of those contexts actually remain. Only the decision has survived, so the architecture in these places isn't coupled just to the systems which implement it, it's coupled to the history of the organisation itself.

Changing today's organisation often requries renegotiating decisions made by people whose names people don't even remember, and that's an extraordinarily expensive way to carry institutional memory.

Why organisations forget

Organisations don't forget because the people inside of them are careless, or because sombody failed to write enough documentation. They forget because organisations are constantly changing while their understanding of themselves is carried by people, systems, and habits that are all temporary.

A decade after a major decision has been made, the decision often remains long after the reasons behind it have disappeared. Systems still behave in peculiar ways. Reports will disagree. Approval processes still exist. Integrations still run every night.

Aks why, and the answer is usually the same: "We've always done it this way".

That's rarely an explanation, and it's evidence that the explanation has more or less already been lost to time.

Institutional memory doesn't disappear in one dramatic event. It erodes slowly through dozens of different mechanisms, each of them individually reasonable.

  • People leave, teams get reorganised, systems get replaced, consultants arrive and depart, and the reasons behind old decisions leave with them. A few years later nobody remembers why two reports disagree, why three systems define "Application" differently, or why one field has behaved strangely for the last eight years, the context evaporated along with whoever used to carry it.
  • That's why the same mistakes get repeated, not because anyone's careless, but because the lesson was only ever stored in people, and every reorganisation disperses the people who held it. Most organisations store enormous amounts of data, and very little of their actual understanding, which is a different thing, and losing the second is what makes losing the first so expensive; chapter 26 picks this up as an architectural problem, not just an organisational one.

9.1 People leave

The most obvious form of forgetting is also the simplest.

  • People retire
  • People resign
  • Consultants finish their contracts
  • Contractors hand over their laptops

Along with all these people goes a remarkable amount of understanding that was never written down, more often than not because no one realised it needed to be.

  • Strange validation rules
  • The reason two systems can't be merged
  • Why one report deliberately excludes a particular department
  • Why an interface retries exactly three times

None of these decisions seem important until the person who understood them is gone. The major mistake here is believing that knowledge belongs to the individuals themselves. In healthy organisations, individuals discover understanding, and then the organisation preserves it in some meaningful way.

9.2 Teams change shape

Organisations reorganise constantly, departments merge, functions split, repsonsibilities move, reporting lines change. Each reorganisation breaks small chains of communication that nobofy realised existed.

People who once solved problems together now sit in different floors with different incentives, different managers, and different piorities. Nothing has been forgotten deliberately.

Yet the organisation's understanding becomes fragmented just because the conversations that sustained it are no longer happening naturall through some course of operations.

9.3 Systems replace systems

The technology inside of an organisation invariably changes faster than the organisation itself.

  • CRMs are replaced
  • ERPs are upgraded
  • Data warehouses are rebuilt

All the data mostly survives all this change, but the meaning mostly doesn't. A migration faithfully copies every field out of a system while leaving behind the reasons those fields existed in the first place, or the years of decisions which accreted into that schema.

Years later the new system contains every value from the old one, but nobody remembers why three nearly identical statuses survived the migration.

The organisation preserved the information, but it wholly lost the meaning.

19
9.4 Documentation ages

Documentation always feels permanent, but it isn't.

You need to see things like wiki pages, as kicking off a slow descent into rot from the moment that they're saved. They age extremely rapidly.

The organisation changes, the work changes, systems are modified, processes evolve, exceptions accumulate and the document remains exactly where it was, looking just as authoritative as it did when it was actually correct.

Written knowledge doesn't really have a way to announce when it's wrong. It just becomes increasingly incomplete until people stop trusting it, and it's ultimately deleted.

9.5 Success makes assumptions invisible

Some of the most dangerous knowledge disappears precisely because nothing has gone wrong. Weird though huh?

You've built some process that works, or an extremely stable and depdendable integration. But when something is successful no one tends to ask why it was successful. It's enough that it works.

Eventually everyone just assumes that it's simply how things are supposed to work. Any original trade-offs disappear from memory, constraints become invisible, temporary decisions and workarounds become permanent.

Success has a way of hiding the assumptions that made it possible.

9.6 Language drifts

Your organisation will more or less continue on using the same words through time, but their meanins may slowly change and drift from the original intent.

One department might use "Application" to mean the funding request, and another might treat the entire lifecycle of that request as an "Application". A third can even inherit both meanings and not even realize they have.

No single person suddenly decides that they're going to redefine a word, it's shaped, by the systems, the processes, by misuse, and by incentives.

It drifts slowly enough that no one really notices, as people arrive and leave, and old contexts disappear.

The problem is that every discussion starts out with a an apparent agreement about what language means, but hides a lot of subtle disagreements in the corners.

We all still speak, and we manage to lumber along, but the organisation at a higher level stops being able to understand itself.Z

9.7 Incentives rewrite history

Not all forgetting is accidental.

Sometimes organisations slowly edit their own memories.

20

Projects that overran become success stories.

Metrics that once exposed problems quietly disappear.

Reports change definitions because the previous definition produced uncomfortable numbers.

Nobody sets out to falsify history.

People simply respond to incentives.

Over time the organisation remembers the version of events that was easiest to live with rather than the one that was most useful to learn from.

That is a particularly dangerous form of forgetting because it feels like certainty.

9.8 Layers hide intent

Every architectural decision solves a problem.

Eventually another decision solves the problems created by the first.

Then another.

And another.

Years later the organisation sees only the accumulated layers.

The original intention has disappeared beneath them.

The architecture survives.

The reasoning does not.

Eventually nobody knows which parts of the system are fundamental and which exist only to support decisions made long ago.

9.9 Forgetting is the default mode

None of these failure modes require incompetence.

In fact, they are often the consequence of perfectly healthy behaviour.

People move on.

Technology improves.

Teams reorganise.

Businesses change.

Documentation ages.

Language evolves.

The remarkable thing is not that organisations forget.

It is that they remember anything at all.

That is why stewardship matters.

The role of architecture is not simply to design systems.

21

It is to preserve understanding against all of the forces that naturally erase it.

Because organisations store vast amounts of information.

What they struggle to preserve is the understanding that gives that information meaning.

The chapters that follow build on this idea. If meaning is worth preserving, and forgetting is inevitable, then architecture cannot be judged solely by the systems it produces. It must also be judged by how effectively it allows an organisation to remember itself through change.

Knowledge decay

In the previous chapter we looked at how organisations forget.

People leave.

Teams reorganise.

Systems are replaced.

Knowledge disappears because the people carrying it disappear.

Knowledge decay is a different problem.

Nobody has to leave.

The organisation can lose understanding while every member of the team remains exactly where they are.

The system changes.

The organisation changes.

Reality changes.

The knowledge stays exactly where it was.

10.1 Knowledge Has a Half-Life

We often talk about documentation as though it were permanent.

It isn't.

A runbook is accurate on Tuesday.

10
23

An engineer improves the deployment on Wednesday.

By Thursday the runbook is slightly wrong.

Nobody notices.

Multiply that process by years rather than days and almost every organisation accumulates documentation that is technically still present but no longer describes the systems people actually operate.

Knowledge ages.

The only question is whether anyone notices before somebody depends on it.

10.2 Reality Keeps Moving

Knowledge decays because reality refuses to stand still.

A team changes its process.

A regulation changes.

A supplier introduces a new product.

An API gains another field.

A definition evolves.

A business starts operating in another country.

Every one of those changes is perfectly reasonable.

Collectively they mean that every document describing "how things currently work" immediately begins drifting away from reality.

The organisation changes continuously.

The documentation changes occasionally.

The gap between the two is where decay lives.

10.3 Authority Outlives Accuracy

One of the dangerous properties of written knowledge is that it continues looking authoritative long after it stops being correct.

A wiki page written five years ago looks exactly like one written yesterday.

A process diagram gives no indication that half the systems it references have already been replaced.

A glossary still defines concepts nobody uses.

Nothing announces the moment a document becomes misleading.

It simply sits there, accumulating confidence it no longer deserves.

The appearance of certainty often survives accuracy by years.

24
10.4 The Silent Failure

Most failures announce themselves.

Systems crash.

Reports fail.

Applications throw errors.

Knowledge decay is quieter.

People stop using documentation because they no longer trust it.

Instead they ask whoever sits nearby.

Then whoever has been there longest.

Eventually the organisation develops an informal understanding that the documentation cannot be relied upon.

Nobody declares this officially.

It simply becomes the culture.

Ironically, organisations often respond by writing more documentation.

The problem was rarely the quantity.

It was that nobody was responsible for keeping it alive.

10.5 Knowledge Without Use Dies

There is another reason knowledge decays.

Unused knowledge isn't exercised.

A disaster recovery plan that has never been rehearsed.

A runbook that nobody follows.

A glossary that nobody references.

An architecture document that no system depends upon.

These things become historical artefacts rather than operational knowledge.

The fastest way for knowledge to become outdated is for it to stop being part of everyday work.

Knowledge survives through use far more reliably than through storage.

10.6 Treat Knowledge Like Food

Organisations treat documentation like concrete.

They should treat it like food.

Every description of "how things currently work" has an expiry date.

25

Some knowledge lasts decades.

A mission statement may remain valid for generations.

Other knowledge has a lifetime measured in weeks.

Deployment procedures.

Integration instructions.

API contracts.

Operational playbooks.

The mistake is assuming they all age at the same rate.

Healthy organisations expect knowledge to expire.

They build review, revision and replacement into the work itself.

10.7 Stewardship Includes Maintenance

Architecture is often associated with creation.

Designing systems.

Producing models.

Writing standards.

Stewardship is equally concerned with maintenance.

Not simply maintaining software.

Maintaining understanding.

Every description of the organisation should be assumed to be drifting unless there is active evidence that it is not.

That applies equally to glossaries, architectural diagrams, process maps, ontologies and operational documentation.

The question is never

"Was this correct?"

The more useful question is

"Why should we believe it is still correct today?"

10.8 Decay Is Inevitable

Knowledge does not decay because people are careless.

It decays because organisations are alive.

Living things change.

Every change leaves yesterday's understanding slightly less complete than it was before.

The answer is not to document everything.

26

Nor is it to endlessly rewrite documentation.

It is to build architecture that keeps knowledge connected to the systems, decisions and work it describes, so that changes naturally pull understanding along with them instead of leaving it behind.

Because knowledge that can only become older is already beginning to disappear.

  • Losing people is only half the problem, chapter 9 covers that. The other half is that knowledge decays even when nobody leaves: a wiki page, a glossary entry, a runbook, all correct the day they were written, and all silently wrong a year later because the system or the organisation around them kept moving and nobody's job was to notice.
  • Nothing announces the moment written knowledge crosses from true to false, it just sits there looking exactly as authoritative as it did when it was accurate. Worth treating anything that describes "how this currently works" the same way you'd treat a perishable good, with an expectation of when it needs checking, not an assumption that it's still true because it was true once.
27

Anatomy not civil engineering

  • Organisations are living things, not structures, they heal, learn, forget, adapt, scar, and compensate, the way a body does. Architecture for an organisation that mostly repeats itself can behave like civil engineering, build it once, keep it standing. Architecture for an organisation built to keep becoming something else has to behave more like anatomy, understanding something that's alive, self-repairing, and constantly renegotiating its own shape.
  • Identity, from chapter 7, is what holds a body together while every cell in it turns over, the thing that's still recognisably the same organism a decade later. Meaning, from chapter 5, is what that identity actually consists of. Time, from chapter 8, is how a body tracks its own condition well enough to heal correctly instead of scarring over the wrong thing. Forgetting, from chapter 9, and decay, from chapter 10, are just what a body does when nobody maintains the systems that keep it healthy, not failures, just neglect.
  • None of this is a metaphor you can fully formalise, and that's the point. Civil engineers ask whether it will stand. Anatomists ask whether it's healthy, and whether it can heal. The rest of this book is mostly the second question, asked about an organisation instead of a body.

Culture

Socio-technical concerns aren't a soft add-on to architecture, they're increasingly recognised as part of it. Get this Part right and the moves in Part V land easily; get it wrong and no move will save you.

Build a culture

  • Autonomy doesn't survive on an org chart or a values statement, it survives on whether people feel like genuine members with a stake, with peers they trust and are judged by, rather than employees waiting on permission from someone above them.
  • The moment "who's your team" narrows to "who's your manager," judgement narrows with it. Worth noticing, quietly, what people actually believe would happen to them if they made a reasonable call and it went wrong, that answer is the real culture, whatever the values statement says.
  • Local knowledge is sacred for the same reason, no central planner, however capable, can ever know as much about a specific problem as the people closest to it. Architecture's job isn't to centralise that knowledge, it's to maximise how much of it survives and gets acted on.
12
30

Borrowing complexity

  • Deliberately choosing the complexity we want to inherit.
13
31

Humility

  • Reality is always richer than any model of it, and every model, however careful, is incomplete by construction; an ontology is a set of promises about what matters enough to name, not a claim to have captured everything that exists. Chapter 28 already puts it as the map is not the territory, humility is what happens when you actually let that sentence change how you build, instead of just agreeing with it as an aphorism.
  • The temptation, once a model is built, is to start treating it as reality itself, arguing with a customer about whether they're really a "Lead" or an "Opportunity" instead of asking what they actually need. That's not a training problem, it's a structural one, every abstraction quietly invites people to forget it was ever a choice, and the organisations that stay honest are the ones that keep asking what the model is leaving out, not just whether it's internally consistent.
  • Humility, here, isn't a virtue you cultivate in people, it's a discipline you build into the architecture itself: version your ontology instead of treating it as finished, leave room for concepts you haven't named yet, and treat every "that doesn't fit the model" moment as information about reality, not a data quality problem to be coerced away. Truth, from chapter 6, is about whether your reporting stays honest, this is the same commitment aimed one level up, at whether your model of the world stays honest about how much of the world it doesn't yet describe. Worth asking, of any model your organisation depends on, what's the last thing reality did that didn't fit, and whether anyone noticed.
14
32

Build Trust

  • Advanced militaries learned decades ago that complex operations can't wait for every decision to flow back through a central command post, so the centre's job changed to providing intent, doctrine, and support rather than control. Good architecture works the same way, sustaining movement instead of directing every movement.
  • Architecture defaults to permission because permission is easy to audit and trust is not, so approval steps accumulate quietly until decisions need sign-off from people who aren't accountable for the outcome. Somewhere in your organisation, an approval step is standing in for evidence nobody's bothered to collect.
15
33

Fix the incentives

  • Consultants are rewarded for recommending transformation, vendors for selling, procurement for buying established products, management for reducing perceived risk, boards for visible governance, and nobody is rewarded for quietly building internal capability. None of that is malice, it's incentives aimed at something other than the organisation's actual interest.
  • Worth asking plainly, for any system that's become complicated, who benefits if it stays that way, and checking whether that's the same party currently extending it.
16
34

Structure teams

  • Organisations calcify constraints layer by layer, the way sediment builds a landscape. Centralising a capability looks sensible from a risk perspective, and for some capabilities genuinely is, but for most it just relocates the complexity until a handful of teams carry the accumulated needs of the whole organisation, indispensable and a bottleneck in the same breath.
  • Scaling those teams to relieve the pressure usually adds coordination overhead faster than it adds capacity. Worth checking whether your platform team's boundaries were drawn around a stable concept, or around whichever system happens to need the most help this quarter.

Teams

Chapter 17 was about where team boundaries should sit. This Part is about how teams actually form and change once boundaries exist. Valve's employee handbook, one of the few management documents ever made fully public, is the clearest working example of the ideas below, and it's worth reading in full alongside this Part.

Let teams form around work

  • Every new initiative typically needs a charter, a sponsor, and a headcount request before anyone can start, and the opportunity has often moved on by the time it's approved. Valve describes an alternative it calls cabals, self-organising groups that form because people choose the work, not because an org chart assigned it, and dissolve once the work is done.
  • Self-organising isn't the same as unmanaged, a cabal still needs a clear owner and shared intent. For your next piece of cross-team work, try forming a temporary, named group of volunteers with an explicit end condition instead of requesting a permanent team.
18
37

Let people move

  • Someone who wants to work on a different problem usually has to go through a formal transfer, or simply can't, because headcount belongs to a team rather than a person. Valve made mobility literal with desks on wheels, people move toward the work that has momentum by default, and that movement is itself a live signal of where energy and value actually sit.
  • Treat long stretches where nobody moves at all as a warning sign rather than evidence of stability, and treat people who do move around as reporting something useful, not as being disloyal.
19
38

Let engineers drive capability

  • People outside software engineering have largely been sold the idea that writing code is the expensive, difficult part. It usually isn't anymore, understanding the organisation is. Treating engineering purely as a delivery arm for someone else's fully-specified requirements outsources the one part that was actually valuable.
  • Product-oriented teams, durable teams that own a capability instead of time-boxed projects, are the right shape, but only if engineering sits inside that ownership instead of underneath it. Worth checking how many capabilities your organisation has shipped that didn't start as someone else's requirement.

The Problem

Still to be written.

Why architecture loses

  • Architecture should be judged by how little effort it takes for an organisation to become something it wasn't designed to be, not by how well it describes what already exists.
  • Field changes take months, every reorg breaks the approval chain, and nobody can say what "Awarded" actually means, that's usually an organisational problem wearing a technology costume.
  • The test: pick one system and ask, if I deleted this tomorrow, what would stop working, and could I explain why. If that stings, the rest of the book is the fix, and you don't need to fix it all at once.
21
41

Entropy is the default

  • Everything drifts toward disorder unless energy is actively spent resisting it, and organisations, and every system inside them, are no exception. Traditional EA tries to fight this head on by reducing entropy directly, standardising everything into stillness, but stillness was never actually available to an organisation whose job is to keep changing.
  • The honest failure mode isn't that entropy wins, it always does eventually, the failure is designing as though it won't, and then being surprised when maintenance, cleanup, and simplification never get funded because none of it looks like new capability.
  • The test: look at whatever "keeps the lights on" work is currently unfunded or perpetually deprioritised in your organisation. That list is entropy's invoice, arriving on schedule.
22
42

How you arrive here

  • Nobody designs a system with an unauditable centre of gravity on purpose. You arrive there one reasonable decision at a time, each one made inside the constraints the last one left behind, until nobody in the room can question the first decision anymore.
  • Blaming the vendor or the last team misses this, the layering is the actual cause, and it compounds quietly for years before anyone notices.
  • The exercise: trace one painful system back through its last five significant changes, and name the constraint each one was working around. Part V is a sequence of counter-moves for exactly this reason, not one redesign.
23
43

The accumulation no one caused

  • Chapter 23 traced this for one system. It's the same pattern at the scale of the whole organisation: exceptions, temporary fixes, duplicated glossaries, local optimisations, abandoned dashboards and service accounts, all of it accretes quietly, and almost never through a single decision anyone could point to and call a mistake.
  • Every one of those additions was reasonable at the time, to the person adding it. Nobody sets out to embalm the organisation in yesterday's decisions, they're just trying to ship something, and the trail of detritus behind them becomes somebody else's problem to notice, eventually.
  • The exercise: list everything that exists in your organisation because it was easier to add than to remove, old processes, unwatched mailboxes, orphaned automation rules, dashboards nobody reads. Nobody owns that list, which is exactly why it keeps growing.
24
44

Complexity is borrowed

  • Nobody creates complexity deliberately, they borrow it. From vendors, whose products carry years of other customers' requirements. From regulation, which encodes constraints nobody in the room actually negotiated. From history, decisions made for an organisation that no longer exists, chapter 26 covers that specifically. From previous systems, which leave behind integrations that only make sense because of a product retired years ago. From other organisations, whose approach gets adopted wholesale because it's safer than justifying something different.
  • None of those sources are avoidable, an organisation that borrows no complexity at all isn't doing anything interesting. The actual job of architecture is deciding which complexity is worth borrowing, and being honest that every yes is a debt, not a gift.
  • The test: for your most complicated system, trace its complexity back to a source, vendor, regulation, history, a previous decision, another organisation's pattern, and ask whether that borrowing was ever actually evaluated, or just inherited by default.
25
45

The cost of changing your mind

  • We talk endlessly about coupling between systems, and almost never about coupling across time. If changing today's organisation requires re-litigating decisions made fifteen years ago, for reasons nobody currently working there was present for, your architecture is coupled to history just as tightly as it could ever be coupled to another system.
  • Every architectural decision is made in a context, a set of priorities, a technology landscape, a set of assumptions about the future, that was reasonable when it was made. The problem isn't that those decisions were wrong, it's that organisations keep negotiating with their own past long after the context that justified it has gone; chapter 9 covered why the reasons behind those decisions get forgotten, this is what that forgetting costs architecturally.
  • The test: for your most painful system, ask how much it would cost to change your mind about one foundational decision made when it was built, not to patch around it, to actually reverse it. Great architecture is measured by how cheap that answer is, because the alternative is an organisation that can only ever move in the direction its old decisions already committed it to.

VI
The Core Moves

Do these in order the first time. Each one is independently useful even if you stop after one.

Define invariants

  • Signal: nobody can agree what "Grant" or "Awarded" means without pointing at a specific field in a specific system
  • Principle: identity, people, organisations, money, relationships, authority, provenance, and audit change slowly; everything else is implementation
  • Practice: write down, in one page, your organisation's five to eight invariants, in plain language, independent of any system
  • Pitfall: confusing an invariant with a current process or org chart, both of which will change
  • Takeaway: do this chapter before any other move, everything after it assumes you've named these
27
48

Give meaning a language

  • Signal: everyone agrees they're talking about "applications," but two departments already define the word differently, and neither definition is written down
  • Principle: describe the organisation as concepts, attributes, and relationships before you describe a single table; the concepts exist before whatever system stores them does. A CRM, an ERP, a database are all just maps, imperfect ones, full of trade-offs, and the map is not the territory; you build an ontology in the first place because maps change constantly and the reality underneath them mostly doesn't
  • Practice: for each invariant from chapter 27, list its attributes, name its relationships to the other invariants explicitly instead of leaving them to be inferred from a foreign key, and turn any field that should only hold a fixed set of values into a controlled vocabulary; group related concepts into a handful of named domains so the model has somewhere to grow
  • Pitfall: modelling columns instead of concepts, and letting whichever system was built first quietly become the definition everyone else has to match
  • Takeaway: a one-page glossary of concepts, attributes, and relationships is worth more than a data dictionary generated from a database; one version of this, run in production, calls a concept a DirectClass, but the name matters less than doing it
28
49

Make meaning operational

  • Signal: the glossary from chapter 28 is three months old and already drifting, because nothing actually depends on it
  • Principle: an ontology nothing depends on is documentation; it becomes architecture once real software is built on the mapping between a concept and where its values actually live. Every abstraction you create, a database, an API, a process, a concept, is a promise that the thing means something and will keep meaning it, and breaking that promise later is never cheap
  • Practice: pick one concept, map each of its attributes to the physical field holding its value today, and build one small thing, a report, an API, a permission, from that mapping instead of from the raw table
  • Pitfall: treating the mapping as a one-off migration exercise instead of the thing every future schema change has to update
  • Takeaway: apply chapter 21's test to the mapping itself, if you deleted it tomorrow and nothing broke, it isn't operational yet
29
50

Commodity vs. Strategic

  • Signal: you're customising a CRM or ERP to represent something it was never built to represent
  • Principle: buy the capability everyone needs the same way; build the capability that makes your understanding of your organisation an advantage. Chapter 25 covers where that complexity actually comes from, and how to decide what's worth taking on
  • Practice: list your major systems, mark each as commodity or strategic, flag any strategic capability currently living inside a commodity tool
  • Pitfall: treating buy-versus-build as a per-system choice instead of a per-capability one
  • Takeaway: any strategic capability flagged inside a commodity tool is your next migration candidate, see Part VI
30
51

Design the exit

  • Signal: nobody can describe how you'd remove a system without describing a crisis
  • Principle: if you can't describe how to replace something, you haven't finished designing it
  • Practice: for every major system, write down what data you'd keep, what would break, and how long it would take to leave
  • Pitfall: treating the exit path as a someday-document instead of a go-live requirement
  • Takeaway: no exit path, no go-live, make it a checklist item, not an aspiration
31
52

Build the interface layer

  • Signal: users know, and complain about, which specific system underneath is slow this week
  • Principle: an interface should present one coherent system and buffer people from whatever is changing underneath it
  • Practice: take your highest-traffic user journey and check whether it touches the underlying system's schema directly, or through an interface; where chapter 29's mapping already exists, check whether the interface can be generated from it instead of hand-built
  • Pitfall: bolting the interface on after the system is already load-bearing, instead of designing it in from day one
  • Takeaway: once meaning is mapped, a surprising amount of interface, APIs, reports, permissions, stops being hand-written and starts being derived
32
53

Measure waiting

  • Signal: your dashboards track uptime and incidents; nobody tracks how long a routine change actually takes
  • Principle: queue time, lead time for change, and dependency count are architectural signals, not just operational ones
  • Practice: pick three metrics from this chapter and start tracking them this quarter, even by hand
  • Pitfall: measuring team velocity instead of organisational lead time, they are not the same number
  • Takeaway: you can't fix what you don't measure, and most organisations aren't measuring the thing that's actually hurting them

Leave it better than you found it

Every architect inherits an organisation they didn't create.

Every architect leaves one behind.

The measure isn't whether the architecture is complete.

It's whether the next steward inherits something that's easier to understand than you inherited.

Recognize the failure modes

  • The failure modes repeat across organisations. The classic vendor arguments, "don't reinvent the wheel," "it's industry standard," "it's more secure," "thousands of organisations already use it," "it's cheaper," "nobody gets fired for buying it," and the classic knee-jerk fixes, more governance, more headcount, another bolted-on tool, more custom fields, more documentation, eventually a full replatform, are both recognisable in advance.
  • Score your own organisation against each pattern with a one-page diagnostic. This chapter is a mirror, not a fix, the fix is chapters 12 to 20 and chapters 27 to 33.

Migrate an existing system

  • Most organisations reading this book already have an embalmed system, not a blank page, and migration has its own sequencing, separate from designing something fresh.
  • Name the invariants hiding inside the current system (chapter 27), give them a language (chapter 28), map them onto what already exists (chapter 29), put an interface in front of it (chapter 32), then plan the exit (chapter 31), in that order. Replacing one vendor with another and calling it a migration skips all of this.
  • You don't need to fix everything at once, pick the one system costing you most on chapter 33's metrics, and start there.

VIII
Recovering a lost function

Everything so far assumes a reader with some capacity to act. Most organisations picking up this book don't have that yet, they have an engineering function that's already exhausted, already distrusted, and already carrying the accumulated weight of every chapter in Part IV. This Part is about that starting condition specifically.

Recognising a struggling function

  • A struggling function isn't just behind, behind is normal, everyone's behind. A struggling function has already tried to fix itself and failed publicly enough that the rest of the organisation has stopped believing it can. The tell isn't the backlog, it's that nobody on the team volunteers information anymore, because volunteering information has cost people before.
  • Burnout, high turnover, and a quiet culture of blame usually travel together, and by the time leadership notices, the function has already learned that the safest move is to look busy and say as little as possible. None of the moves in Part V will work on a team operating like that, they'll just look like one more initiative to survive.
  • The test: ask the team, directly, what happened the last time someone tried to change something significant here. If the honest answer involves someone being blamed, sidelined, or quietly leaving, you're not starting from Part IV's problem, you're starting from this one.
40
63

Trust comes before method

  • Part II argued that culture and trust have to exist before the moves in Part V land. In a struggling function, that's not a sequencing note, it's the entire first phase of work, and skipping it is the single most common way transformation attempts fail twice in the same organisation.
  • You cannot credibly ask a team to name invariants, or admit where the god objects are, if the last person who asked those questions used the answers to justify a reorganisation. Rebuilding that trust looks unglamorous, showing up, following through on small commitments, removing one piece of friction without asking for anything in return, and it has to happen before you introduce a single concept from Part V.
  • The test: pick the smallest possible improvement you can make and deliver without asking the team for anything in return, then watch whether people start volunteering information afterwards. If they don't, trust isn't back yet, and neither Part IV's diagnosis nor Part V's moves will get an honest answer.
41
64

Triage a portfolio

  • Chapter 35 covers migrating one system. A struggling function usually has a dozen systems in roughly the same state, and the mistake is applying that same playbook everywhere at once, with a team that's already stretched thin.
  • Triage here looks less like picking the system costing you most, chapter 33's metrics still apply, and more like picking the system where a visible win is actually achievable with the trust and capacity you currently have. An ambitious fix that fails publicly does more damage to a struggling function than a modest fix that visibly works.
  • The test: rank your systems by pain and by achievability separately, then start with whichever one scores highest on achievability, not pain. Chapter 35's method still applies once you've picked the target, this chapter is about picking it differently when capacity and trust are both already scarce.
